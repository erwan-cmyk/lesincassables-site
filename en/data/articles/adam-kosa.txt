Strasbourg, July 2009. In the chamber of the European Parliament, a newly elected Hungarian member, 34 years old, prepares to address his colleagues. He does not reach for the microphone. His hands rise, trace signs in the air, and at his side an interpreter translates each gesture aloud for the other MEPs, most of whom are discovering, live, a language they had never seen expressed in this chamber. No one, in the history of the institution, had yet legislated here in sign language. Ádám Kósa has just entered the history books — but his mandate, over fifteen years, will above all be a matter of concrete parliamentary work, not only of symbol.

Nothing in his background destined him to remain on the sidelines. Born in 1975, deaf, he grows up with Hungarian Sign Language as his mother tongue and studies law at Pázmány Péter Catholic University, where he earns his master's degree in 2000, before completing his training at Semmelweis University in Budapest. As a lawyer, he specializes in defending the rights of people with disabilities and fighting discrimination — a terrain he knows from the inside as much as through the law books, having grown up in an educational and administrative system designed, like the Parliament he will later join, for hearing people.

In 2005, at just 30, he takes over the presidency of the Hungarian Association of the Deaf and Hard of Hearing (SINOSZ). He will stay there seventeen years, until 2022, juggling his European mandate with the leadership of an organization that represents an entire community. From 2008, he also chairs Hungary's council of disability organizations, and from 2006 sits on the legal committee of the International Committee of Sports for the Deaf — an activist commitment that precedes and accompanies his entire political career, rather than following it.

The precise origin of his deafness has never been made public in detail, and nothing allows it to be established with certainty. What is known, on the other hand, is that it never kept him away from the arenas where power is decided. Elected in 2009 under the banner of Fidesz, Viktor Orbán's party, Ádám Kósa becomes the first deaf MEP in the history of the European Parliament and the first sign language user ever to sit there. He will be reelected in 2014, then in 2019, serving three consecutive terms, from July 1, 2009, to July 15, 2024 — fifteen years spent between Brussels and Strasbourg.

Once there, he does not settle for representing a historic first: he pushes for the institution to change its own habits. A member of the Committee on Employment and Social Affairs and a substitute on the Committee on Development, he champions pilot projects for sign language interpretation and real-time captioning within the European institutions, so that debates become accessible to those who, like him, do not follow them by ear. He also campaigns for a broader and more symbolic goal: having sign language recognized as the twenty-fourth official language of the European Union, on the same footing as French, German or Hungarian. In 2013, this work earns him the title of MEP of the Year in the employment and social affairs category, an award conferred by his peers in Brussels — rare recognition for a parliamentarian from a country of fewer than ten million inhabitants, rarer still for legislative work carried by the hands rather than the voice.

What is striking about Ádám Kósa's trajectory is not only that he walked through a door no one had opened before him, but that he held the role over the long haul — fifteen years in office, in a place where the customs, the procedure, down to the very architecture of the debates, had been designed without ever imagining that an elected member might express himself other than out loud. On his arrival, systems had to be adapted, interpreters trained, room made for a language the chamber had never heard — or rather, never seen.

His mandate ends on July 15, 2024, at the close of the ninth European legislative term. He leaves Parliament with an institution behind him a little more accustomed to the idea that accessibility is not a one-off accommodation but a question of method, to be built into the design of debates rather than added after the fact. The interpretation arrangements he helped put in place do not disappear with his departure: they remain available to those who will succeed him, deaf or hearing.

A lawyer by training, head of an association for seventeen years, an elected official for fifteen, Ádám Kósa will have occupied, almost without interruption since the mid-2000s, a rare position: that of a man who speaks on behalf of a community while sitting, in his own name, at the heart of European legislative power. A trajectory that has less to do with revenge than with demonstration — patient and repeated over fifteen years — that an institution meant to represent all European citizens can learn to hear otherwise than through the spoken word.
