Backstage at the Alexander McQueen show in London, in March 1999, a 22-year-old American struggles to catch her breath, surrounded by Kate Moss and Naomi Campbell. She's wearing hand-carved wooden legs, delivered barely seven hours earlier, and is about to open the runway for the "No. 13" show. Overcome with panic, she repeats a line to herself, which she'll later tell ABC News: "Aimee, you've done the Olympics, you can do this." She walks the runway twice. That night, the former Paralympic sprinter stops being just an athlete: she becomes an image.

Aimee Mullins is born in 1976 in Allentown, Pennsylvania, into a family of Irish descent. She's born with fibular hemimelia, a congenital condition that leaves her without a fibula in either leg. Doctors tell her parents she will likely never walk and will need a wheelchair. At one year old, both her legs are amputated below the knee. Against that prognosis, she learns to walk on prosthetics starting at age two.

She grows up in the Allentown area, leading an active childhood: swimming, cycling, skiing, team sports. Nothing about her daily life marks her out as different. She graduates from high school in 1993, before being admitted to one of the most selective universities in the country.

At Georgetown, she enrolls in the Walsh School of Foreign Service on a full scholarship. According to Northeastern University and several official biographies, that scholarship is funded by the U.S. Department of Defense, and Mullins becomes, at 17, the youngest person in the country ever granted a Pentagon security clearance, working as an analyst over the summers. On the track, she joins the NCAA Division I track team, competing against able-bodied athletes — a first for an amputee at that level of college competition.

In 1996, she represents the United States at the Atlanta Paralympic Games, in the 100 meters and long jump. Several university biographies, including Georgetown's and Northeastern's, credit her with world records in her category during this period. The International Paralympic Committee today presents her as a pioneer of Paralympic track and field for her generation. She retires from elite competition in 1998.

It's during these years that she starts running on carbon-fiber blades modeled on the hind legs of a cheetah, developed with engineers and specialized prosthetists — a type of prosthetic that will later become a standard for amputee runners worldwide. Mullins today owns about a dozen different pairs of prosthetics, some displayed in museums, which she presents less as medical corrections than as extensions of her identity.

The McQueen show isn't a one-off. The legs she wears that night, carved from solid ash by British craftsman Bob Watts and engraved with floral motifs by Paul Ferguson, let her vary her height between 5'8" and 6'1". Mullins goes on to model for several magazines, including Vogue and Harper's Bazaar, before signing a global contract with L'Oréal Paris in 2011.

On screen, she appears in artist Matthew Barney's Cremaster 3, in an episode of Poirot alongside David Suchet, and, in 2016, in Stranger Things, where she plays Terry Ives, the mother of the character Eleven. On the TED stage, where she speaks several times between 2009 and 2010, she sums up her philosophy on adversity in one line: "It's part of our life."

Mullins chairs the Women's Sports Foundation from 2007 to 2009, before being named chef de mission of the U.S. Paralympic delegation for the 2012 London Games, the same year Secretary of State Hillary Clinton appoints her to the council promoting women and girls through sport. She's inducted into the National Women's Hall of Fame in 2017 and delivers Northeastern University's commencement address in 2018.

Today, Aimee Mullins splits her time between film, public speaking, and her role as a voice on questions of body, design, and identity. Married since 2016 to British actor Rupert Friend, she became a mother to a daughter in 2024. Three decades after Atlanta, she keeps telling the same story: that of a body that reinvented itself, without ever trying to make itself invisible.
