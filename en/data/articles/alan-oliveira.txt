London, September 2, 2012, the Olympic Stadium. In the last hundred meters of the T44 200 m final, Oscar Pistorius holds a comfortable lead. Twenty meters from the line, a young Brazilian of twenty appears at his shoulder and goes past him. On the clock: 21.45 seconds for Alan Oliveira, 21.52 for Pistorius. Seven hundredths of a second are enough to end one of the most secure reigns in Paralympic sport.

That evening, the name of Alan Fonteles Cardoso Oliveira emerges from anonymity to take a lasting place in the history of sprinting. He is born on August 21, 1992, in Marabá, a mining town in the state of Pará, in northern Brazil. At 21 days old, an intestinal infection turns into septicemia; to save his life, doctors amputate both his legs below the knee.

As a child, in Marabá, he one day watches the sprinter Robson Caetano, Olympic bronze medalist in the 200 m in Seoul in 1988, win a race, and immediately asks his parents to sign him up for athletics. He is eight. His first steps as a runner are taken on wooden prostheses, ill suited to running, under the coach Suzete Montalvão. The talent shows early: Brazilian champion in the 100 m at thirteen, in 2005, he retains his title two years later. In 2007, he receives his first modern carbon-fiber running blades. The following year, at sixteen, he becomes junior world champion in the 100 m and 200 m, before taking silver in the 4x100 m relay at his first Paralympic Games, in Beijing.

The following years confirm his progress: IWAS world titles in 2010, two bronze medals at the World Championships in Christchurch in 2011. But it is in London, in 2012, that he moves up to another level, by beating the man the whole world expected to see triumph. Oscar Pistorius, reigning triple Paralympic champion in the 100 m, 200 m and 400 m, had just raced against able-bodied athletes at the Olympic Games held in the same city a few weeks earlier. His defeat by Oliveira lands like an earthquake.

On the track, minutes after the finish, Pistorius questions the length of his rival's prostheses. "We aren't racing a fair race. I gave it my best," he declares to the cameras, arguing that Oliveira's blades gave him a stride impossible to compete with. The International Paralympic Committee then points out that all the athletes were measured and their prostheses approved before the race, in accordance with the classification rules in force. Oliveira, for his part, says he changed blades only a few weeks earlier and speaks of his hurt at being criticized by an athlete he admires: "He is a really great idol, and to listen to that coming from a really great athlete is really difficult." And: "I don't know who he's picking a fight with, it's not with me." Two days later, Pistorius apologizes for the timing of his complaint, without entirely abandoning the substance of his objection.

The episode does not slow the Brazilian's rise. In 2013, at the World Championships in Lyon, he wins three titles — 100 m, 200 m and 400 m — and breaks the 200 m world record in 20.66 seconds, erasing the mark left by Pistorius. A few days later, in London, he also lowers his own 100 m world record to 10.57 seconds. The same year, he is nominated for the Laureus World Sports Awards in the Sportsperson of the Year with a Disability category, and named Brazil's best athlete.

A break in 2014 interrupts that momentum. Back in 2015, he finishes second in the 200 m and third in the 100 m at the World Championships in Doha. At the Rio Games, in 2016, in front of his home crowd, an individual medal nonetheless eludes the man who had dreamed of four golds: eliminated in the heats of the 100 m and 200 m, he still leaves with silver in the 4x100 m relay. He extends his career to the Tokyo Games, held in 2021, where he lines up in the 100 m, then continues, past thirty, to race competitively in Brazil.

Brazilian champion for fifteen consecutive years, Alan Oliveira remains forever associated with those seven hundredths of a second snatched from Oscar Pistorius in front of the world's cameras — one of the rare moments when Paralympic sprinting held, for the space of an evening, the center of the world's sports news.
