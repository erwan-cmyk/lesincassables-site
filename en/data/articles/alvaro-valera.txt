Paris, August 2024. At the opening ceremony of the Paralympic Games, the Spanish delegation advances behind its flag. It is Álvaro Valera who carries it, with the calm smile of a man for whom the international stage has been familiar ground for close to a quarter of a century. This is not, however, the first time the city has smiled on him: twenty-six years earlier, as a teenager, he had already won a world title here, with a racket in his hand rather than a standard.

Born on October 16, 1982, in Seville, Álvaro Valera grows up in a family turned toward racket sports: his mother and his two sisters play tennis, his father shares that passion and tries his hand at table tennis as well. At five, a diagnosis comes: Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease, a genetic neuropathy that progressively weakens the nerves of the limbs, down to the hands and feet. The word arrives to name a fragility already there; it closes no doors.

Around the age of ten, near the pool of a Seville club, he discovers table tennis playing with friends. "From the very first day, it completely hooked me," he would tell Telemadrid. Won over by that enthusiasm, his father sets up a table in the basement of the family home; young Álvaro trains there tirelessly before joining a registered club team. Table tennis quickly finds its place in a family already given over to racket sports.

His progress is fast: by fourteen, he is on national podiums, then European ones. In 1998, at sixteen, he is crowned junior world champion — in Paris, already. A year later, he forms with Jordi Morales a partnership that will last more than twenty years, all the way to the Paralympic podiums.

In 2000, in Sydney, he has only just turned eighteen when he steps onto the top of the podium in singles. That gold remains, to this day, the only Paralympic title won by a Spaniard in table tennis.

Six more consecutive editions follow — Athens 2004, Beijing 2008, London 2012, Rio 2016, Tokyo 2020 and then Paris 2024 — bringing his total to seven Paralympic appearances. The record fills out: bronze in Beijing, two silver medals in London, in singles and in the team event, silver in Rio, then bronze in doubles in Tokyo alongside Jordi Morales. Across those seven meetings, the count stands at six medals — including that unique Sydney gold, never since matched by a compatriot in table tennis. That longevity comes with constant reinvention: as strength fades in his arms and legs, Valera leans more on positioning, anticipation and technique. Another sign tells, in outline, the progression of the disease: classified in class 8 at the start of his career, he moves to class 7 and then, since 2011, to class 6, one of the most severe among standing players. The body has gone on writing a story that the childhood diagnosis had only opened.

Twenty-six years after his junior title in the same city, carrying the Spanish flag in Paris closes a circle: that of the teenager who was discovering international competition and of the man who has since built one of the longest careers in Spanish Paralympic sport.

Alongside all this, Álvaro Valera has studied tourism. Away from competition, he has built up, in recent years, a short-term rental business in the Madrid region; he now runs Sagasta Suite, a company renting upmarket apartments in Madrid. The elite athlete has become a businessman, handling bookings and property between two training camps, without ever leaving the competition table or giving up daily practice.

The price of performance he does not hide. "Over the years, I get a little weaker," he said in 2021 to the Telemadrid channel — an observation made without emphasis, in the tone of someone who long ago made peace with a disease that has never dictated the tempo of his career.

Now forty-three, he goes on training at Club Tenis de Mesa Rivas, near Madrid, while steering his company. Long the world number one in his class, he remains a benchmark of Spanish Paralympic sport — proof, over seven Games, that a progressive disease can redraw a trajectory without ever stopping it.
