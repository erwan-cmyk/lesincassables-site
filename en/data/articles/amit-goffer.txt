In the driveway of his house, one day in 2004, Amit Goffer watches two of his children strap an assembly of metal and motors onto the legs of a paraplegic volunteer. His son stays close to the power cord, ready to unplug the device in case of a fall. His daughter operates the tilt sensor meant to keep the torso upright. Goffer himself switches the machine on with the limited mobility he retains in his arms. Then it happens: the man takes a step, then another, then several more. Goffer is so absorbed by this halting gait that he does not immediately notice that his volunteer is crying.

Nothing in this engineer's path suggested he would one day spend his days watching artificial legs move forward in his yard. Born in 1953, a graduate of the Technion and then of Tel Aviv University, with a doctorate in electrical and computer engineering from Drexel University in Pennsylvania, Amit Goffer builds his career in medical imaging: first at the Israeli manufacturer Elscint, then at the head of Odin Medical, a company he founds to develop MRI scanners that can be used directly in the operating room. A conventional researcher-and-entrepreneur profile, until an afternoon in 1996 that upends his life.

That year, near Sepphoris, a village in northern Israel known for its biblical past, Goffer rents ATVs for an outing with his children. He is riding alongside his daughter when his vehicle's brakes fail: he hits a tree and breaks his neck against the branches. The injury deprives him of the use of his legs and most of his arms. Nine months of hospitalization in Israel follow, a period he will describe to Newsweek as one immense black hole. In January 1997, on leaving the hospital, he chooses to stop part of his pain medication — one of the best decisions of his life, he will say: "I became human again."

The idea for the exoskeleton comes about a year later, as he browses the internet and takes an interest in how certain creatures, such as scorpions, carry a rigid frame capable of supporting their own weight. "How is it possible that no one ever thought of this?" he will tell Newsweek. It will take him seven years to turn that intuition into a working prototype — the one his own children test, that day in 2004, in his driveway: a structure fitted to the legs, crutches for stabilization, a remote control on the wrist.

Recognition is slow to follow. Presented in 2006 at a robotics conference in Zurich, the concept runs into skepticism from specialists who doubt that such a device could ever make anyone walk again. Goffer presses on, backed by the Technion's incubator and a government grant. In March 2013, it is he himself who presents the ReWalk to U.S. President Barack Obama, on a visit to Jerusalem, during a demonstration led by two paraplegic veterans, one Israeli, the other American.

The company he founded in 2001 under the name Argo Medical Technologies, since renamed ReWalk Robotics, reaps the rewards the following year. In June 2014, the FDA clears the ReWalk Personal for home use in the United States: the first exoskeleton of its kind approved for personal use in the country. Three months later, ReWalk lists on the Nasdaq under the ticker RWLK and raises about $36 million. A few years later, the company claims nearly 400 users around the world, among them American military veterans. Goffer himself will never join that list: operating a ReWalk requires an upper-body control that his own quadriplegia does not allow.

He steps down as ReWalk's chief executive as early as 2012 to become president and chief technology officer, then withdraws from the board of directors entirely in November 2015. He does not stop there: he co-founds UPnRIDE Robotics, where he remains president and chief technology officer, to develop an electric wheelchair that lets its user stand and move about upright, built on a balancing principle close to that of the Segway. Designed in particular for people whose hands cannot handle the controls of an exoskeleton, the device in turn receives FDA clearance for the U.S. market in February 2020.

Renamed Lifeward Ltd. in 2024, the company he founded continues to develop and sell the ReWalk. Amit Goffer dies in 2025, at about 72. The BIRD Foundation, which had supported his research, pays tribute on his death to "a true innovator whose empathy and perseverance gave new hope to people with paralysis." From the driveway of his house, where a stranger had taken his first steps in 2004, to the hundreds of people who have since walked again or stood upright thanks to his machines, Goffer will have spent nearly three decades solving, for himself as much as for others, a problem that medicine had left aside.
