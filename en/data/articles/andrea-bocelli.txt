Once a year, in July, the hills of Lajatico, in Tuscany, fill with the sound of tens of thousands of people seated in an open-air amphitheater carved into the slope. The rest of the year, the place lives up to its name: the Teatro del Silenzio, the Theatre of Silence, stands empty and quiet. That evening, as every summer since 2006, a son of the village returns to sing facing the land where he was born. Andrea Bocelli can see neither the full rows of seats nor the Tuscan hills he has known by heart since childhood.

He is born on September 22, 1958, in Lajatico, Tuscany, into a family of small farmers. Doctors diagnose congenital glaucoma early on, a condition that dangerously raises pressure inside the eye and gradually damages vision. The first years of his life are marked by hospital stays and operations, notably in Turin. They manage to preserve some vision — about one-tenth, according to several biographies — in his right eye alone; his left eye already perceives almost nothing. With no mainstream school willing to enroll him, he's sent around age seven to a boarding school for visually impaired children, where he only comes home for holidays. It's there that he starts piano, at six, before moving on to flute and saxophone.

At twelve, everything changes in an instant. During a football match at the boarding school, he's put in goal — a position he's never played. "A ball hit me right in the face. From that blow, a hemorrhage… and the rest is history," he would recount decades later in the documentary Andrea Bocelli: Because I Believe, presented at the Toronto film festival in September 2024. Doctors try everything — even leeches, as a last resort, according to his biography — to save what little sight remains. Nothing works: the impact takes the use of the one eye that could still see. Andrea Bocelli becomes permanently and completely blind. His brother Alberto recalls it in the same documentary, in a stark phrase: "That's when he lost it. That's when the darkness fell."

The music, though, stays intact. His parents, keen to secure him a profession, nonetheless push him toward a classical education: he earns a law degree from the University of Pisa and works for a time as a court-appointed defense lawyer, while singing at night in piano bars to pay for lessons with tenor Franco Corelli. In 1992, a demo tape of "Miserere," written by singer Zucchero Fornaciari, reaches Luciano Pavarotti, who pushes to have it heard more widely. In 1994, Bocelli wins the "New Talent" section of the Sanremo Music Festival with "Il mare calmo della sera." His first album, Bocelli, follows the next year, carried by "Con te partirò," reworked in 1997 as a duet with soprano Sarah Brightman under the title "Time to Say Goodbye," which becomes an international hit. Romanza, released the same year, stands as one of the best-selling albums in history.

What follows are stages that blindness never kept him from: a duet with Céline Dion, "The Prayer," which wins a Golden Globe and earns an Oscar nomination; the opening ceremony of the 2006 World Cup in Berlin — the same man a football once left completely blind, singing to open the world's biggest tournament in that sport; concerts before Popes John Paul II and Benedict XVI; more than 90 million records sold over his career. In 2006, he secures from his hometown the construction of the Teatro del Silenzio, which he presides over as honorary chairman and where, year after year, he brings in Plácido Domingo, Laura Pausini, or full opera productions like Turandot.

His private life follows the same thread of continuity. A first marriage to Enrica Cenzatti, from whom he separates in 2002, produces two sons, Amos and Matteo. In 2014, he marries Veronica Berti, with whom he has a daughter, Virginia. In July 2024, marking thirty years in music, he brings together José Carreras, Plácido Domingo, and Ed Sheeran on the stage of the Teatro del Silenzio in front of more than 40,000 spectators — a concert later released as a film, distributed in about fifty countries. In September 2025, he sings in front of St. Peter's Basilica in Rome for a hundred thousand people; the following month, he performs at New York's Metropolitan Opera.

"The fact that I am blind is not what defines my life," he has said. Every summer, though, it's still him — and the silence he learned to inhabit since the age of twelve — that fills the hills of Lajatico once more.
