One day in 1955, in a dance studio in Burbank, California, a twelve-year-old girl finishes a variation from Swan Lake in front of the parents who have come to her school's annual recital. In the audience is a man who, a few weeks later, will change the course of her life: Walt Disney, there to find faces for a new program aimed at children. He spots the young dancer, has her audition, and hires her. Annette Funicello thus becomes the last recruit among the twenty-four original Mouseketeers of the Mickey Mouse Club — and, very quickly, the most popular of them: by the end of the first season she alone receives up to 6,000 fan letters a month, more than any other member of the troupe.

Born on October 22, 1942, in Utica, New York, before her family moved to California during her childhood, Annette Funicello becomes within a few years one of the most recognizable figures on American television. The Mickey Mouse Club ends in 1959, but Disney keeps betting on her: she records albums under the single first name "Annette," and appears in The Shaggy Dog, Babes in Toyland, The Monkey's Uncle. Then, from 1963 on, she becomes one of the faces of a genre that was exploding at the time, the beach party movie, alongside Frankie Avalon: Beach Party, Beach Blanket Bingo and their sequels make her a pop icon of sixties America, a smooth and reassuring image in the middle of a beach cinema that was itself growing steadily bolder.

It is precisely a reunion with Frankie Avalon, in 1987, that marks a quieter turning point. The two actors, joined by the host Dick Clark, set off on a promotional tour for the film Back to the Beach, meant to revive their double act twenty years on. Onstage, at 44, Annette Funicello starts to feel dizziness, headaches, unexplained losses of balance. The examinations that follow lead to a diagnosis of multiple sclerosis, a chronic autoimmune disease that attacks the myelin sheath protecting the nerve fibers and that can, depending on the patient, affect walking, balance, sight or speech.

For five years, she chooses to say nothing about the diagnosis, including to some of those closest to her. But the silence has a price. Her public appearances, marked by a hesitant walk and visible balance problems, feed press rumors that the former Mouseketeer has been drinking. The actress, whose image had always been that of a model young woman, finds herself accused, by implication, of a vice she never had, while she keeps quiet about the real, neurological cause of her symptoms.

In 1992, she decides to take back control of her own story and makes her illness public, in part precisely to put an end to those rumors of drunkenness. The gesture changes everything: two years later, in an interview with the Orange County Register, she describes how other people's eyes shifted once the truth was known. "Now that I've gone public with my illness, they can't do enough," she says. "They even send me home remedies to try. Everyone says, 'God bless you and I'm praying for you.'" Suspicion had given way, overnight, to solicitude.

The year after her disclosure, in 1993, Annette Funicello turns her experience into a structured commitment: she creates the Annette Funicello Research Fund for Neurological Diseases, housed at the California Community Foundation, to finance research into multiple sclerosis and neurological diseases. She becomes one of the first American celebrities of that stature to put her name and her fame behind research into a disease whose progression she continues, meanwhile, to suffer.

The disease, for its part, keeps advancing. Annette Funicello gradually loses the use of her legs in the mid-2000s, then her speech toward the end of the decade. Her last public appearance dates from 1998. She dies on April 8, 2013, in Bakersfield, California, at 70, from complications related to multiple sclerosis.

What remains, beyond the beach movies and the Mickey Mouse ears, is the choice she made in 1992: to prefer the truth, however hard to say, over a silence that left the field open to rumor. The fund that bears her name goes on financing research into neurological diseases long after her death, the concrete trace of a career that began with a dance turn that caught someone's eye and ended with a commitment that, for its part, continues.
