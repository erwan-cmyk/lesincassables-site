Philadelphia, March 19, 2011. On the mat at the NCAA wrestling championships, Anthony Robles dominates Matt McDonough, the defending champion from the University of Iowa. When the referee raises his arm, the score reads 7-1. Robles has just completed a perfect season, 36 wins and no losses, and been named the tournament's outstanding wrestler. He is 22, he has one leg, and he has just entered the history of his sport.

Anthony Robles is born on July 20, 1988, in La Mirada, California, without a right leg, an agenesis whose cause has never been identified. He is offered a prosthetic; he turns it down from the age of three and chooses crutches, then his arms and his left leg, to get around, to run, to climb.

He grows up mainly in the care of his mother, Judy Robles. At 14, the summer before he starts high school, the family leaves California for Mesa, Arizona. It is there, on the mats of a local gym, that his life turns.

An older cousin introduces him to wrestling; a coach, having seen him lifting weights, invites him to join the club. The beginner weighs barely 90 pounds and loses nearly every match: 5 wins and 8 losses in his first year, ranked last in his weight class in the whole city. But something takes hold. "I walked away with the biggest smile," he would tell Time magazine years later, thinking back to that first practice, where he had been turned every which way.

Over the following years, Robles crushes the competition. As a junior and a senior, he posts a record of 96 wins and no losses, takes two consecutive Arizona state titles and ends his school career at 129 wins and 15 losses. Despite that record, he draws no great enthusiasm from the major college programs. It is finally at Arizona State University, a few miles from home, that he joins the wrestling team in the fall of 2006.

His start at Arizona State is gradual rather than dazzling: fourth place at the NCAA championship in 2009, seventh in 2010. It is after that harder season that letters from schoolchildren, moved by his story, begin reaching him by the dozen. They remind him that his story goes beyond sport alone, an episode he now describes as the moment when competition took on another meaning for him.

His senior season, in 2010-11, is perfect: 36 wins, no losses, a third Pac-10 conference title. In the NCAA championship final, in Philadelphia, he meets Matt McDonough, the defending champion, and beats him 7-1. He comes away with the tournament's outstanding wrestler award. "It's a dream come true," he says at the time on the public radio network NPR.

That summer, at the ESPY Awards, he receives both the award for best male athlete with a disability and the Jimmy V Award for Perseverance. In 2012, he publishes his autobiography with the journalist Austin Murphy, Unstoppable: From Underdog to Undefeated, and enters the National Wrestling Hall of Fame, which awards him its Medal of Courage. The same year, he joins ESPN as an analyst for college wrestling broadcasts, a role he has held ever since.

Robles does not go on to Olympic wrestling. He turns to speaking, touring American companies and campuses to tell his story, then to coaching, running the team at his old high school in Mesa for a few seasons. In 2024, his story returns to the spotlight with Unstoppable, premiered at the Toronto festival on September 6 and released in theaters at the end of the year: a film produced by, among others, Ben Affleck and Matt Damon, in which Jharrel Jerome plays him and Jennifer Lopez plays his mother. Robles himself serves as a consultant and doubles for his own character in the wrestling scenes.

Now married to Laura and the father of a young son, Abel, Anthony Robles goes on commentating college wrestling for ESPN and speaking across the United States. Thirteen years after Philadelphia, his story has ended up leaving the gyms for movie screens, without ever really leaving the mat where it all began.
