Rio de Janeiro, September 14, 2016. On the piste at Carioca Arena 3, Bebe Vio lands the decisive touch at 15-7 against China's Zhou Jingjing. She rips off her mask, bursts into tears, and throws herself into the arms of the president of the Italian Paralympic Committee. "Non ci credo ancora, è un'emozione bellissima" — I still can't believe it, it's a beautiful feeling — she says, her voice shaking. At 19, the fencer from Venice has just become Paralympic champion in individual foil, category B.

Beatrice Vio, known as Bebe, is born on March 4, 1997, in Venice and grows up in Mogliano Veneto, in the province of Treviso, alongside her brother and sister. She puts on her first fencing gloves at 5 and moves up through the youth ranks without anything setting her apart — just one child among others on the regional pistes of the Veneto.

Everything changes in November 2008. At 11, she's struck by fulminant meningitis, caused, according to several Italian media reports, by a serogroup C meningococcus — the same strain behind a documented outbreak then affecting the province of Treviso. The infection worsens rapidly, causing extensive tissue necrosis that threatens her life. She spends 104 days in the hospital; to save her, doctors have to amputate both her legs below the knee and both her forearms. Years later, she would sum up the severity of the diagnosis in an interview with the newspaper Il Giornale: "Sì, io sono felice. Considera che il 97 per cento di quelli che hanno quello che ho avuto io di solito muoiono. Io sono ancora qui" — yes, I'm happy. Consider that 97 percent of people who get what I had usually die. I'm still here.

Her parents, Ruggero and Teresa, go on to found the association Art4sport, meant to help other amputee children find their way back into sport. For Bebe herself, that path first means making do: in early 2010, she takes her first steps back into wheelchair fencing, the foil simply taped to her forearm, for lack of a proper prosthetic. The Centro Protesi in Budrio then develops, with the Italian Paralympic Committee, an attachment able to hold the weapon. She becomes the first athlete in Europe to have a prosthetic fitted to her fencing arm, and the first disabled fencer in the world to compete with all four prosthetic limbs.

In 2011, she joins the Italian Paralympic national team. Titles follow quickly: European champion in 2014, world champion in Eger, Hungary, in 2015, European champion again the year of her Rio triumph. She adds a team bronze medal and carries the Italian flag at the closing ceremony.

Her medal collection keeps growing: another world title in Rome in 2017, another European title in Terni in 2018, and more to come. At the Tokyo Games, held in 2021 because of the pandemic, she retains her individual title and adds team silver, this time carrying the Italian flag at the opening ceremony. In Paris, in 2024, she closes out this Paralympic cycle, for now, with an individual bronze and a team bronze.

Between competitions, Bebe Vio has built a life that extends well beyond the piste. She hosts a show on Italian broadcaster RAI, has published two books, and speaks about disability with a bluntness all her own. In an interview for the show Le Iene, picked up by the Italian press, she dismisses the idea that people should go easy on her, saying of how others see her: "Ti definiscono normale solo perché hai tutti i pezzi" — they call you normal just because you have all your pieces.

In May 2023, she earns a degree in communication and international relations from John Cabot University in Rome — not without incident: a few weeks before graduation, her car is broken into and her bag, containing the laptop holding her nearly finished thesis, is stolen. She rewrites it in a few days with a friend's help.

"E io amo le mie protesi, rappresentano quello che sono: 50 e 50, mezza donna e mezzo robot. E amo le cicatrici, sono la mia normalità," she told the newspaper Il Giornale in 2017 — and I love my prosthetics, they represent what I am: fifty-fifty, half woman and half robot. And I love my scars, they're my normal. More than fifteen years after coming close to death at 11, the line still sums up Bebe Vio's trajectory: nothing to erase, everything to carry.
