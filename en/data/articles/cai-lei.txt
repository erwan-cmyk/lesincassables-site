On July 19, 2026, at the China AI Gala, a voice rings out through the hall to thank the research teams in attendance. It matters little which team crosses the finish line first, it says in substance, as long as someone crosses it. The voice is Cai Lei's, reconstructed by an artificial intelligence from old recordings. The man receiving the Special AI Contribution Award that evening has been unable to speak for several months; the award recognizes precisely the use he makes of that technology in his fight against his own disease.

Seven years earlier, Cai Lei stood on the other side of the stage: one of the most prominent executives in Chinese e-commerce. Born in 1978 in Shangqiu, in Henan province, a graduate in finance and taxation of the Central University of Finance and Economics in Beijing, he is reported to have begun his career at Samsung, the direct-selling group Amway, and then the property developer Vanke, according to the Chinese press, before joining the e-commerce giant JD.com. There he becomes a vice president at 36, makes his name as one of the architects of the group's electronic invoicing, and in parallel founds four companies one after another, at a rate of about one a year. His success makes him, according to the South China Morning Post, a multimillionaire.

In September 2019, at 41, a few months after the birth of his child, Cai Lei learns that he has amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), an incurable neurodegenerative disease, sometimes referred to in China as the disease that freezes the body, which progressively destroys motor neurons until paralysis sets in. Rather than withdraw, he chooses to turn the methods that built his career against the disease. As early as November 2019, he registers a medical research organization: it will become the foundation of AskHelpU, a platform designed to connect patients, researchers, hospitals, and pharmaceutical companies.

Seven years on, AskHelpU claims more than 18,000 registered patients (some of whom have had their full genome sequenced) and works with some sixty research teams, around fifty biotechnology companies, and a dozen or so hospitals across China. According to the Chinese press, the platform is said to have advanced nearly 300 candidate treatments through the preclinical stage, about thirty of which have reached clinical trials — up from around fifteen at the start of 2026, a sign of continued acceleration. Cai Lei says he has helped cut to a few months, for certain molecules, an entry into clinical trials that usually takes more than a decade, thanks in particular to artificial intelligence tools capable of combing through tens of thousands of scientific publications — the very field that will earn him, seven years later, his award at the July 2026 gala. A neuroscience researcher quoted by the Chinese press estimates that this work has saved ALS research at least ten years; Cai Lei himself puts it closer to twenty.

That intensity comes at a price, and the disease, for its part, has never stopped advancing: the loss of walking, then of his arms, then, in late 2025, of speech. Cai Lei now communicates solely through an eye-tracking device capable of transcribing about sixty characters a minute, with a voice synthesizer reconstructing his own voice from recordings made before he lost it. Confined for nearly two years to a room of about ten square meters, on respiratory support at night, he is nonetheless reported to keep working about ten hours a day, broken up by the pauses needed to relieve his muscles. His wife, Duan Rui, gave up her career to fund the research by becoming a livestream sales host, and now divides her time between that work and raising their son; the couple has lived apart as a health precaution since a stay in intensive care in 2024. Cai Lei is said to have offered her a divorce several times, most recently in 2025, so that she could return to a lighter life — each time, she is said to have refused.

At the start of 2026, his functional score on the ALSFRS-R scale (which tracks the disease's progression from 48, normal function, down to 0, near-total paralysis) had fallen to a single digit. The Chinese press now describes him as being in the terminal phase, with a common cold or a choking episode now enough to put his life in danger. Yet in early August 2026, he was still supervising his teams and reaching out to researchers and laboratories from his bed, with the movement of his eyes as his only tool. On January 1, 2026, he published an open letter, written entirely by eye tracking, addressed to fellow patients around the world. The goal he has set out since his diagnosis — to help a million patients before he dies — remains, seven years later and day after day, what keeps him working.
