In the mid-1970s, an idle teenager hangs around Ipanema beach, in Rio de Janeiro. He left school early, enrolled in journalism in 1978 before dropping out after three weeks, and nothing suggests that Agenor de Miranda Araújo Neto is going to become one of the most significant lyricists of his generation. The spark comes from a musician friend, Léo Jaime, who recommends him to a band forming in the city: Barão Vermelho is looking for a singer, and Cazuza — the nickname will remain his only stage name — shows up with several lyrics already written.

The band, until then confined to covers, begins composing around his lyrics. Success comes in stages: the title song of the film Bete Balanço brings Barão Vermelho to the attention of the general public, then the album Maior Abandonado, released in September 1984, passes one hundred thousand copies sold and establishes the band among the benchmarks of the emerging Brazilian rock scene. In January 1985, Cazuza takes the stage at the very first edition of Rock in Rio, before hundreds of thousands of spectators. It is also during that year, according to several biographers, that he contracts HIV.

A few months later, after artistic tensions with his bandmates, he leaves Barão Vermelho, taking with him several songs intended for his first solo record. What follows surpasses everything he has known until then: in a nine-year career, Cazuza sells more than five million albums, places eleven songs at the top of the Brazilian charts and eighteen others in the top 10. "Exagerado," "Ideologia" and "O Tempo Não Pára" become instant classics of a direct, unaffected style of writing, which will lead Caetano Veloso, in remarks attributed to him, to present Cazuza as the greatest Brazilian poet of his generation.

On stage as in life, Cazuza cultivates excess: alcohol, cocaine, endless nights, chronic lateness to rehearsals. Openly bisexual, he moves from one relationship to the next with men and women without letting himself be locked into a category, already claiming at eighteen, in front of his mother, the right not to settle the question once and for all. This freedom of tone, in a Brazil still locked down by conservatism, feeds the scandalous aura of the figure as much as his lyrics do.

The turning point comes at the end of the 1980s. On January 24, 1989, in Recife, Cazuza gives what will remain his last concert: visibly weakened, he has to go backstage in the middle of the show to receive oxygen before coming back to finish it. Three weeks later, the journalist Zeca Camargo questions him for the Folha de S. Paulo about a recent hospitalization. Irritated by the insistent questions, Cazuza raises his glass of wine and lets out a sentence that will travel the whole country: "Escreve aí, Zeca. A maldita é a aids" — "Write it down, Zeca. The cursed one is AIDS."

Published in February 1989, this admission makes him the first Brazilian public figure to publicly reveal that he is HIV-positive, at a time when the disease is still surrounded by fear and ignorance. The press does not always show restraint: in July of the same year, the weekly Veja runs a headline on a Cazuza, victim of AIDS, who "agoniza em praça pública" — is "dying in the public square" — a phrase the singer receives as a humiliation and which brings on a collapse requiring emergency hospitalization. He nevertheless keeps composing and releasing records, including the album Burguesia in 1989, refusing to withdraw from public life.

Cazuza dies on July 7, 1990, in Rio de Janeiro, at 32, of AIDS-related septic shock. Thousands of fans follow his funeral procession to the São João Batista cemetery, in Botafogo.

Three months later, his parents, Lucinha and João Araújo, found the Sociedade Viva Cazuza to help HIV-positive children abandoned by their families, at a time when very few facilities exist for them in Brazil. The organization will go on to support several hundred children and young people over the following decades. In 2004, the film Cazuza — O Tempo Não Pára, with Daniel de Oliveira in the title role, brings his story to a whole generation of Brazilians who never saw his concerts. Thirty-six years after his death, his songs are still played on Brazilian radio, and his name remains associated, in Brazil, with one of the turning points in the way society learned to talk about AIDS in terms other than fear and rejection.
