On December 18, 1999, a crowd gathers outside Colombo Town Hall for what is meant to be Chandrika Kumaratunga's very last campaign rally before the presidential election scheduled three days later. As the incumbent president prepares to return to her vehicle, a woman pushes her way through the crowd and jumps over the security barrier. Bodyguards rush to restrain her. She then detonates the explosive charge strapped to her body.

The explosion kills 34 people, among them close aides and police officers, and injures more than 105. A few minutes later, a second bomb goes off in Ja-Ela, on the outskirts of Colombo, at a rally of the main opposition party: twelve more people lose their lives there. The female suicide bomber who targeted Kumaratunga belonged to the Liberation Tigers of Tamil Eelam (LTTE), at war with the Sri Lankan state for more than fifteen years.

Kumaratunga survives by a narrow margin. A piece of shrapnel strikes the bridge of her nose and damages the optic nerve of her right eye, while other fragments lodge in her neck, in the immediate vicinity of her brain. She permanently loses the sight in that eye and has worn an ocular prosthesis ever since.

From her hospital bed, a few hours after the attack, she asks that a message be passed on to the population: stay calm, harm no one, go to places of worship to pray rather than give in to anger. No retaliatory riots against the Tamil minority break out that night, in a country where intercommunal tension is nonetheless extreme — a calm that is directly linked to that appeal.

Three days after nearly dying, on December 21, 1999, she wins the presidential election against Ranil Wickremesinghe and is sworn in for a second term the very next day. She will govern for six more years, until November 19, 2005, becoming the longest-serving president in the history of Sri Lanka.

Nothing in the trajectory of Chandrika Bandaranaike Kumaratunga was ever a matter of quiet certainty. Born in 1945 in Colombo, she grows up in a family shaped as much by power as by political violence. Her father, S.W.R.D. Bandaranaike, founder of the Sri Lanka Freedom Party and prime minister, is assassinated in 1959, when she is fourteen. Her mother, Sirimavo Bandaranaike, succeeds him at the head of the party and becomes, in 1960, the first woman in the world to lead a government. After studying political science, economics, law and journalism in Paris and then London, Chandrika returns home and marries the actor turned politician Vijaya Kumaratunga. He is assassinated in turn in 1988. She finds herself a widow, with two children.

Yet she does not leave public life. She takes the helm of the People's Alliance, becomes prime minister in August 1994 and then president three months later, on November 12, 1994 — the fifth president of Sri Lanka. Her term falls within one of the deadliest phases of the Sri Lankan civil war, which since 1983 has pitted the state against the Tamil separatist guerrillas and which will claim, over its entire duration, more than 60,000 lives according to the Encyclopaedia Britannica. Kumaratunga first tries the path of negotiation and proposes a constitutional reform opening the way to greater regional autonomy, before returning to a more military line when the LTTE breaks the ceasefire in 1995. No documented accusation of war crimes has ever been brought against her personally: the most heavily established responsibilities concern the final phase of the conflict, in 2009, under the presidency of her successor Mahinda Rajapaksa, several years after the end of her own term.

Constitutionally ineligible for a third term, she leaves the presidency in November 2005. She does not retire, however: she goes on to chair the CBK Foundation for Democracy and Justice and the South Asia Policy & Research Institute, two organizations she founds to promote a more inclusive Sri Lanka, sits on the board of the Club de Madrid from 2009, and in 2015 takes charge of the official body responsible for national unity and reconciliation between communities. She leaves behind the memory of a leader who went on governing for six years with one eye fewer, without ever making an issue of it, and whose first reflex, wounded and in shock, had been to ask her people not to answer violence with violence.
