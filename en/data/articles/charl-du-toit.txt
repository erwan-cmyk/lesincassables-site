On this August evening in 2012, in London's Olympic Stadium, Charl du Toit has just missed out on the 100 meters final. He is nineteen, it is his first Paralympic selection, and he will go home without a medal. Yet the first thing he asks his coach, Suzanne Ferreira, has nothing to do with his own race. He wants to know whether he can carry the South African flag for his teammate Fanie van der Merwe, who is running in the final that evening. Van der Merwe will win gold. Du Toit will keep his word.

The scene says a good deal about who the man is: a sprinter who, before thinking of his own results, thinks of his team. Charl du Toit is born on March 26, 1993, in Pretoria, South Africa, with cerebral palsy that mainly affects the right side of his body — a less responsive arm, calves that developed unevenly. In his family, no one ever made an issue of it. His parents, Wessel and Elmarie, themselves ran the 400 and 800 meters at a good level; his father has been coaching for forty years. As a child, Charl has to undergo an operation to lengthen an Achilles tendon, but nothing stops him from playing first-team hockey for five years, dabbling in cricket and tennis, or becoming head boy of his Pretoria high school, Hoërskool Akasia. He simply works harder than the others to keep up.

Everything changes at seventeen. A groin injury takes him to a physiotherapist, who asks him a simple question: why doesn't he compete in Paralympic sport? Du Toit rejects the idea at first — he sees nothing abnormal about himself. Tests follow, which clarify his situation and steer him toward the T37 class, which brings together athletes with cerebral palsy or a comparable neurological impairment mainly affecting one side of the body. In March 2011, he enrolls at Stellenbosch University and begins training under Suzanne Ferreira, after a final year of high school coached by his own father. His first international outing comes as early as December, at the IWAS World Games. Three months later, a solid time over 800 meters opens the doors to London.

Back from England without a medal but with the certainty that he belongs, he strings together podiums: two bronze medals at the World Championships in Lyon in 2013 (400 meters and 4x100 relay), silver in the 100 meters at the Commonwealth Games in Glasgow in 2014, then silver in the 400 meters at the World Championships in Doha in 2015. The progress is real, but gold keeps eluding him.

He arrives in Rio in 2016 transformed. In the 100 meters, he becomes Paralympic champion in 11.45 seconds, having already lowered the event's world record to 11.43 at a meet in Nottwil, Switzerland, then to 11.42 in the heats, on the Rio track itself. In the 400 meters, he adds a second title, in a time of 51.13 seconds that stands as a new Paralympic record. He leaves South America a double champion, and will be named, a few months later, Sportsman of the Year with a Disability at the South African Sports Awards. The following year, at the 2017 World Championships in London, he confirms it by winning his first two individual world titles, in the 200 and 400 meters.

Tokyo, in 2021, offers him an altogether different story. At twenty-eight, he is one of the flag bearers of the South African delegation at the opening ceremony. In the 400 meters final, on September 1, he runs a time almost identical to the one that had earned him gold in Rio five years earlier — a little over 51 seconds — but finishes only fifth: four rivals go under 51 seconds, and the winner breaks the symbolic 50-second barrier for the first time. Du Toit will recount having felt, 150 meters from the line, something like a cramp or a buildup of lactic acid in the hip affected by his cerebral palsy, just as he was trying to accelerate. In the 100 meters, he finishes eighth. In his remarks after the Games, he takes the opportunity to challenge the South African public and sponsors: para sport, he says in substance, should not be followed only for two weeks every four years, but supported throughout the Olympic cycle.

His last race takes place in Birmingham, at the 2022 Commonwealth Games: a silver medal in the 100 meters, in 11.54 seconds, won at twenty-nine. On the track, right after the finish, he announces his retirement. "I am so privileged to have had this wonderful career, but I just feel it's time," he says then, speaking of tears of joy and a career ending he could not have written better himself. Since then, Charl du Toit has been pursuing a master's degree at Stellenbosch University on the impact of physical education on pupils with a physical impairment, and intends to stay connected to South African athletics — this time off the track.
