Washington, September 1977. In the small room of the Cellar Door, in Georgetown, Charles Mingus plays what will be his last night in the club where he has so often triumphed. He weighs 262 pounds (119 kilos), nearly 50 pounds less than his usual weight. That evening, his musicians have left their scores back in New York: furious, the bassist walks off the stage and threatens to cancel the concert, before his wife and manager, Sue Mingus, talks him into going back up. Sitting, motionless — at his death the Washington Post would write, "Seated, he looked like a coiled Buddha" — he offers, that night, with bitter irony: "For five years I didn't want to think about music. I played the piano a little. I didn't touch the bass. Just this year I'm getting my energy to start writing again."

Two months later, at the end of 1977, the diagnosis comes: amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), the neurodegenerative disease Americans call Lou Gehrig's disease and French medicine calls Charcot's disease. It gradually destroys the motor neurons that command the voluntary muscles, without ever touching intelligence or sensation. For a man whose whole life was in his hands — the ones that plucked the strings of the double bass, the ones that wrote out scores at the piano — the verdict lands like a sentence.

Charles Mingus was born on April 22, 1922, on a US Army base in Nogales, Arizona, where his father, an army sergeant, was then posted. The family settles shortly afterward in Watts, in south Los Angeles. His stepmother allows only church music in the house, but the boy falls for Duke Ellington. He studies trombone and then cello — an instrument he has to give up, because at the time it is nearly impossible for a Black musician to make a career in classical music. It is the saxophonist Buddy Collette who brings him into his swing band as a bassist, though he has never touched a double bass. In the 1940s, he is already playing alongside Louis Armstrong and crossing paths with Charlie Parker and Thelonious Monk. In the 1950s, refusing to leave control of his work to others, he creates his own label and publishing house, then founds the Jazz Workshop, where he demands that his musicians invent in real time rather than read from a score.

This uncompromising temperament, which earns him the nickname "the angry man of jazz," gives rise to a considerable body of work: Pithecanthropus Erectus in 1956, then Mingus Ah Um in 1959, with its heartbreaking Goodbye Pork Pie Hat — the album would enter the Library of Congress's National Recording Registry in 2003. Then, in 1963, The Black Saint and the Sinner Lady, often cited as one of the summits of orchestration in the history of jazz.

In 1978, the disease advances fast. Confined to a wheelchair, Mingus loses the use of his hands: he can no longer write a note on paper or compose at the piano. He does not stop creating, though. He sings, humming his last melodies into a tape recorder; his musicians — the trumpeter Jack Walrath, the saxophonist Ricky Ford, the bandleader Paul Jeffrey and his longtime collaborator Sy Johnson — collect these tapes and turn them into orchestrated pieces. In June, President Jimmy Carter honors him at a concert at the White House. The following month, in New York, his quintet plays the Village Vanguard without him: it is the drummer Dannie Richmond, with Eddie Gomez on bass, who now carries his music onstage.

That summer, Charles and Sue Mingus move to Cuernavaca, Mexico, where the couple hope to find a treatment American medicine cannot offer. "Mexico offered hope," Sue Mingus would recount years later, recalling the period in which she discovers, she says, a side of her husband that "did not make the newspapers": "Paralyzed, and in a chair he had more energy than 50 people running around the block. He ruled our household." Meanwhile, in New York, the singer Joni Mitchell sets words to his last compositions, for an album that would appear under the title Mingus.

Charles Mingus dies of a heart attack on January 5, 1979, in Cuernavaca, a little more than a year after the diagnosis. A believer in reincarnation, he did not want a jazz funeral; Sue Mingus has his body cremated and scatters his ashes in the Ganges, in India, according to his last wishes. Sy Johnson, his longtime collaborator and friend, would sum up that final year as "Mingus's finest hour as a human being."

Sue Mingus would devote the following decades to keeping her husband's work alive, founding the organization Let My Children Hear Music in 1986. On June 3, 1989, at Alice Tully Hall in New York, the conductor Gunther Schuller leads a thirty-piece orchestra in the premiere of Epitaph, a score of 4,235 bars — nearly two hours of music — assembled from scattered manuscripts and considered one of the longest works ever written for a jazz orchestra. In 1993, the Library of Congress acquires his entire archive, an operation it would itself describe as the most important acquisition of a jazz manuscript collection in its history.
