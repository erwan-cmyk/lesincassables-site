In the garage of the family home, in Maitland, Florida, a teenager lies down on the floor and tries a push-up. He manages one. Just one. On that day in 2017, nothing suggests that the same young man, a little under three years later, will cross the finish line of a full Ironman — 3.8 kilometers of swimming, 180 kilometers on the bike, a marathon for dessert — fourteen minutes inside the fateful seventeen-hour cutoff.

Chris Nikic is born on October 6, 1999, in Florida, with Down syndrome — the presence of an extra copy of chromosome 21, which shapes development and, in his case, brings serious medical complications. At five months, he undergoes open-heart surgery. He does not walk until he is four, does not eat solid food until he is five. As a teenager, he goes through four more operations, on his ears this time, which temporarily keep him out of the pool and cause him to put on some fifteen kilos while he recovers.

He grows up in Maitland, in the outer suburbs of Orlando, where his parents sign him up for Special Olympics Florida around the age of nine or ten — golf first, then swimming, cycling, running, sport after sport, with nothing along the way to suggest an endurance career. It is his father, Nik Nikic, an entrepreneur, who formulates the principle that will become the backbone of everything that follows: improve by barely 1 percent each day, comparing yourself to no one but yourself the day before. That single push-up in the garage is the starting point.

In 2017, Chris meets Dan Grieb, an experienced triathlete, who becomes both his coach and his race guide — a mandatory presence, in this kind of event, for an athlete with an intellectual disability. Under his direction, the distances stretch year after year: sprint, Olympic, then, in May 2020, a half-Ironman — 1.9 kilometers of swimming, 90 kilometers on the bike, 21 kilometers of running — already a first for an athlete with Down syndrome. All that is left, then, is to double the stakes.

On November 7, 2020, at Panama City Beach, Chris Nikic sets off on Ironman Florida, Dan Grieb at his side. The swim is done in 1:54, the bike in 8:12, the run in 6:18 — with the transitions on top of that. He crosses the line in 16 hours, 46 minutes, and 9 seconds, fourteen minutes before the seventeen-hour cutoff beyond which a time is no longer recognized. Guinness World Records certifies him, the following year, as the first person with Down syndrome to finish a full Ironman. Along the way, he has raised close to $40,000 for the Ironman Foundation.

Chris Nikic never casts that day as a story of Christlike endurance. Asked about it by Guinness World Records, he sums it up in his own way, with the humor that will become his trademark on the conference circuit: "People thought that I couldn't do anything. But I said 'hell no!'" Asked about his plans, he adds, half-seriously: "I'm still doing IRONMANs because I'm here for the ladies."

The performance changes his trajectory. In 2021, he receives the Jimmy V Award for Perseverance at the ESPY Awards, the American ceremony that honors the year's best athletes. That same year, he finishes the Boston Marathon in 6:01:22. He becomes a public speaker, publishes the book 1% Better, and goes on to complete the six races that make up the Abbott World Marathon Majors — Boston, Chicago, New York, London, Berlin, Tokyo — a set completed in 2024.

Two years after Panama City Beach, Chris Nikic resets the counter. On October 6, 2022 — his twenty-third birthday — he lines up at the start of the Ironman World Championship in Kona, on the Big Island of Hawaii, Dan Grieb once again at his side. The wind, that day, throws his entire bike leg off. "I actually had to ride against the wind. The wind just attacked me at 30 miles per hour," he would recount. He crosses the line all the same in 16 hours, 31 minutes, and 27 seconds, improving on his own 2020 time and becoming the first athlete with Down syndrome to finish the Ironman World Championship.

Today, Chris Nikic divides his time between endurance races, speaking engagements, and his role as a Special Olympics ambassador around the world, where he carries his 1 percent a day method as something anyone can take up, Down syndrome or not. He likes to close his talks with a question he invariably turns back on his audience: "I want to know who is next."
