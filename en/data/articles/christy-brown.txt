In 1937, in the family kitchen in Kimmage, Dublin, a five-year-old boy lies on the tiled floor while his brothers and sisters bustle around him. Nobody, not even the doctors, believes he understands what is being said in the room. That day, he grips a piece of chalk between the toes of his left foot — the only limb of his body he can command — and traces a letter on his sister's slate. His mother, Bridget, leans over, looks at the imperfect but recognizable mark, and understands in an instant what no one around her had been willing to consider: her son thinks, and he has just found a way to prove it.

Christy Brown is born on June 5, 1932, at the Rotunda Hospital in Dublin, into a large family of modest means. His mother, Bridget Fagan, and his father, Patrick Brown, a bricklayer, would have twenty-two children, nine of whom do not survive infancy. Christy survives, but with severe cerebral palsy that leaves him no control over his limbs apart from his left foot. The doctors of the day declare him profoundly intellectually disabled and recommend, as was then customary, that he be placed in an institution. Bridget refuses. For years, with no favorable diagnosis and no established method, she goes on talking to him, teaching him his letters, convinced that her son's intelligence is intact behind a body that will not obey him.

His schooling remains minimal: a few irregular spells at St Brendan's School-Clinic in Sandymount, but the bulk of his education is built at home, patiently, with his mother. It is with that same left foot that he would learn, as a teenager, to paint and then to type, every word wrested from a body that never quite cooperates. The turning point comes when Dr. Robert Collis, a physician and writer, meets him at the Sandymount clinic and detects in this motionless young man a genuine literary temperament. Collis puts his contacts in the publishing world at Brown's service and helps him turn his notebooks into a manuscript.

That manuscript becomes My Left Foot, published in 1954. The book — an unsparing account of a Dublin childhood lived from the floor of a shared room — causes a sensation in Ireland and then abroad. Overnight, Christy Brown becomes a recognized author, without his daily life really changing: he goes on living with his parents, in Dublin, surrounded by his large family.

The book's success brings him heavy mail from readers, among them an American from Connecticut, Beth Moore. Their correspondence intensifies, they meet in 1960, then begin a more intimate relationship in 1965. Moore becomes a decisive presence in his work: it is she who imposes a strict writing discipline on him and limits his access to alcohol, making possible the completion of the novel that would become his major work. Their relationship ends around 1967.

In 1970 comes Down All the Days, a largely autobiographical novel dedicated to Beth Moore, which takes up and extends the material of My Left Foot. The critic Bernard Share, in the pages of the Irish Times, hails it as "the most important Irish novel since Ulysses." Translated into fourteen languages and an international bestseller, the book would appear on the longlist for the Lost Man Booker Prize, drawn up retrospectively for works from 1970. Another novel largely inspired by his relationship with Moore follows, A Shadow on Summer (1974), then Wild Grow the Lilies (1976), three collections of poetry, and, published after his death, A Promising Career (1982) — an entire body of work composed with the toes of a single foot.

In 1972, at forty, he marries Mary Carr, an Englishwoman he met at a party in London, in a ceremony at the Dublin registry office. The couple settle first in County Dublin, then in Kerry, before moving to Somerset, in England. According to the biography Georgina Louise Hambleton devoted to him in 2007, these years are marked by a gradual estrangement from his family and those close to him, a growing dependence on alcohol, and an isolation that several of the people who knew him attribute to his wife's influence.

Christy Brown dies on September 7, 1981, at forty-nine, choking during a meal at his home in Somerset. Unexplained bruises found on his body would feed suspicions of mistreatment during his final years, without ever being formally resolved. He is buried at Glasnevin Cemetery in Dublin, among the great Irish figures whose ranks he had ended up joining.

Eight years after his death, the director Jim Sheridan brings his story to the screen in My Left Foot (1989), with Daniel Day-Lewis as Christy Brown and Brenda Fricker as Bridget. The film comes away with two Oscars — best actor and best supporting actress — and shows the world what his mother had seen first, one afternoon in 1937, in a stroke of chalk on a slate.
