December 2016, in a grocery store in Boston's North End. On the counter of the Golden Goose Market sit bags of homemade cookies, baked the day before in a commercial kitchen rented for the occasion. Collette Divitto and her mother, Rosemary Alfredo, have delivered their very first small batch without quite knowing what to expect. Within a week, all one hundred bags placed on the shelf have sold.

That modest success would become the starting point of Collettey's Cookies, today a profitable company based in Charlestown, a Boston neighborhood. But before getting there, Collette Divitto had had to face, month after month, a harder reality: a diploma in hand and no door opening.

Born in 1990, she grows up with Down syndrome and an early passion for baking — she says she started cooking at the age of four. The high school years are rougher: social isolation and, according to her own recollections as reported in the American press, episodes of bullying. She nonetheless holds on to cooking classes, then enters a post-secondary program at Clemson University, in South Carolina, designed for young adults with intellectual disabilities, which she completes at 26. Diploma in hand, she moves to Boston on her own and applies for jobs in bakeries and pastry shops. The rejections pile up. She is told, politely, that she is not a good fit, without the real reason ever being named.

Rather than keep pressing employers who do not want her, Collette Divitto chooses to create her own job. With her mother, she invests a little under $20,000 in personal savings, most of it swallowed up by the rental of a licensed commercial kitchen. No outside investor agrees to back them: Rosemary Alfredo will later recount that the main obstacle had less to do with the numbers than with persistent doubt about her daughter's abilities.

The success at the Golden Goose Market does not stay quiet for long. A local CBS affiliate airs a story on the young entrepreneur at the very end of 2016; the segment spreads, shared on social media, and orders soon pour in from across the country. Collette Divitto will tell CNBC, in 2022, that she felt then, for the first time in her life, a sense of confidence and pride she had never known before.

The company grows without giving up its original mission. Its flagship product, "The Amazing Cookie" — chocolate chip and cinnamon — goes on sale online, then on the shelves of 7-Eleven convenience stores and even in the stands of TD Garden, Boston's big sports arena. In 2018, Collette Divitto founds Collettey's Leadership Programs, a nonprofit that trains other entrepreneurs with disabilities to start and sustain their own businesses. That same year, she is reported to have represented the Massachusetts Down Syndrome Congress in an address at the United Nations, in New York.

Fifteen employees work for Collettey's Cookies today, several of them with disabilities. Cumulative revenue already exceeded $1.2 million in early 2022, for more than 550,000 cookies sold, according to figures published at the time by CNBC. Production has kept growing since: in mid-2024, the Greater Boston Chamber of Commerce cited more than 700,000 cookies sold since the beginning. The company also gives a share of its profits — about 5%, according to the same source — to the Collettey's Leadership foundation. According to a local television station, Collettey's products are now said to be carried in more than a thousand outlets across the country, with a recent entry into the retailer Walmart.

That trajectory has also earned Collette Divitto more institutional recognition: in 2024, Massachusetts Governor Maura Healey appoints her to an advisory board devoted to small businesses and the state's economic diversity. That same year, a short documentary about her journey, "The 17 Percent," directed by Aaron Johnson — then a student at Chapman University, in California — wins a Student Academy Award in the documentary category, before becoming eligible for the Oscars. The film's title refers to the estimated employment rate of people with neurological disabilities in the United States.

From her beginnings marked by rejection, Collette Divitto retains above all the conviction that pushed her to keep going. "I started my business when no one would hire me," she will sum up in 2024 to the trade magazine Specialty Food — a sentence that says, better than any long speech, where a company born of rejection came from, one that has become, fifteen jobs later, an employer for other people who had first been told, as she had, that they were not the right fit.
