On the last day of swimming competition at the Rio Paralympic Games, in September 2016, Daniel Dias dives into the Olympic Park pool one final time, for the mixed 4x100 m medley relay. The stands, firmly on his side, chant his name. The Brazilian team finishes third. That bronze, almost a footnote on paper, brings to twenty-four the number of Paralympic medals the swimmer has accumulated since his debut in Beijing eight years earlier. That day, in the relative anonymity of a relay race, Daniel Dias becomes the most decorated male Paralympic swimmer in history.

Daniel de Faria Dias is born on May 24, 1988, in Campinas, in the state of São Paulo. At his birth, doctors note congenital malformations affecting all four limbs. His parents then make a choice that will weigh on everything that follows: rather than steering him toward a special-education track, they enroll him in an ordinary school. Young Daniel grows up there among the other children, with the daily difficulties that entails, but without his disability organizing his entire existence.

Nothing, at that point, marks him out for elite swimming. It is at sixteen, in 2004, that he discovers on television the Athens Paralympic Games and the career of Clodoaldo Silva, a Brazilian swimmer already crowned with world titles. The spark is immediate. Daniel Dias gets in the water and, in two months, learns to master all four strokes — freestyle, backstroke, breaststroke, butterfly. His progress is so rapid that, barely two years later, he competes in his first World Championships, in Durban, and leaves with five medals, including three titles.

Beijing, in 2008, marks his entry into the big leagues. At twenty, at his first Paralympic Games, he becomes one of the most decorated athletes of the entire Games, all sports combined: four titles, four silver medals and one bronze. That haul earns him, the following year, the first of his three Laureus World Sports Awards, the honor given each year to the world's best athlete with a disability.

London, in 2012, confirms that Beijing was no isolated flash. Entered in six events — freestyle, backstroke, butterfly and medley, in the S5, SB4 and SM5 classes, reserved for swimmers with the heaviest impairments across all four limbs on a scale running from S1, the most severe impairment, to S10, the mildest — Daniel Dias wins gold in every race, breaking the world record each time. A second Laureus Award, received in 2013, salutes that perfect harvest.

Rio, in 2016, is the peak of a career built at home under the eyes of an entire country. There he adds four more Paralympic titles, bringing his total to fourteen gold medals in three editions of the Games. It is in that famous last-day relay that he passes the twenty-four-medal mark and enters the history of his sport. The same year, he receives a third Laureus Award, a record in that category that no other athlete with a disability has equaled since.

In January 2021, Daniel Dias announces that the Tokyo Games, postponed by a year because of the pandemic, will be the last of his career. He explains that he has weighed the decision for a long time, driven by the desire to turn to new goals, and confides that he never imagined, when he started out, going as far as sport has taken him. Being able to say goodbye at the Paralympic movement's biggest event, he adds, has something special about it. In Tokyo, the following summer, he picks up three more bronze medals, bringing his final Paralympic total to twenty-seven medals, fourteen of them gold — an all-time record among male Paralympic swimmers.

Over the whole of his career, Daniel Dias will also have collected some forty medals at the World Championships, thirty-one of them gold, and an unbeaten record at the Parapan American Games, where he has never stood on a podium anywhere but the top step. Alongside his competitive years, he had pursued studies in mechatronics engineering and physical education, later completed by a degree in marketing. Since retiring from sport, he has devoted himself to the institute that bears his name, founded in 2014 to promote access to sport for people with disabilities, and sits on Brazil's athletes' commission. Married to Raquel Andrade Ribeiro Dias, whom he met in 2007, and a father, he says today that he finds his greatest reward in the words his children say to him when he comes home from a long day of training.
