On March 14, 2004, in a room at the Museum of the History of Science in Oxford, a 25-year-old Briton sits down in front of a blackboard that once belonged to Albert Einstein. Facing him, mathematicians are preparing to check, digit by digit, what he is about to recite entirely from memory: the first 22,514 decimal places of pi. He has prepared for a few weeks, learning the long sequence backwards as well as forwards. It will take him five hours and nine minutes, seated without a break, to reach the end of the exercise, an ordeal he would later compare to a marathon run entirely inside the head. Numbers, he would explain afterward, are in a way his first language; what he did that day was therefore closer to a test of physical endurance. The performance goes into the record books as a European record, not a world one, and also serves as a fundraiser for the National Society for Epilepsy. A choice that, as will become clear, is no accident.

The man's name is Daniel Tammet. Nothing in his childhood in Barking and Dagenham, in London's working-class East End, seemed to point him toward a room full of Oxford mathematicians. The eldest of nine children, he goes through a series of severe epileptic seizures at the age of three, which stop once he is treated. An episode like that usually leaves after-effects on the memory; in his case, the opposite happens. What remains is a radically singular way of perceiving the world, in which numbers appear to him spontaneously as shapes, colors and textures. "I see numbers in my head as colors and shapes and textures," he would later tell American television. "So when I see a long sequence, the sequence forms landscapes in my mind."

It is shortly after the Oxford feat that this perception finally gets a name. Simon Baron-Cohen, of the Autism Research Centre at the University of Cambridge, examines him and delivers a triple diagnosis: Asperger's syndrome, synesthesia and savant syndrome. The American researcher Darold Treffert would place him among fewer than a hundred "prodigious savants" recorded worldwide, people in whom extraordinary abilities, here mathematical and linguistic, coexist with autistic functioning.

The name itself is a choice. Born Daniel Paul Corney, he has it legally changed to Tammet, an Estonian word associated with the oak — a language he learned out of sheer personal attraction, well before anyone took a public interest in his linguistic gifts.

British television puts that attraction to the test the very next year. For the documentary "The Boy with the Amazing Brain," broadcast on Channel 4 in 2005, Daniel Tammet takes on a challenge: to learn Icelandic, reputed to be one of the most complex languages in Europe, in a single week, with total immersion to back him up. Seven days later, he appears on the set of Kastljós, the flagship news program of the Icelandic public broadcaster RÚV, and holds a full conversation in the language, in front of an entire country.

In 2006, he tells the story of that childhood and that extraordinary perception of the world in a book, "Born on a Blue Day," published in France the following year as "Je suis né un jour bleu." The book becomes an international success, translated into some twenty languages and praised by critics — the magazine Booklist went so far as to compare his memoir to Benjamin Franklin's — before being named, in 2008, best book for young adults by the American Library Association.

That success opens for Daniel Tammet a writing career he would never leave, with "Embracing the Wide Sky" in 2009, "Thinking in Numbers" in 2012, then "Every Word Is a Bird We Teach to Sing" in 2017. In 2011, he takes the TED stage to describe his linguistic, numerical and visual synesthesia to an audience that would pass two million views online. As early as 2002, well before the recognition, he had also cofounded Optimnem, an online language-learning platform, with his partner at the time, the engineer Neil Mitchell. A first-class honors graduate of the Open University, elected a fellow of the Royal Society of Arts in 2012, he speaks about ten languages today. He now lives in Paris, married to the photographer Jérôme Tabet, whom he met on a promotional tour for his book — far, by now, from the silence of Einstein's blackboard and the five hours spent unspooling, one digit after another, the decimals of pi.
