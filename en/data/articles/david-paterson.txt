It is a little past noon on Monday, March 17, 2008, when David Paterson rises before the two chambers of the New York State legislature, gathered in Albany. The resignation of Eliot Spitzer, caught up in a prostitution scandal, has only just taken effect. No campaign, no election: lieutenant governor for a little over a year, Paterson is sworn in under constitutional emergency. "Today is Monday. There is work to be done," he tells the assembly, before introducing himself to a state that still barely knows him: "Let me reintroduce myself. I am David Paterson and I am the governor of New York State." He becomes, that day, the 55th governor of New York — the first legally blind governor in the state's history.

Born on May 20, 1954, in Brooklyn, he grows up in a political family: his father, Basil Paterson, would become a state senator and then the first Black New Yorker appointed secretary of state. At three months, an ear infection in the infant spreads to the optic nerve: it costs him all the vision in his left eye and leaves him only very limited sight in his right. His mother, Portia, refuses to let him learn braille or use a white cane or a guide dog. "I think she wanted me to use my own ingenuity and creativity to try to be as adept at governing obstacles as possible," he would tell CBS News decades later. The family moves to Hempstead, on Long Island, so that David can attend regular school rather than be steered into special education: he becomes the first student with a disability to be mainstreamed in the town's public schools, graduating from high school in 1971.

He goes on to Columbia University, where he earns a bachelor's degree in history in 1977, before entering Hofstra Law School. He fails the New York bar exam — which he would attribute to the absence of accommodations suited to his vision — and works nonetheless at the Queens district attorney's office, then for the Manhattan borough president, David Dinkins.

In 1985, at 31, he is elected to the New York State Senate to represent Harlem, the seat his father once held. He stays there more than twenty years and becomes Democratic minority leader, the first non-white person to lead a legislative caucus in the state. In 2004, he is the first visually impaired speaker to address a Democratic National Convention. Two years later, Eliot Spitzer picks him as his running mate; the ticket is elected with 69 percent of the vote.

The term Paterson inherits in March 2008 is nothing like an electoral victory: it is an institutional bequest, in the middle of one of the worst financial crises the state has faced since the 1970s, with a budget deficit running into billions of dollars and a hostile legislature. He also marks his term by ordering, as early as 2008, that state agencies recognize same-sex marriages performed elsewhere — a step ahead of the legalization of same-sex marriage in the state, voted in 2011.

Paterson is not, however, the first blind American to lead a state: that precedent belongs to Bob C. Riley, in Arkansas, totally blind after a wound received in combat on Guam in 1944, who served as acting governor for eleven days in January 1975, between Dale Bumpers's resignation and the swearing-in of his elected successor. Paterson remains, by far, the blind person who has held the office of governor of an American state the longest: nearly three years, until December 31, 2010.

The end of his term is tarnished by two separate affairs, unconnected to his vision. In early 2010, the New York Times reveals that a close aide, David Johnson, is the subject of a domestic violence complaint; people around Paterson are said to have contacted the complainant before she withdrew her request for a protective order. An independent investigation, entrusted to former state chief judge Judith Kaye, concludes that these facts do not amount to witness tampering, while criticizing the governor's judgment in having relied solely on his aide's account; no criminal charges are brought against him. That same year, the state Commission on Public Integrity fines him roughly $62,000 for having stated under oath that he had always intended to pay for 2009 World Series tickets he had in fact received for free — here again without criminal prosecution. Under pressure, on February 26, 2010, he gives up his bid for reelection and leaves office on December 31 of that year, never having been elected governor at the ballot box.

Life after Albany sees him reinvent himself: host of a daily show on the radio station WOR from 2011, adjunct professor of political science at New York University, then professor of public health policy at Touro College, in Harlem. In 2014, Governor Andrew Cuomo names him chairman of the New York State Democratic Party. In 2020, he publishes his memoir, Black, Blind, and In Charge: A Story of Overcoming Adversity and Visionary Leadership. In March 2023, on the fifteenth anniversary of his swearing-in, a historic Albany building is renamed in his honor.

What remains is the image of that Monday in March 2008, when a man who had never campaigned for the job presented himself to the state he would lead for nearly three years: "Let me reintroduce myself. I am David Paterson and I am the governor of New York State."
