In October 2009, a rally car sets off on the icy hairpin bends of the Raid de Himalaya, an event known for its demands: eight days, 1,700 kilometers, peaks at 5,500 meters of altitude where the air thins and the thermometer drops below zero. At the wheel of a modified vehicle she drives herself, a driver who cannot use her legs. Deepa Malik is not discovering hardship that day: driving, moving forward, pushing back a limit is what she has been doing for years.

This Indian woman from Haryana, born on September 30, 1970, will later become a figure of world Paralympic sport. But before the athletics tracks, there were the rallies: she obtains one of the first licenses issued to a person with a disability by the Federation of Motor Sports Clubs of India, crosses nine high-altitude passes in Ladakh in 2011, then drives from Chennai to Delhi in her car in 2013 — 3,278 kilometers in one go, a national record for a paraplegic woman.

The daughter of an Indian Army officer — her brother will himself become a brigadier — she grows up surrounded by sport and movement. An active young woman with a passion for motorcycles, she marries Bikram Singh Malik, also an officer, with whom she shares that taste for two wheels. Two daughters are born of their union, Devika and Ambika; the elder, today a graduate in sport psychology, will grow up in her mother's sporting wake.

The illness, for its part, goes back to childhood. At five, Deepa Malik is treated for three years for a spinal cord tumor. She recovers, grows up, marries, becomes a mother. Then, in 1999, at twenty-nine, the tumor reappears. Three operations are needed to remove it; their aftereffects leave her paralyzed in the lower limbs. Her elder daughter is going through her own health problems at the same time, and Deepa Malik has to relearn everything — getting around, running her household, being a mother — from a wheelchair, she who until then had lived to the rhythm of motorcycles and sport.

Six years of rehabilitation follow. A retired army cook, met by chance, suggests she throw herself into a new activity: she opens a restaurant in Ahmednagar, which she runs from 2003 to 2010, employing young people from disadvantaged backgrounds. It is in those same years that she returns to competition, first through swimming — silver at the FESPIC Games in Kuala Lumpur in 2006, then a one-kilometer swim against the current of the Yamuna River in 2008 — before devoting herself to athletics: javelin, discus and shot put, in the F53 class, reserved for athletes with a lower-limb impairment.

She qualifies for the Beijing Games in 2008 in the javelin, without winning a medal there. The podiums then come one by one: bronze at the 2010 Asian Para Games, silver in the shot put at the World Championships in Christchurch in 2011, then an Asian record in the javelin at the Asian Para Games in Incheon in 2014.

The summit comes in Rio de Janeiro, in September 2016. In the F53 shot put final, Deepa Malik sends the shot 4.61 meters and takes silver — becoming the first Indian woman, across all disciplines, to win a Paralympic medal. That evening, she tells the BBC that it is incredible to fulfill a lifelong ambition, and says she hopes her country will finally wake up and recognize the potential of Paralympic sport. The state of Haryana and the Indian government will, in the aftermath, pay her several tens of millions of rupees in reward.

The honors follow her path more than they herald it. India presents her with the Arjuna Award as early as 2012, then the Padma Shri, one of the country's highest civilian decorations, in 2017. In 2018, at forty-eight, she climbs onto a continental podium once more, with two bronze medals at the Asian Para Games in Jakarta. The following year, she becomes the first female Paralympic athlete to receive the Khel Ratna, India's highest sporting honor.

In February 2020, she is elected president of the Paralympic Committee of India, a term she will hold until 2024. The woman who once battled, alone at the wheel, to complete a Himalayan raid now sits at the tables where the future of Paralympic sport in her country is decided — a role that extends, in another form, the same stubbornness that carried her from the three operations of 1999 to the podium in Rio.
