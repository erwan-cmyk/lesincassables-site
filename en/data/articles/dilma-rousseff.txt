On January 1, 2011, on the ramp of the Palácio do Planalto in Brasília, Dilma Rousseff receives the green-and-yellow presidential sash from the hands of Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva. Before tens of thousands of Brazilians gathered on the Esplanade of the Ministries, she becomes that day the first woman elected to the presidency of Brazil, the most populous country in Latin America. Few people in the crowd, at that moment, grasp the full length of the road traveled to get there.

Forty years earlier, on the night of January 16, 1970, the young Dilma Rousseff, twenty-two, is arrested in São Paulo. Involved since the late 1960s in clandestine armed resistance movements opposed to the military dictatorship that has ruled Brazil since 1964, she is taken to the premises of the political police known as Oban, where she endures twenty-two days of interrogation: beatings, including a blow to the face that will cost her a tooth, the palmatória — the wooden paddle used to strike detainees — and electric shocks. A fellow prisoner will later recount that she had even been given electric shocks with a simple car wire. Released in late 1972 after nearly three years of detention, she leaves prison ten kilos lighter and carries away from those months a thyroid disease that will never leave her.

She rebuilds herself far from the spotlight. An economist by training, she settles in Porto Alegre, in Rio Grande do Sul, where she climbs the ranks of the public administration before joining the federal government alongside Lula, elected president in 2002. Minister of mines and energy from 2003, then minister-chief of the Casa Civil — the equivalent of a general secretariat of the presidency — from 2005, she establishes herself as a rigorous manager, more at home with technical files than on campaign platforms. Yet it is she whom Lula chooses, in 2010, to succeed him at the head of the country and of the Workers' Party.

On April 25, 2009, while holding this key government post, she announces herself that she is being treated for cancer. A routine mammogram has revealed a suspicious lymph node under her left armpit. The diagnosis comes in: diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, a cancer of the lymphatic system detected at an early stage, in a single node. Her doctors, including the oncologist Paulo Hoff, speak at the time of a better than 90 percent chance of a cure. She undergoes four months of chemotherapy, then radiotherapy completed in early September 2009. For seven months, while her hair grows back, she wears a wig in public, before reappearing with her natural hair at the end of the year — her doctors then declare her cured.

Barely a year and a half later, in October 2010, she wins the presidential election against the opposition candidate José Serra, under the banner of the Workers' Party. On the day of her inauguration, her body still bears the marks of two ordeals: a thyroid disease inherited from her political detention forty years earlier, and the recent remission of a cancer. None of it has interrupted her trajectory to the summit of the state.

Narrowly reelected in 2014, Dilma Rousseff sees her second term collapse amid a severe economic recession and a political crisis fueled by the Petrobras corruption scandal, which splatters a large part of the Brazilian political class without, however, leading to personal charges against her. On August 31, 2016, at the end of an impeachment process lasting several months and of massive demonstrations across the country, the Brazilian Senate votes to remove her from office by 61 votes to 20. The grounds cited are budgetary: the pedaladas fiscais, maneuvers consisting of delaying transfers of public funds to artificially dress up the state's accounts. No accusation of personal enrichment or corruption is leveled against her in this proceeding, and no criminal conviction for corruption will be handed down against her. The impeachment remains, to this day, one of the most contested episodes in recent Brazilian political history: many of her supporters, in Brazil and abroad, call it a parliamentary coup, while her opponents defend it as the regular application of the Constitution. The debate is still not settled.

Removed from power, Dilma Rousseff does not leave the international stage. In March 2023, she takes over the presidency of the New Development Bank, the financial institution created by the BRICS countries to finance infrastructure projects in emerging economies, based in Shanghai. Reelected to the post in March 2025 for five more years, she continues, from China, to weigh on the major balances of the world economy.

What remains is a singular path, which twice passes through the ordeal of the body inflicted first by power and then by illness — state torture in the dictatorship's jails, then a cancer fought while carrying full governmental responsibility — without ever deviating from a trajectory that led a former political prisoner all the way to the head of the largest country in Latin America.
