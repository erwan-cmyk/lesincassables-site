One evening at the end of the nineteenth century, Edvard Munch is walking along a road overlooking Kristiania, present-day Oslo, in the company of two friends. The sun is setting, and the sky suddenly turns a blood red that stops him in his tracks. His companions walk on; he stays motionless, seized by the sensation of an infinite scream passing through nature. From this scene, recorded in his notebooks, will emerge the following year one of the most reproduced images in the history of art: The Scream, painted in 1893.

Born on December 12, 1863, in Ådalsbruk, in the municipality of Løten, Edvard Munch grows up in Kristiania in a family struck early by bereavement. His mother, Laura Cathrine, dies of tuberculosis when he is five. Nine years later, his older sister Sophie, to whom he is very close, succumbs to the same disease. A brother dies a few months after his wedding, another sister is diagnosed with mental illness, and their father, a military doctor of anxious temperament and rigid piety, raises his surviving children alone. Munch later sums up this family inheritance in a formula often repeated by his biographers: he said he had inherited two of humanity's worst scourges, tuberculosis and madness.

Despite fragile health and a schooling broken up by repeated illnesses, Munch turns to painting at the end of the 1870s. His stays in Paris and then Berlin, where he frequents circles of Scandinavian writers and artists, nourish a body of work centered on love, anxiety and death, which he gathers under the title of the Frieze of Life. His fame grows in the 1890s and 1900s, alongside an increasingly chaotic personal life, marked by growing alcohol consumption and tormented love affairs.

The most turbulent of these relationships binds him, between 1898 and 1902, to Tulla Larsen, a young woman of the bourgeoisie who wishes to marry him. Munch evades her and breaks things off several times. In September 1902, during an argument, a shot goes off from a pistol and severs part of a finger on his left hand. The exact circumstances of the incident remain debated by historians; no criminal charges result from it. The episode closes an already trying decade and precedes a progressive worsening of his nervous condition.

In the fall of 1908, in Copenhagen, Munch goes through a severe crisis: acute anxiety, hallucinations, a feeling of being persecuted, all amplified by an alcoholism that has by now taken hold. He has himself voluntarily admitted to the private clinic of Dr. Daniel Jacobson, where he stays for about eight months. The treatment combines prolonged rest, a strict diet, baths and electrotherapy, a method then in vogue for treating nervous disorders. From his room, Munch continues to organize exhibitions of his work in Denmark, Germany, Norway and Sweden. Unlike many diagnoses made after the fact about artists long dead, this episode is documented in his lifetime: medical records, testimonies, drawings and writings by Munch himself, including a self-portrait painted at the clinic in 1909. Munch himself drew the link between his psychological fragility and his work: without anxiety and illness, he said in essence, he would have been like a ship without a rudder.

On his release, in the spring of 1909, he gives up alcohol for good and returns to Norway. His art changes register: the colors brighten, the brushstrokes broaden, the subjects turn toward work in the fields and light rather than anguish. He wins the prestigious commission for the murals of the Aula of the University of Kristiania and becomes an official figure of Norwegian art, decorated with the Royal Order of St. Olav. Art historians remain divided on this period: some judge the late output calmer but less inhabited than that of the 1890s, while others praise a body of work that remained abundant and demanding into old age.

Settled from 1916 on the Ekely estate, on the outskirts of Oslo, Munch paints there in relative solitude for nearly thirty years, surrounded by hundreds of canvases. In the 1930s, the Nazi regime classifies part of his work as so-called degenerate art and removes some eighty of his paintings from German museums; Munch refuses any appropriation of his work for propaganda purposes. He dies at Ekely on January 23, 1944, at the age of 80. He bequeaths to the city of Oslo everything still in his possession, roughly 1,100 paintings, 4,500 drawings and 18,000 prints, a collection at the origin of the Munch Museum, now housed in a new building opened in 2021 in Bjørvika, on the shores of the Oslo fjord.
