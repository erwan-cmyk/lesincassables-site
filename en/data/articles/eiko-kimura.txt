In the summer of 2019, for the first time in its history, Japan's House of Councillors physically alters its chamber: an access ramp is installed, rows of seats are removed to make room for electric wheelchairs, and the assembly allows, for the first time ever, personal care assistants to remain at lawmakers' sides during sessions. These accommodations are designed for two new members, newly elected on the list of the young Reiwa Shinsengumi party, who take their seats on July 29: Yasuhiko Funago and Eiko Kimura. That day, it is Japan's parliament that has to adapt to its elected members, and not the other way around.

Eiko Kimura is eight months old when she falls from her baby walker, tumbling down a step at her parents' home. The impact damages her cervical vertebrae and leaves her quadriplegic: since then, she can move only her right hand, the one she now uses to steer her electric wheelchair to her seat in parliament. Some sources also mention a later diagnosis of cerebral palsy, without the exact link to that early childhood accident being clearly established. What is certain is that this near-total immobility has never stopped her from waging, for decades, one of the most concrete political battles in contemporary Japan.

Because before sitting in parliament, Kimura spends most of her adult life in the field. In 1994, in Tama, on the outskirts of Tokyo, she founds an organization called Tsubasa, designed to help severely disabled people leave institutions or the family home and live independently, supported by personal assistants rather than managed by a facility. Some fifteen people will regain their independence this way thanks to Tsubasa. Kimura also writes a play inspired by their real-life stories, which she performs across Japan and then, once, in New York. It is there that she learns that a resident of an American facility had not had a single visitor from outside in twenty years — proof, she realizes, that the isolation of severely disabled people is not unique to Japan.

In 2019, she runs on the national list of Reiwa Shinsengumi, a left-wing party founded by the actor turned politician Taro Yamamoto, which makes the inclusion of people with disabilities and of parents living in precarious conditions one of the planks of its campaign. The proportional vote gives the party two seats: hers and Funago's. Kimura thus becomes, alongside Funago, one of the first two people so severely disabled to sit in the House of Councillors.

Once elected, she gives no ground. A member of the Committee on Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism, she directly challenges the minister over the cramped spaces reserved for wheelchairs on high-speed trains, with photographs to back it up, taken from her own wheelchair on a Shinkansen where only two spots were set aside for her. The spaces will go from two to six. Since her arrival, the Diet has also had ramps, elevators and accessible restrooms installed — accommodations she had been demanding for years without being heard. Kimura also sits on the Committee on Fundamental National Policies and on a special committee on development assistance, and campaigns for a reform of social care services, so that personal care assistants can accompany people with disabilities outside their homes — at work, at university, in leisure activities — something Japanese law still restricts today.

Her daily life as a lawmaker remains, by necessity, a matter of constant adaptation. To draft a text, she dictates to an assistant who types or writes in her place; to follow the news, she relies on her phone's voice recognition. She admits to feeling awkward about keeping her assistants waiting, to the point of speaking faster than she would like, even though they never take offense.

What is striking, in the way she speaks, is the absence of bitterness and the steadiness of her course. "I believe that a better society for people with disabilities will also be better for everyone else to live in," she explains. She observes that many of her fellow lawmakers have never, in their entire lives, spent time around a person with a disability — a separation that begins as early as school — and that some, out of awkwardness, address her assistants rather than her directly. "I think one of the reasons is that people with disabilities are not valued members of society and are seen as a burden," she says. Her goal fits in a single sentence, as simple to state as it is immense to achieve: "I want to see more people who want to know more about people with disabilities."

Since that summer of 2019 when two seats were removed from the chamber to let her in, Eiko Kimura continues, right hand resting on the controls of her wheelchair, to shift the lines of a parliament that, before her, had never had to make room for someone like her.
