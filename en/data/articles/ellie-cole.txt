At an aquatic center in Melbourne, a two-year-old girl enters the big pool for the first time. The instructors warn the family: it will probably take a year before she can swim in a straight line. Ellie Cole manages it in two weeks.

That speed is no isolated fluke. A few months earlier, a rare tumor affecting the nerves of her right leg, a neurosarcoma, had been diagnosed. Chemotherapy fails to stop it and, in February 1994, the leg is amputated above the knee. Eight weeks later, her mother enrolls her in swimming lessons, more to help her rehabilitation than to make an athlete of her. Water becomes, almost by accident, the ground on which Ellie Cole will build her entire life.

She grows up in the suburbs of Melbourne, between the schools of Mount Eliza and Frankston, and trains seriously from the early 2000s. She will later recount that as a child, in the 1990s, she never came across a character who looked like her in the books she read, and that she remembers the sometimes hurtful reactions of other children to her missing leg. In 2006, she wins her first international medal, a silver, in the 100 m backstroke at the para swimming World Championships in Durban. Classified S9, the category for swimmers with a single-limb amputation, she joins the Australian team for the Beijing Paralympic Games, in 2008. She is sixteen. She leaves with three medals, including a silver in the 100 m butterfly.

Four years later, in London, she establishes herself as one of the most complete swimmers of her generation: four Paralympic titles, in the 100 m backstroke, the 100 m freestyle and two relays, rounded out by two bronze medals. Her career nearly comes to an abrupt halt right afterward, though: two shoulder reconstruction operations keep her away from the pool for a long time. She returns all the same and triumphs at the World Championships in Glasgow, in 2015, with three titles, including a world record in the 100 m backstroke.

In Rio, in 2016, she adds six medals to her total, two of them gold, among them the 4x100 m freestyle relay, which the Australian team wins while setting a new world record. The same year, Ellie Cole also breaks the world record in the S9 50 m freestyle, until then held by South Africa's Natalie du Toit, an athlete she has long described as her role model.

The Tokyo Games, held in 2021 because of the pandemic, are the last of her career. There she adds a silver and a bronze medal in relays, bringing her total to seventeen Paralympic medals collected over four consecutive editions, a record that makes her the most decorated female Paralympian in Australian history. She carries the national flag at the closing ceremony.

She could have stopped there. She chooses to extend by one season, through the Commonwealth Games in Birmingham, in the summer of 2022, to say a better goodbye to years of training shared with the able-bodied swimmers of the Australian team, whom she regards as a second team. Her retirement from sport becomes official in August 2022. Once away from the pool, she takes an unsparing look at the way her disability has long been perceived solely through the prism of sporting performance. She tells the public broadcaster ABC: "It's a shame that to be seen with a disability in Australia at the moment, to be really seen, you have to become a world-class athlete, and that's ridiculous."

Since then, Ellie Cole has stayed in the public sphere without occupying the same role. She appears in the Netflix documentary Rising Phoenix, about the Paralympic movement, hosts coverage of the Paris 2024 Paralympic Games, sits on the board of Paralympics Australia and takes part, the same year, in the reality show I'm a Celebrity... Get Me Out of Here!. Made a Member of the Order of Australia in 2024, she becomes a mother the same year with her partner Silvia Scognamiglio, whom she met at the London Games, where Scognamiglio was working for the Paralympic committee. Their son, Felix, born prematurely, gives his name to the main character of a children's book she publishes shortly afterward, some of whose characters are inspired by Paralympic athletes she has known: the story of a boy who discovers disability by making new friends at school. The book she would have liked to find, as a little girl, on the library shelves of her native Australia.
