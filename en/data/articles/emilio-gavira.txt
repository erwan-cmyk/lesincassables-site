On February 17, 2013, at the Goya Awards ceremony in Madrid, a name not much used to red carpets slips in among the nominees for best new actor: that of Emilio Gavira, for his role as Jesusín in Blancanieves, by Pablo Berger. The film, shot in black and white and without dialogue, transposes the Brothers Grimm tale to the bullfighting Spain of the 1920s, where the heroine finds refuge with a troupe of dwarf bullfighters. Gavira leaves without the statuette that night, but he enters, for the first time, the very small circle of Spanish cinema's rising names.

He is born on December 14, 1964, in Fuengirola, on the Andalusian coast. Around the age of five, his parents move to Alcázar de San Juan, in Castilla-La Mancha, where he grows up and goes to school at the Trinitarian college. It is there, under the guidance of a teacher known as Father Isidro, that he steps on stage for the first time, in an entremés by Cervantes. It is there too that he takes his first piano lessons, with Tere Garrido, at the nearby conservatory in Campo de Criptana. Of that adopted town, he likes to quote a Spanish saying, he told El Semanal de la Mancha in 2019: "uno es de donde pace y no de donde nace" — you are from where you grew up, not only from where you were born.

In 1985, he leaves for Madrid to study music. He starts on the harpsichord, then enters the Escuela Superior de Canto, where he trains as a baritone. Madrid's lyric stages, among them the National Auditorium, take him on as a singer of opera and zarzuela, the popular Spanish lyric form. Of his shift toward comedy and film, he gave, in the same interview, a disarming explanation: "Descubrí que era lo mío cuando empezaron a pagarme" — I found out it was my thing when they started paying me.

The big screen opens to him in 1998, when the director Javier Fesser gives him the part of an extraterrestrial in El milagro de P. Tinto, a black comedy that became a cult film in Spain. That same year, he joins the cast of the stage production Carmen, ópera sangrienta, then Los misterios de la ópera in 2000: the singer gradually becomes an actor, without ever quite leaving the opera stage.

His film career changes scale in 2003 with La gran aventura de Mortadelo y Filemón, an enormous popular hit adapted from a cult Spanish comic strip, in which he plays Rompetechos, a character he would take up again in 2014 in the sequel, Mortadelo y Filemón contra Jimmy el Cachondo. In between, Javier Fesser calls him back for something else entirely, drama, with Camino (2008). His filmography also includes El puente de San Luis Rey, adapted from the Thornton Wilder novel.

But it is Blancanieves, in 2012, that brings him his broadest recognition: a Goya nomination for best new actor and, the same season, a nomination for the Medals of the Spanish Cinema Writers Circle. He then alternates comedy and drama, through to Matar a Dios (2017), in which he plays a vagrant who claims to be God — a role that wins him the best actor prize at the Gáldar International Festival in 2018 — and then El fotógrafo de Mauthausen, the same year. On television, he appears in Cuéntame cómo pasó, a cult series on Spanish public television, as well as in Agente 700 and Olmos y Robles. On stage, he plays Valle-Inclán (Divinas palabras, at the National Drama Center), Shakespeare and Fletcher (Enrique VIII y la cisma de Inglaterra, with the National Classical Theater Company) and Lorca (Amor de Don Perlimplín con Belisa en su jardín).

In September 2021, Alcázar de San Juan pays him a tribute uncommon for an actor still fully at work: the city council gives his name to the town's theater-auditorium, opened twenty-two years earlier. On the 18th, alongside Mayor Rosa Melchor, in front of the new plaque, he tells the local press: "No tengo palabras" — I have no words.

On set as in the press, Emilio Gavira has never dodged questions about the way his body is looked at. A guest in 2024 on La Revuelta, David Broncano's show, which has become the most watched on Spanish television, he publicly criticizes a joke about dwarfism made shortly before by Alfonso Guerra, a former deputy prime minister of Spain, on a rival channel. The following summer, approached by the daily El Español after the controversy around the birthday party of the soccer player Lamine Yamal — where people of short stature were reportedly hired as entertainment — he refuses any summary condemnation and calls for light to be shed on the real conditions of that work.

A member of the Spanish Film Academy and of the Academy of Performing Arts, he goes on splitting his time between film sets, the theater and the opera stage. On the substance, his position has barely shifted in fifteen years of interviews. "Yo soy enano. Ya está." — I'm a dwarf, that's it —, he was still saying in 2024 to the outlet El Generacional, before adding, not without professional pride: "Yo he interpretado papeles que ya quisieran actores de 1,90 cm" — I've played roles that actors of 1.90 would love to have.
