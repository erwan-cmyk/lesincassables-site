On the Khumbu ridge, above 6,000 meters, a man plants his trekking poles in the snow to measure the width of a crevasse before jumping. Around him, the seracs crack and avalanches rumble in the distance, over toward the Lo La pass. He sees none of it — neither the deep blue of the ice nor the void beneath his crampons — but he hears it, counts it, imagines it. On that day in May 2001, somewhere between base camp and the roof of the world, Erik Weihenmayer is about to become the first blind person to reach the summit of Everest.

Born on September 23, 1968, in Princeton, New Jersey, he grows up between Florida, Hong Kong and then Connecticut. Juvenile retinoschisis, diagnosed when he is only fifteen months old, gradually eats away at his retina; doctors predict complete blindness around the age of thirteen. For a long time the boy refuses the white cane and braille, clinging to everything that still connects him to the sighted world. "I couldn't live in a bubble," he would later tell ABILITY Magazine: he water-skis, runs in the woods, jumps off rocks. The medical prediction comes true: at thirteen, Erik Weihenmayer is completely blind.

Three years later, at a summer camp for visually impaired young people, he touches a climbing wall for the first time and discovers that he can "read" the rock with his fingertips. That same year, he receives his first guide dog. Captain of the wrestling team at his Connecticut high school, then a student at Boston College, where he earns a double major in English and communications, he becomes a teacher and wrestling coach at a middle school in Phoenix, Arizona. It is there that he meets Ellie Reeve, his future wife, in 1993.

Climbing becomes a through line. In 1995, he reaches the summit of Denali, in Alaska. The following year, he makes the first ascent by a blind climber of "The Nose," on El Capitan, in the Yosemite Valley — four days of vertical rock alongside the climber Hans Florine. In 1997, he marries Ellie on the summit of Kilimanjaro, which he has just climbed. Aconcagua, in Argentina, follows in 1999, then Mount Vinson, in Antarctica, in January 2000. Four of the seven summits are done; the highest remains.

On May 25, 2001, at ten o'clock in the morning Nepal time, Erik Weihenmayer sets foot on the summit of Everest, at the end of the National Federation of the Blind-Allegra expedition led by Pasquale Scaturro. Nineteen climbers — among them the father and son Sherman and Brad Bull — reach the summit that day, a record for the mountain. To get across crevasses and ladders, Weihenmayer measures distances with his poles and takes his bearings from the bells attached to his teammates, who describe the terrain to him out loud. "The dude just ran away from us," Scaturro would say of him to Sports Illustrated. Time magazine, which put him on its cover, would write that "no one has ever done anything like it."

He then climbs Elbrus, in Russia, on June 13, 2002, and Mount Kosciuszko, in Australia, on September 5, 2002: he becomes the first blind person, and one of only some hundred and fifty mountaineers, to have completed the Seven Summits. In 2008, he adds Carstensz Pyramid, in Indonesia, a summit included in another definition of the Seven Summits — thereby closing out the eight highest points of the seven continents.

In 2003, he co-founds No Barriers, an organization that designs expeditions and programs to help people with disabilities, wounded veterans and struggling teenagers push their limits. "A mindset that helps you find purpose in life," he would sum up for AFAR magazine. In 2011, the organization launches No Barriers Warriors, aimed at veterans marked by war.

Other expeditions follow. In September 2014, with Lonnie Bedwell, a Navy veteran who had gone blind, he kayaks the 277 miles of the Grand Canyon in twenty-one days. In 2010, on a tandem bike, he finishes the Leadville 100, a mountain ultra-endurance race. In September 2016, for the twentieth anniversary of his El Capitan ascent, he climbs the wall again by the East Buttress route in under twenty-four hours.

One remark from Pasquale Scaturro, spoken on the descent and recounted by Erik Weihenmayer on his official website, has stayed with him: "Don't make Everest the greatest thing you ever do." Married to Ellie since the ceremony on Kilimanjaro, and a father, he lives today in Golden, Colorado.

In December 2025, Guinness World Records named him an ICON for the whole body of his records and his philosophy of pushing limits. At 57, he is still climbing — granite domes near Rio, north faces in the Dolomites — and remains involved in No Barriers. "We just want to keep changing lives," he tells Guinness World Records. From lectures to expeditions, the former Arizona teacher carries on what he began on the slopes of Denali, in 1995: showing, one summit after another, that blindness was never the central obstacle in his story.
