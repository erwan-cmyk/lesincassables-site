She is fifteen when, in a classroom at the St. Michael computer center, she takes her very first programming course. Her parents, who have just noticed her taste for computers, have enrolled her in the one-year training program. Around her, she would recall later, most of her classmates were probably wondering what she was doing there. She, on the other hand, knows immediately that she has found her path: she "loved the idea of solving problems and creating things," as she would put it. Nearly thirty years later, in April 2022, Farida Bedwei joins Microsoft as a principal software engineer, assigned to work on metaverse technologies, a trajectory that makes her one of the few West African women to hold a position of that scale at one of the largest technology groups in the world.

Farida Nana Efua Bedwei is born on April 6, 1979, in Lagos, Nigeria, to Ghanaian parents. Her father's work with the United Nations Development Programme leads the family to move several times during her childhood: Dominica, Grenada, then the United Kingdom. Diagnosed with cerebral palsy at the age of one, she does not discover Ghana, her family's home country, until she is nine. For lack of suitable schools, her mother then teaches her at home, repeating an instruction that will become a rule for life: to remove the words "I can't" from her vocabulary and replace them with "I'll try." At twelve, Farida enters mainstream education, but stays only three years: the unadapted grounds of Ghanaian secondary schools force her out of the conventional school system once more, until the computer course that, at fifteen, changes everything.

She then stacks up qualifications while working: a one-year diploma in computer science at the University of Hertfordshire, in Britain, in 2004 and 2005, then a certificate in project management from the Ghana Institute of Management and Public Administration, in 2009. Her career begins at Soft Company Ltd as a developer, before she joins Rancard Solutions in 2001, where she will spend nine years climbing the ranks to the position of senior software architect, working in particular on mobile platforms, a content management system and an online payroll application.

The turning point comes in 2010, when she joins a microfinance institution to design digital products. There she discovers an entire slice of the Ghanaian economy, lending to small traders, to cooperatives, to the informal savings collectors known as susu, that still runs largely on paper. With Derrick Dankyi, she cofounds Logiciel Ltd in April 2011 and takes charge of its technology. The company develops gKudi, an online banking platform designed to digitize the management of microfinance institutions. From about a hundred client institutions at the outset, the software will in time equip several hundred across Ghana, giving an informal sector run mostly by women management tools comparable to those of conventional banks.

The success of Logiciel establishes Farida Bedwei as a figure of Ghana's digital scene: the World Economic Forum names her one of its Young Global Leaders in 2016, and several official honors mark her path over the years. But her work is not confined to banking code. Back in 2010, she had published "Definition of a Miracle," a novel drawing heavily on her own childhood, whose heroine, an eight-year-old girl, grows up with cerebral palsy in a country where disability, in her words, "is still largely misunderstood and a bit of a taboo in various parts of Ghana." In 2018, with the studio Leti Arts, she creates Karmzah, a disabled superheroine whose power comes from her crutches, a character born, she explains, "because I found there was no representation of me." The comic finds an international audience. Bedwei also hosts an online awareness show, "Demystifying Disabilities," and has sat since 2013 on the board of Sharecare Ghana, an organization devoted to autoimmune and neurological conditions.

In April 2022, after more than a decade leading Logiciel's technology, she duly joins Microsoft as a principal software engineer, a position she still holds. There she puts at the service of a global group the same rigor that, almost thirty years earlier, had made her stand out in a classroom where no one expected her, without ever ceasing, alongside it, to argue that disability should stop being a taboo subject in Africa's digital economies.
