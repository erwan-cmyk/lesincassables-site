On August 7, 1984, in Los Angeles, the United States women's volleyball team loses three sets to none to China in the Olympic final. A clear-cut score, but an unprecedented feat: the United States had never won an Olympic medal in the sport. At the center of the American setup, one player stands a head above all the others, 1.96 meters, the most feared hitter of the tournament: Flo Hyman. That evening, she goes home with silver, and with a fame that reaches well beyond the world of sport.

Flora Jean Hyman is born in July 1954 in Inglewood, in the suburbs of Los Angeles, the second of eight children. Her father, George Hyman, works for a railroad company; her mother, Warrene, runs a small place of her own, the Pink Kitty Café. The girl quickly outgrows every child her age. "When they were three foot tall, I was four foot tall," she would tell Women's Sports magazine in 1983, remembering the years when she was nicknamed, at school, the "Jolly Green Giant." Her mother, a tall woman herself, teaches her to carry that difference as a strength rather than a burden.

It is on the beaches of southern Los Angeles that she discovers volleyball as a teenager, alongside one of her older sisters, in a sport then played mostly by a white, well-off clientele from the nearby seaside towns. At Morningside High School in Inglewood, her hitting already makes an impression. After a first year of note at El Camino College, she joins the U.S. national program, before enrolling at the University of Houston.

At Houston, she becomes the first woman in the school's history to receive an athletic scholarship. A mathematics major and a three-time All-American, she receives in 1977 the very first Broderick Award, honoring the country's best college athlete. In that same period, she nonetheless breaks off her studies to devote herself entirely to the national team. "You can go to school when you're 60," she would explain in 1979: elite sport, for its part, does not wait.

Under head coach Arie Selinger, the United States team is transformed and Flo Hyman becomes its centerpiece. Her hitting, which the press compares to Julius Erving's flights in basketball, earns her the nickname "Clutchman." Best hitter at the 1981 World Cup in Tokyo, a bronze medalist at the 1982 World Championship, she carries American women's volleyball to a level it had never reached before. "If you want to win the war, you've got to pay the price," she told the New York Times at the time.

That demand pays off in Los Angeles. Captain and oldest player on a team carried along by the Californian crowd, she leads the United States to that Olympic silver medal, which has remained historic. Now a public figure, she puts her fame at the service of causes close to her: she joins Coretta Scott King, Sally Ride and Geraldine Ferraro in campaigns for women's rights, and testifies before Congress in support of Title IX, the law guaranteeing women equal access to college sport.

For lack of a professional league of the right standard in the United States, she then goes to play in Japan, for Daiei, which she helps lift among the country's best teams. There she becomes a popular figure, appears in a string of commercials and even acts in an American action film, The Order of the Black Eagle. She is already looking further ahead: the Seoul Olympic Games, in 1988.

She would never take part. On January 24, 1986, during a Japanese league match between Daiei and Hitachi, in Matsue, in western Japan, Flo Hyman is substituted in the middle of the third set. Sitting on the bench, she collapses. Taken to the city's Red Cross hospital, she is pronounced dead that evening. She is 31. At the time, the first reports point to cardiac arrest.

The truth only emerges after her death. An autopsy performed on January 30 in Culver City, California, rules out a heart attack: Flo Hyman had been suffering, without ever knowing it, from Marfan syndrome, a genetic disorder of the connective tissue that had weakened her aorta until it ruptured. The pathologist Victor Rosen, who carried out the examination, notes "a dime-sized weak spot in her aorta" — a malformation present since birth, but invisible throughout her career, including years of competition at the highest level.

Her sudden death helps make that rare disease better known, still poorly detected at the time, particularly among very tall athletes. As early as 1987, the Women's Sports Foundation created the Flo Hyman Award in her memory, which bears her name and honors each year an athlete embodying "dignity, spirit, and commitment to excellence" — the very qualities that defined her. Martina Navratilova, Chris Evert and Billie Jean King are among its recipients. Inducted into the Volleyball Hall of Fame as early as 1988, Flo Hyman saw, in 2025, her jersey retired by the University of Houston and her name enter the Team USA Hall of Fame. Nearly forty years after her death, the award that bears her name still recalls who she was.
