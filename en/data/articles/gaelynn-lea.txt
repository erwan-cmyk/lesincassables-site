In the very first days of 2016, in the offices of a company in Duluth, Minnesota, Gaelynn Lea and her friend Leah set up a camera after business hours. In the video they are about to shoot for an amateur contest run by the public radio network NPR, the frame at first shows only her hands on the bow and the strings of the violin, not her face, not her wheelchair. That choice is anything but accidental. "I wanted it to be about the music," she would explain a few weeks later, once the news of her win was out.

Gaelynn Lea grows up in Duluth, a port city set on the shores of Lake Superior. Her parents run a local dinner theater, where as a child she works as an usher and a lighting technician. She is born with osteogenesis imperfecta, a genetic condition that makes bones fragile and brings her many fractures in her earliest years. Around the age of nine, an orchestra performs at her elementary school: she comes away shaken, scores perfectly on a test of musical ear, and catches the attention of a teacher, Susan Sommerfeld, who decides to guide her through learning an instrument. It will be the violin, smaller and therefore easier to handle than the cello she had dreamed of at first.

Tucking the instrument under her chin, as classical tradition requires, is impossible for her. With her teacher, she invents her own posture: the violin held upright in front of her, like a miniature cello, braced against her foot so it will not slip, the bow held the way a double bass player holds one. She cannot use her fourth finger and has to rewrite for herself the fingerings of the classical scores she is taught. As a teenager, she turns down an operation, a "rodding," which would have meant inserting metal rods into the bones of her arms to stabilize them, for fear it might damage her nerves and her ability to play. She would tell the BBC much later that she had chosen not to walk, and that she is happy.

After high school, Gaelynn Lea goes off to study political science at Macalester College, in Saint Paul, with the idea of one day becoming a lawyer or a disability rights advocate. It is in this period that she turns toward folk and Celtic music, and starts working with the loop pedals that will let her, alone on stage, layer several tracks of violin and voice. In 2011, she forms the duo The Murder of Crows with Alan Sparhawk, of the band Low. Her first solo album, All the Roads that Lead Us Home, comes out in 2015.

The following year, she sends that video shot with Leah to NPR's Tiny Desk Contest, more than 6,000 entries from all fifty states. Her song, Someday We'll Linger in the Sun, which is about the trials a couple goes through together over time, catches the attention of a jury that includes Dan Auerbach, of The Black Keys, who praises a performance that sets about "absolutely obliterating your heart." In March 2016, Gaelynn Lea is named the winner, against thousands of non-disabled entrants. She flies to Washington to film her concert at NPR headquarters and begins a four-city tour offered by the network.

What follows is the career of a touring musician: tours in the United States and then in Europe, new albums, among them Learning How to Stay in 2018, and stages and studios shared with artists such as Wilco, the Decemberists and Michael Stipe. Alongside that, she keeps teaching violin. On stage, she blends singing, loops and violin in a style that owes as much to her classical training as to the Celtic tradition and to improvisation.

That visibility she also puts to work in a wider fight: making the music industry more accessible, after years of touring venues poorly suited to wheelchairs. In 2021, she co-founds RAMPD alongside the singer Lachi, and becomes its vice president. "Disability is diversity," she sums up in an interview with Buffalo public radio. She defends a similar idea in a TEDxYale talk devoted to sexuality and disability.

In 2022, the director Sam Gold, who had noticed an instrumental piece on her album Learning How to Stay, commissions from her the original music for a Broadway Macbeth, headlined by Daniel Craig and Ruth Negga. From her office in Duluth, she composes more than fifty themes and musical transitions remotely. "What's cool is that the play informs the music," she would say of the experience.

In April 2026, Gaelynn Lea publishes her first book, an autobiographical account titled It Wasn't Meant to Be Perfect, with Algonquin Books. Ten years after her Tiny Desk Contest win, she tells there, in her own blunt way, the story of a life she would not change. "I have an awesome life, just so you know," she said recently, irritated that people assume her days must be unhappy.
