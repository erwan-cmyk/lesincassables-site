In a New Jersey classroom, a teacher one day sets a box of seashells down on his desk. Among the pupils who step forward to look, a ten-year-old boy does not look: he touches. His fingers follow the spirals, count the ridges, compare the rough grain of the outside with the pearly polish of the inside. "I was just enthralled. They were so elegant, so beautiful, such wonderful contrasts between the outsides and the insides, and so on. The shapes were out of this world," he would recall decades later. Out of that box of seashells comes the vocation of Geerat Vermeij, who will become one of the most influential paleobiologists of his generation — without ever having seen a single one of the tens of thousands of shells he would study with his hands.

Geerat J. Vermeij is born on September 28, 1946, in Sappemeer, in the Netherlands. He loses his sight entirely at the age of three — several sources, including the University of California, Davis, where he would later teach, point to childhood glaucoma, though neither his National Academy of Sciences profile nor the MacArthur Foundation's confirms the exact medical cause. His parents, keen naturalists, take him walking and cycling through the Dutch countryside, describe out loud what he cannot touch, and bring him into the family chores like any of his brothers and sisters. He learns braille at the Prins Alexander Stichting boarding school, in Huis ter Heide, before his family emigrates to the United States in 1955 and settles in New Jersey.

Vermeij goes through mainstream schooling, then enters Princeton, where he earns a degree in biology and geology in 1968, before moving on to Yale for a doctorate completed in 1971. He goes on to teach zoology at the University of Maryland for nearly twenty years, before joining, in 1989, the Department of Earth and Planetary Sciences at the University of California, Davis, where he is today professor emeritus.

His method has not changed since that New Jersey classroom: his hands. Vermeij "reads" a shell the way others read a text — the thickness of the wall, the presence of ribs or spines, the shape of the aperture, a repair scar left by the attack of a crab or a fish millions of years earlier. Out of these tactile observations, repeated across tens of thousands of fossil and living specimens collected on expeditions to Guam, the Philippines, the Galápagos, Panama, and the Aleutian Islands, he builds his theory of "escalation": an evolutionary arms race in which prey and predators endlessly refine their respective weapons and defenses. In the field, sighted assistants — often his wife, the biologist Edith Zipser, whom he met at Yale — guide him and describe the colors to him; he carries out the hands-on examination that has become the signature of his work. "Most people who try to imagine what it's like to be blind think of blackness... The world for me is not black," he would explain in 2005 on the American public radio network NPR.

Out of this patient work comes a considerable body of writing: more than 300 scientific papers and several books that are standard references in their field, from Evolution and Escalation (1987) to Nature: An Economic History (2004), by way of his autobiography, Privileged Hands, published in 1996 — where he writes: "My family effectively broke through the information barrier by making available the full richness of the print media to me." In 2023 comes The Evolution of Power, which extends his theory of evolutionary competition all the way to human societies. "We are without any question the most powerful species, both collectively and individually, that has ever lived on Earth," he writes there.

This work earns him a MacArthur Fellowship in 1992, the famous "genius grant," then, in 2000, the Daniel Giraud Elliot Medal from the National Academy of Sciences: according to the citation quoted by the University of California, Davis, he is honored for having "extract[ed] major generalizations about biological evolution from the fossil record, by feeling details of shell anatomy that other scientists only see." Twenty-two years later, in 2022, the Academy elects him to its membership in turn.

Asked what accounts for such a singular career, Vermeij points less to blindness than to a discipline of work: "If you don't take academic or intellectual risks, you're not going to be very interesting," he told NPR, again. Now professor emeritus, he keeps writing and publishing — the same hands that had closed around a box of seashells in a New Jersey classroom go on, nearly seventy years later, deciphering by touch several hundred million years of competition between prey and predators.
