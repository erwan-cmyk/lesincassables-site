On May 31, 1973, on the tarmac of Reykjavik airport, the photographers covering the meeting between Georges Pompidou and Richard Nixon notice a puffy face and a heavier gait than usual. In the press, no one knows why. What will only come out much later is that the American intelligence services, for their part, are already looking for the answer: according to later revelations from CIA archives, this official trip to Iceland was reportedly used to have the French president's urine discreetly analyzed, in the hope of identifying the disease that seems to be eating away at him. Pompidou himself will never say a public word about his health. He will die ten months later without ever having spoken, before the French people, the name of the illness that was killing him.

Nothing in his path destined him for this stubborn silence. Born in 1911 in Montboudif, in the Cantal, the son of schoolteachers, Georges Pompidou climbs the ladder of the republican meritocracy with rare steadiness: a graduate of the École normale supérieure, ranked first in the agrégation in literature in 1934, a teacher of classics in Marseille and then at the Lycée Henri-IV in Paris. Spotted by Charles de Gaulle at the Liberation, he runs the Rothschild bank from 1954 to 1958 before returning to the general's side as his chief of staff. From 1962 to 1968, he occupies Matignon, the prime minister's office, longer than any other prime minister of the Fifth Republic, weathers the crisis of May 1968, then is elected to the Élysée in June 1969 — though not before seeing, the previous year, his wife Claude targeted by the accusations of the Markovic affair, a rumor campaign built around photographs found in the car of a murdered bodyguard, which historians today regard as a deliberate attempt to destabilize him before the election. The investigation will be dropped in 1973 for lack of evidence.

According to the hematologist Jean Bernard, who is treating him, Pompidou is in fact suffering, as early as 1968, from Waldenström's macroglobulinemia — a rare cancer that causes an abnormal proliferation of antibody-producing cells in the bone marrow. He would therefore have entered the Élysée already knowing of it, or at least suspecting it. Of this, he will never say anything publicly: no press conference, no statement, no admission. In the history of the Fifth Republic, no other president will have concealed, for so long or so completely, an illness that doomed him.

1973 is the year the body begins to betray the secret. The corticosteroids prescribed to slow the disease swell his face, make him put on weight and weigh down his gait — the images of the Reykjavik summit remain the best-known record of it. Pompidou nonetheless continues to carry out his duties in full: he confronts the oil shock that follows the Yom Kippur War in the fall of 1973 and pursues an active diplomacy, while delegating more and more of the management of domestic affairs to his secretary general, Édouard Balladur. "I trust you, I am counting on you to see to everything and to bring me only the most important problems," he is said to have told him that year, according to the account Balladur will give later. He cuts back on his travel and concentrates his strength on foreign policy.

From the winter of 1973-1974, the deterioration becomes impossible to hide completely. Pompidou limits his public appearances still further and withdraws to his apartment at 24 quai de Béthune, on the Île Saint-Louis, where round-the-clock medical care is now organized around him. He continues to work and to receive visitors, but power, in practice, tightens around a small circle of close associates who manage his daily life.

Georges Pompidou dies on April 2, 1974, at 9 p.m., in his apartment on the Île Saint-Louis. The officially recorded cause is septicemia, a consequence of the weakening of his immune system brought on by the prednisone treatment. He was 62 and governed France until his final days. Only on April 8 — six days after his death — does his physician, Dr. Jean Vignalou, issue the very first statement on his health, referring in very measured terms to a "benign lesion of vascular origin, located in the anorectal region, and intermittently extremely painful." The French then discover, after the fact, what their president had chosen to keep from them for five years.

This total concealment remains a case apart in French political history. A few years later, François Mitterrand will in turn keep silent, for most of his two terms, about the cancer diagnosed as early as 1981 — a secret revealed in 1996 by the book of his physician, Claude Gubler, Le Grand Secret. The two precedents, Pompidou and then Mitterrand, will fuel a lasting debate in France that is still not settled: that of citizens' right to know the state of health of the person who governs them, set against the determination of a sick man to carry out his duties to the end, in silence.
