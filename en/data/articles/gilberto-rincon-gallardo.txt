In 1968, as the Mexican army opens fire on students at Tlatelolco and the country holds its breath ahead of the Mexico City Olympics, a young activist of twenty-nine is arrested and thrown in prison. It will not be the last time. Over the course of his life, Gilberto Rincón Gallardo will be jailed thirty-two times for his political commitments, a figure those close to him will later cite as the exact measure of his stubbornness.

He was born on May 15, 1939, in Mexico City, with shortened arms as a result of a congenital malformation. In the Mexico of the 1940s and 1950s, few doors open easily to a child who is different. Rincón Gallardo decides very early not to wait for them to be opened for him: at nineteen, in 1958, he joins the presidential campaign of Luis H. Álvarez, a figure of the National Action Party, a right-wing formation. Nothing, at that point, hints at the trajectory to follow.

For Rincón Gallardo's itinerary is of a kind rarely attributed to politicians: he moves, decade after decade, from conservatism toward the radical left. A law student at the National Autonomous University of Mexico, he campaigns alongside figures such as Heberto Castillo, Valentín Campa and Demetrio Vallejo, takes part in the National Liberation Movement led by former president Lázaro Cárdenas, then joins the Mexican Communist Party, which he represents as a federal deputy between 1977 and 1980. In the late 1980s, he is among the founders of the Party of the Democratic Revolution alongside Cuauhtémoc Cárdenas and Heberto Castillo, before leaving it in turn, in the 1990s, to found his own formation, the Social Democracy Party.

It is under that banner that he runs in the 2000 presidential election, against Vicente Fox and the candidates of the major historic parties. Rincón Gallardo has no chance of winning, and he knows it. But a candidate with shortened arms who shakes hands, holds rallies and defends a platform in front of the national cameras changes, without any theoretical speech, what Mexicans associate with a political leader. His candidacy will remain a symbolic milestone long after the modest score it earns at the ballot box.

Three years later, on July 11, 2003, President Vicente Fox appoints him the first president of the National Council to Prevent Discrimination, CONAPRED, the institution Mexico has just created to fight discrimination. Felipe Calderón confirms him in the post in 2006. Rincón Gallardo remains there until his death, making CONAPRED an institution recognized well beyond Mexico's borders. He is credited today with fathering Mexico's accession to the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities, a text he champions with the conviction of someone who personally knows what is at stake, without ever making it the sole axis of his public discourse.

That conviction costs him dearly during Mexico's years of lead. The country is then going through what history will remember as the Dirty War: repression of student and left-wing movements, arbitrary imprisonment, disappearances. Rincón Gallardo is among the very first young activists to be jailed in 1968. His wife, Silvia Pavón, will sum it up after his death in the pages of the daily La Jornada: "tuvo que sufrir persecución y cárcel cuando el país era rabiosamente autoritario" (he had to suffer persecution and prison when the country was rabidly authoritarian).

Gilberto Rincón Gallardo dies on August 30, 2008, in Mexico City, at sixty-nine. According to the Mexican press, his death follows a complication after surgery, against a background of cardiac frailty; the precise circumstances are reported with restraint at the time. He leaves behind a CONAPRED that is still active, an international convention that Mexico eventually ratified, and a political trajectory that nothing, at his beginnings in 1958, made it possible to predict.

Of this man, what people often remember first are the shortened arms, the prison, the parties founded and left one after another. What is sometimes forgotten is the essential: Rincón Gallardo never presented his disability as an obstacle to overcome in order to earn his place in politics. He made it, on the contrary, a starting point for questioning an entire country about the way it treats those it considers different — people with disabilities, but also the religious, ethnic and sexual minorities that CONAPRED protects today. An entire life spent refusing to let others decide, in his place, what he could or could not accomplish.
