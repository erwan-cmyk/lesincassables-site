On October 5, 1973, Golda Meir receives alarming intelligence: Egyptian and Syrian troops are massing on Israel's borders. The next morning, the day of Yom Kippur, six hours before hostilities break out, she gathers her defense minister, Moshe Dayan, and her chief of staff, David Elazar, in Tel Aviv. Dayan hesitates to order a general mobilization, fearing that Israel would appear as the aggressor in the eyes of the world; Elazar demands one. Meir decides: she orders the full mobilization of the army, but refuses the preemptive strike her generals propose, for fear of losing the international support, American in particular, that the Jewish state will need in the days ahead. A few hours later, the Yom Kippur War breaks out. Golda Meir is 75 and has led Israel for four years.

Born Golda Mabovitch in Kyiv in 1898, she grows up in Milwaukee, in the United States, where her family has fled the pogroms, before emigrating to Mandatory Palestine in 1921 to join a kibbutz. She patiently climbs the ranks of the Zionist movement and then of the nascent state apparatus: a signatory of Israel's declaration of independence in 1948, Israel's first ambassador to Moscow, minister of labor, then foreign minister from 1956 to 1966. She is a woman known for her plain speaking and her stamina for work, in a political world then almost exclusively male.

It is in that last post, in late 1965, that she is diagnosed with lymphoma. She is 67. Golda Meir decides, together with a small circle of close associates, to tell no one else. Two fainting spells nearly give her away: she loses consciousness during a meeting of her party in Israel, then again during a bus journey in southern Africa. In January 1966, citing exhaustion and health problems, she leaves the foreign ministry. The general public will never learn, during her political lifetime, the real reason for this withdrawal; according to later reconstructions, she fears above all that such an announcement would add to the anxiety of a country still young and surrounded by threats.

Three years later, on the death of Prime Minister Levi Eshkol, the Labor Party turns to her. On March 17, 1969, Golda Meir becomes prime minister of Israel — to this day, she remains the only woman to have held the office. For five years, she governs the country while continuing to visit Hadassah Hospital in Jerusalem for consultations held late in the evening, sometimes after midnight, away from journalists and, more often than not, from her own staff. From diagnosis to death, she will carry the secret of her illness alone for thirteen years; it will only be fully pieced together later, by historians and by the medical staff who treated her.

It is under this invisible weight that she faces the two hardest ordeals of her tenure. In September 1972, after the murder of eleven Israeli athletes and coaches at the Munich Olympics, she appeals to the world to save the hostages and condemns acts she deems unspeakable; judging the international response derisory, she gives the green light to a covert Mossad campaign targeting those responsible for the attack.

A year later comes the Yom Kippur War. Her decision to order general mobilization, taken in the urgency of that October morning, will later be judged by the commission of inquiry charged with examining the conduct of the conflict as a decision made with wisdom, common sense and speed. The country is nonetheless left reeling from its lack of preparation for the surprise attack.

A commission chaired by Justice Shimon Agranat is tasked with establishing responsibility. Its interim report, published in April 1974, clears Golda Meir and Moshe Dayan of direct responsibility, laying it mainly at the door of the heads of military intelligence. But public opinion, wounded by the loss of life and by the feeling of having been caught off guard, is not satisfied. Though exonerated by the commission, Meir chooses to go. On April 11, 1974, she announces her resignation. "It is beyond my strength to continue carrying this burden," she declares. She officially leaves office on June 3, 1974, handing over to Yitzhak Rabin.

Golda Meir dies on December 8, 1978, at Hadassah Hospital in Jerusalem — the very hospital where, for thirteen years, she had gone in secret to treat the disease that finally took her. She was 80. During the five years in which she held in her hands the decisions of war and peace for a country surrounded on every side, she also carried, alone, those concerning her own survival.
