One day in October 2016, in the corridors of the European Parliament, the European Conservatives and Reformists group announces the name of its candidate for the presidency of the institution. Helga Stevens steps forward, accompanied as always by a sign language interpreter. For the first time in the history of the European Parliament, a candidate for its highest office cannot hear a single word spoken in the chamber where she has sat for more than two years. She will not win the post, but the gesture has already changed something: a deaf woman has just put herself forward for one of the most visible institutional positions in the European Union.

Nothing in Helga Stevens's childhood hinted at this kind of trajectory. Born in 1968 in Flanders, she is deaf from birth; no precise cause has ever been established. "I was born deaf," she will tell the Dutch website Doof.nl years later. "I can hear something with a hearing aid, but not enough to understand what is being said." Her parents first enroll her at the royal institute for the deaf and hard of hearing in Hasselt, where she learns Flemish Sign Language. Around the age of ten, they make a decision that will change everything: they transfer her to a mainstream school, in Sint-Truiden. Overnight, she goes from an environment where sign language is the norm to a classroom where she is the only pupil who cannot hear the teacher's voice. Yet there she develops a spoken delivery considered exceptional for a deaf person — less a gift than years of repeated work.

She finishes secondary school and then enrolls in law at the Katholieke Universiteit Leuven. She graduates — and becomes Belgium's first deaf lawyer. Before that, one experience had already drawn a line through her life: in 1987, a Rotary scholarship sends her to spend a year in St. Louis, Missouri. There she discovers American Deaf culture and visits Gallaudet University, the only institution in the world designed entirely for deaf and hard-of-hearing students. She comes back with a conviction that will never leave her: a right is not obtained by waiting for it, it is claimed. In 1993, a Fulbright scholarship takes her to Berkeley, where she earns a master's degree in law at Boalt Hall School of Law.

Back in Belgium, she puts that legal training at the service of a broader cause. From 1996, she gets involved with the European Union of the Deaf and the Flemish federation of deaf organizations, where she campaigns in particular for the official recognition of sign language and of the profession of sign language interpreter. This grassroots work leads her into politics, first at the local level, as a municipal councilor, before the elections come one after another. In 2004, she is elected to the Flemish Parliament under the colors of the Nieuw-Vlaamse Alliantie; she is reelected in 2009. In 2007, she enters the Belgian Senate, where she is returned in 2010. Election after election, the same observation holds: the Flemish electorate does not vote for a deaf candidate, it votes for a candidate.

In May 2014, she is elected to the European Parliament. Six months later, in November, her colleagues choose her as vice-chair of the European Conservatives and Reformists group. She also takes over the chair of the parliament's Disability Intergroup, a platform she uses to carry accessibility and inclusion issues to the European level. It is from that legitimacy, accumulated term after term since the municipal level, that the October 2016 candidacy is born: a symbol, certainly, but a symbol built on twenty years of concrete political work rather than on a publicity stunt.

The candidacy is also part of a broader movement. In 2003, Iceland's Sigurlín Margrét becomes Europe's first deaf member of parliament; in the decade that follows, it is largely deaf women who carry this movement for political representation across the continent. Helga Stevens embodies its most visible culmination: she who has climbed, one by one, every rung of Belgian and European power, from the municipal council to the chamber in Strasbourg, without ever skipping a step or being offered a shortcut.

The rest of her European term follows the same furrow: social policy, migration, security, and always that fight for the recognition of deaf and hard-of-hearing people, which she carries by organizing international conferences and by representing, in the hushed corridors of Brussels and Strasbourg, a community that still struggles to make itself heard there — in the literal sense of the word.

Asked one day what drove her to lead such an exposed public life, she summed up her conviction in a sentence that has become a kind of signature: "People can do much more than they think. They just need to be given the chance." That is exactly what she will have set out to prove, case after case, election after election, for more than twenty years now.
