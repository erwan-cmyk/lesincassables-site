In Cardiff, Wales, a few days before the opening of the London 2012 Paralympic Games, a handful of athletes from the Pacific train on facilities made available by the Australian Paralympic Committee. Among them, a woman from the Solomon Islands throws a shot from a chair not designed for her: she owns no throwing frame suited to her discipline. The Australian officials notice this a week before the competition and have a frame built to her measurements, as a matter of urgency. Delivered on a Wednesday, the equipment is tried out for the first time that same day, in the late afternoon. A few days later, Hellen Saohaga settles into it for good, at London's Olympic Stadium, and becomes the first athlete in her country's history to take part in the Paralympic Games.

Hellen Saohaga is born on December 12, 1987, in the Solomon Islands, with a severe congenital malformation of the legs, present from birth, that requires her to get around in a wheelchair. In this South Pacific archipelago of hundreds of islands, the question of adapted sport almost never comes up at the time: there is then no structure, no equipment and no tradition of competition for people with disabilities. The national Paralympic committee obtains international recognition only in 2011 — barely a year before Hellen Saohaga prepares to represent her country on the biggest stage in world para sport.

It all begins in March 2012, at a competition held in Brisbane, Australia, where Hellen Saohaga tries the shot put for the first time in the F57 class, reserved for seated throwers with a lower-limb impairment. It is there that she is spotted, before obtaining a universality place, the mechanism that allows nations without a Paralympic tradition to send a representative to the Games all the same. In a few months, she goes from a first attempt at the shot put to the role of flag bearer for her delegation.

Ahead of the Games, the Australian team brings her into a two-week training camp in Cardiff, alongside five other Oceanian delegations. There she discovers the technical demands of a discipline she has practiced for barely a few months. "I'm very grateful for the opportunity to be in Cardiff before the Games. I'm happy because I have learnt so much about the technical requirements of what I do," she tells the local press at the time. She adds: "Had I not had this opportunity I would have looked very bad in the competition. I've gained confidence over these two weeks, observing the Australian team, watching what they do."

The chair delivered at the last minute makes a concrete difference to her throwing. "The wheelchair's really helped. I can move faster and throw further distances now because I'm not so tired," she explains. Until then, she had trained without dedicated equipment, in a country that neither manufactures nor imports it.

On September 8, 2012, Hellen Saohaga takes part in the women's shot put final. She finishes fifteenth of seventeen competitors — a modest placing, nowhere near the world's best specialists in the discipline, and it is not that number that gives the day its meaning. For the Solomon Islands, she is the first to wear the national Paralympic jersey and the first to march, flag in hand, at the opening ceremony. That day, an athlete now exists where, a year earlier, no structure made it possible even to consider taking part.

Hellen Saohaga does not stop at this single appearance. Three years later, at the 2015 Pacific Games, she switches disciplines and enters the seated table tennis, in the women's singles. Beaten by Fiji's Roden Merewalesi, then by Papua New Guinea's Vero Paul Nime at the end of a tight match decided three sets to two, she nonetheless leaves with a bronze medal.

In 2023, when the Solomon Islands host the Pacific Games on their own soil for the first time, in Honiara, Hellen Saohaga is named one of the nine ambassadors of the event, alongside established athletes such as weightlifter Jenly Tegu Wini. Her mission: to go out and meet the country's communities to present the Games and inspire young Solomon Islanders to take up sport. Eleven years after having to train in a chair that was not her own, for lack of equipment available in her country, she has become the one who opens the way for those who come after her.

Hellen Saohaga's story is not told in records or gold medals. It is told in a date, 2012, and a number: one — the woman who, first, put her country on the list of Paralympic nations. Firsts of this kind are never repeated. She remains, to this day, the first.
