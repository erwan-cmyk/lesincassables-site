Sydney, October 2000. On the track at the Paralympic Games, in the home straight of the 5,000 meters in the T11 class, reserved for blind or nearly blind athletes, a Kenyan runner raises his arms as he crosses the finish line first. Henry Wanyoike sees neither the line, nor the scoreboard confirming his time, nor his country's flag about to be raised up the pole. He has run every stride tethered by a foot-long rope, tied to his right hand and to the left hand of his friend Joseph Kibunja. That day, Wanyoike becomes Paralympic champion. Five years earlier, he did not know whether he would ever run again.

In the spring of 1995, Wanyoike is twenty years old. Two years after leaving high school, where he had stood out as a promising distance runner over 5,000 and 10,000 meters, he leads the ordinary life of a young Kenyan athlete. He would later recount going to bed, on the night of May 1, like any healthy young man, and waking up the next day plunged into darkness. A stroke cost him 95 percent of his vision that night; the rest would fade away gradually over the following years. "I was thinking that was the end of me. My dream would never come true," he would confide years later to TIME magazine.

The months that follow take him away from the track. Wanyoike goes to the clinic in Kikuyu, near Nairobi, where newly blind patients are first taught to knit, to regain a measure of independence in everyday tasks. It is a long way from athletics, and nothing then suggests he will return to it. But the idea of running never leaves him: as a child, he ran barefoot, with Joseph Kibunja, the roughly five kilometers separating their village from the school. Three years after the stroke, in 1998, it is this same Kibunja who agrees to become his guide. Since then, the two men have run tethered by a blue and green rope, the right hand of one in the left hand of the other, Kibunja signaling turns and obstacles by voice and by slight movements of the arm.

The duo progresses quickly. After Sydney gold in 2000 over 5,000 meters (15:46.29), Wanyoike sets world records in his class over 5,000 and 10,000 meters at the 2002 World Championships, then wins 1,500-meter gold at the 2003 All-Africa Games. The following year, at the Athens Games, he claims two more gold medals, over 5,000 meters (15:11.07) and 10,000 meters (31:37.25). He then turns to the marathon, where the trust between the two men is tested over an entirely different duration. In May 2004, in Boston, he runs 2:33:20. A year later, he lowers his own world record in London, to 2:32:51, before shattering it a week later in Hamburg, in 2:31:31 — two world records in seven days, which would earn him a nomination for the Laureus World Sportsperson of the Year with a Disability award, in 2005 and again in 2006. A pace that many well-trained able-bodied runners would not turn down.

Wanyoike does not stop at sport. After his Athens victories, he creates the Henry Wanyoike Foundation, with the ambition of changing the way disability is seen in Kenya. The foundation has since distributed around 400 white canes, 200 wheelchairs and several dozen hearing aids, supported more than 12,000 children and founded a school, the House of Hope, which today takes in some eighty pupils. Every year, the Hope for the Future charity run draws thousands of participants; around 11,000 were at the start in 2018. Wanyoike has visited more than 700 schools across the country, and his story now features in Kenyan primary school textbooks.

Years later, looking back on the road traveled since that night in May 1995, he would tell TIME magazine: "For 17 years, since I lost my sight, I think I have done so many (more) things than what I did for 21 years before. The most important thing is to accept yourself."

Joseph Kibunja will have been there for all of these races, from Sydney to Hamburg, a rope tied between their two hands. More than three decades after that night in May 1995 when everything changed, it is this rope, and the friendship that holds it, that will have allowed Henry Wanyoike to turn darkness into finish lines, crossed one after another, faster than almost everyone.
