One day, on a walk in the woods near the family's vacation house, a bird sings in the canopy. The child walking beside his parents stops short, tilts his head, then repeats aloud — with the very intonation of the voice he has been hearing for months on records of birdsong — the name of the species that has just made itself heard. He is six years old, and it is the first complete sentence he has spoken in his life. His parents, who no longer dared to wonder what they could hope for from him, have just discovered that their son hears and retains everything he listens to.

That child's name is Hikari Ōe. He will become one of Japan's best-selling composers of chamber music. Born on June 13, 1963, he is the eldest son of the writer Kenzaburō Ōe and of Yukari Ōe, sister of the filmmaker Jūzō Itami. At his birth, doctors diagnose an encephalocele, a congenital malformation in which part of the brain protrudes through an opening in the skull, then commonly referred to as a brain hernia. He undergoes emergency surgery, a long and risky operation.

According to several biographies devoted to the Ōe family, the doctors then give the parents a very grim prognosis and go so far as to suggest forgoing the operation and letting the child die. Kenzaburō and his wife refuse and choose surgery. The months that follow are ones of deep distress for the writer, who goes through this period in an existential doubt he would later speak about without evasion.

The month after his son's birth, Kenzaburō Ōe leaves to cover a peace rally in Hiroshima. There he meets hibakusha, survivors of the atomic bombings, and the doctor who cares for them at the Red Cross hospital gives him a piece of advice he will hold onto for a long time: never despair, but never hope too much either. The stay acts as a turning point in his relationship to fatherhood. He would later say that he had been trained as a writer and as a human being by the birth of his son.

The early years remain difficult. Hikari barely speaks and seems largely unresponsive to those around him; by the criteria of the time, he is diagnosed with an autistic disorder, an intellectual disability, epilepsy and a severe visual impairment. To soothe him, his parents play recordings of birdsong beside his crib, then records of Mozart and Chopin. It is from this patient listening that the episode of the walk in the woods will emerge, years later.

Encouraged by this breakthrough, his parents enroll him in piano lessons with a teacher, Kumiko Tamura. Hikari develops an ear that those around him consider exceptional: he retains and reproduces from memory any music heard a single time, and can identify a work by Mozart after barely a few bars, linking it to its corresponding Köchel catalog number. He composes without ever improvising at the keyboard first: the pieces take shape in his head before being set down on paper — a way of working that his father's translator and biographer John Nathan would describe as a unique case in the medical literature on savant syndrome.

As an adult, Hikari devotes himself to composing chamber music. His first record, released in the early 1990s, meets with a success unexpected for a contemporary classical work. His father would later say that the disc sold "better than any of my novels," adding, "and I'm proud of that," and put the figure at 400,000 copies sold; other sources speak, across his entire discography and over time, of more than a million records sold. Further albums follow throughout the 1990s and 2000s, and part of his catalog — a dozen works, according to some sources — is now published in sheet music in several countries and several languages.

Hikari's life has deeply nourished his father's work. As early as 1964, Kenzaburō Ōe draws from it the novel A Personal Matter, which portrays a father confronted with the birth of a brain-damaged son, and then, in 1996, the memoir A Healing Family. In 1994, the Swedish Academy awards Kenzaburō Ōe the Nobel Prize in Literature, honoring in particular a body of work in which his son's life holds a central place.

Hikari long continued to live with his parents, composing in the very room where his father wrote. Kenzaburō Ōe died in March 2023, at the age of 88. In Japanese, the name Hikari means light — the very word the biographer Lindsley Cameron took up for the title of the book she devoted to this story, The Music of Light. Where words had long been lacking, music, for Hikari Ōe, will have remained the language he chose for himself.
