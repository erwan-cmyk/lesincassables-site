On January 24, 1999, in his palace in Amman, King Hussein of Jordan signs a decree that overturns, in a few lines, a dynastic balance thirty-four years old. He strips his younger brother, Prince Hassan, of the title of crown prince he had held since 1965, to pass it to his eldest son, Abdullah, then thirty-six and until then destined for a military career. The man making this decision has just returned from six months of treatment in the United States for a cancer of the lymphatic system. He has fourteen days left to live.

The news stuns Jordan. Hassan had held the position of crown prince for more than three decades, serving several times as regent of the kingdom in his brother's absence. In a public letter, Hussein reproaches Hassan for the way he handled certain affairs of the kingdom during that regency, and chooses to return to a direct father-to-son succession. Hassan bows to the decision without publicly contesting the king's choice.

Hussein bin Talal is only sixteen when he ascends the throne, on August 11, 1952, after the forced abdication of his father Talal, who suffered from schizophrenia and was removed from power after a little over a year of reign. Officially crowned the following year, on coming of age, the young king inherits a recently created kingdom, wedged between the major regional powers and directly exposed to the upheavals of the Arab-Israeli conflict. He reigns for nearly forty-seven years, one of the longest reigns in the history of the contemporary Middle East.

This half-century in power passes through crisis after crisis. The Six-Day War, in June 1967, costs Jordan control of the West Bank and East Jerusalem. Three years later, the growing presence on Jordanian soil of Palestinian armed groups, who come to openly challenge royal authority, culminates in a wave of hijackings of civilian airliners by the Popular Front for the Liberation of Palestine, in September 1970. Hussein then orders his army to intervene: the fighting that follows, known as Black September, pits the Jordanian army against the Palestinian fedayeen for several days in Amman and in the north of the country, before continuing intermittently until the summer of 1971. The human toll, largely civilian, remains disputed to this day: the Jordanian authorities of the time and several historians put forward figures on the order of two to three thousand dead, while sources close to the Palestine Liberation Organization have spoken of several tens of thousands of victims.

The Jordanian sovereign then distinguishes himself as one of the few Arab heads of state to bet lastingly on the diplomatic path. In 1988, he renounces Jordanian claims to the West Bank in favor of the Palestine Liberation Organization. Six years later, in 1994, he signs a peace treaty with Israel that makes Jordan the second Arab country, after Egypt, to officially recognize the Jewish state. A pilot and amateur radio enthusiast, he cultivates throughout his reign the image of an accessible monarch, in contrast with the recurring violence that marked his political career — a man who, over the decades, escaped several attempts to destabilize his rule.

It is in July 1998 that doctors at the Mayo Clinic, in Minnesota, diagnose him with lymphoma, a cancer of the lymphatic system. The type of tumor, he is told, in principle responds well to chemotherapy, and the physicians say at the time that they are optimistic about his chances of recovery. Hussein begins his treatment there and remains in the United States for six months, before returning to Jordan in January 1999.

It is in this short interval, between his return home and the relapse that strikes him down six days later, that he finds the strength to sign the decree stripping Hassan of his title of crown prince. The disease regains the upper hand almost at once: rushed back to the Mayo Clinic, Hussein undergoes a second bone marrow transplant there, which fails. Doctors report multiple organ failure. In early February, in a coma, he is repatriated one last time to Jordan and flown by helicopter to the medical center that bears his name, in Amman. King Hussein dies on February 7, 1999, at the age of 63, surrounded by four of his five sons and Queen Noor.

His funeral, held the next day in the streets of Amman, draws nearly 800,000 Jordanians as well as an unusual number of heads of state and government from around the world. Abdullah, crown prince for barely two weeks, immediately succeeds him on the Hashemite throne, as Abdullah II.
