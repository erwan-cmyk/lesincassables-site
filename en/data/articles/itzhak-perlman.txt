On September 29, 2025, the Beacon Theatre in Manhattan is packed well before the first note. Onstage, surrounded by the klezmer musicians he has played with for three decades, Itzhak Perlman is celebrating his 80th birthday with "In the Fiddler's House," the program that caused a sensation when it was created. The violinist jokes with the audience, laughingly recounts his unrequited love for the New York Mets, then raises his bow. The gesture has not changed since childhood: seated, focused, transfigured from the first bar.

Itzhak Perlman is born on August 31, 1945, in Tel Aviv, then under British mandate, three years before the creation of the State of Israel. His parents, Chaim and Shoshana, left Poland in the 1930s and met there. The boy is not yet three when a violin heard on the radio fascinates him: he asks for one, is at first refused admission to his neighborhood conservatory — judged too small to hold the instrument — and teaches himself a few tunes before anyone takes his talent seriously.

It is at four that polio deprives him of the use of his legs. The boy learns to get around again with leg braces and crutches. Nothing around him holds back his desire to play: his parents encourage him to keep up his music, and he discovers that a violin can be played just as well sitting down. It is a self-evident fact he would sum up decades later, in Newsweek magazine, in a line that has become his signature: "Separate your ability from your disability. That is my motto."

From the age of five, he enters the Academy of Music in Tel Aviv, where the teacher Rivka Goldgart oversees his training; he gives his first public recital at ten. In 1958, a team from the Ed Sullivan Show, in Israel to find young talent, spots him. Carried by a scholarship from the America-Israel Cultural Foundation, the teenager moves to New York and enters the Juilliard School, where he studies with Ivan Galamian and then with Galamian's assistant, Dorothy DeLay — a teacher who questioned him rather than dictating his gestures. Perlman would tell the Academy of Achievement how she corrected him: "Sugarplum, what is your concept of G sharp?"

His appearance on the Ed Sullivan Show, that same year, 1958, makes him known to millions of American viewers: he is only 13. Five years later, in 1963, he makes his Carnegie Hall debut. In 1964, he wins the Leventritt Competition, the absolute benchmark for young American violinists: that victory opens the doors of the great orchestras to him and launches, before he is even twenty, his international career.

In that same decade, he marries Toby Friedlander, a violinist he met during his student years; they wed in 1967 and remain, to this day, inseparable. Perlman soon performs with all the great orchestras of the world, from New York to Berlin. His recordings, from 1977 onward, bring him a run of awards; in 1993, John Williams chooses him to play the violin themes of Steven Spielberg's Schindler's List — one of his most notable film collaborations. At the turn of the 2000s, he adds conducting to his activities, becoming principal guest conductor of the Detroit Symphony Orchestra and then music adviser to the Saint Louis Symphony.

With Toby, he founds in the 1990s the Perlman Music Program, for promising young string players, at first in the summer and then year-round. In 2003, he succeeds his own teacher, Dorothy DeLay, in the violin chair at the Juilliard School. Of his students he asks more than technique: "I want you to be a magician. Not a musician, a magician," he tells them again and again.

The honors pile up over the decades: fifteen Grammy Awards between 1977 and 1995, joined in 2008 by a Grammy for lifetime achievement; the Medal of Liberty presented by Ronald Reagan in 1986; the National Medal of Arts in 2000; the Kennedy Center Honors in 2003; the Presidential Medal of Freedom, presented by Barack Obama in 2015; the Genesis Prize in 2016, whose million dollars he turned over to initiatives for the inclusion of people with disabilities. In January 2009, at Obama's inauguration, he plays John Williams's "Air and Simple Gifts" with Yo-Yo Ma, Gabriela Montero and Anthony McGill: with the extreme cold threatening to damage the instruments, the quartet mimes its performance over a recording made the day before, in front of millions of viewers, without anyone noticing at the time.

Now in his eighties, Itzhak Perlman continues to tour, with dates announced through 2027. He has long campaigned for the accessibility of public places to people with disabilities, a fight complicated, he acknowledges, by travel, hotels and concert halls that are still too often poorly adapted. Nearly eight decades after hearing, as a child, a first violin on the radio, he goes on playing seated, exactly as he always has.
