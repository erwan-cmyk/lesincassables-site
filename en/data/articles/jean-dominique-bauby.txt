On April 2, 1974, France learns of the death of President Georges Pompidou. That day, in the pages of Le Quotidien de Paris, a 21-year-old reporter signs his very first article. His name is Jean-Dominique Bauby. Nothing in that discreet byline at the bottom of a page hints at what will follow: two decades of Paris journalism, the editorship of a major women's magazine, then an entire book dictated letter by letter, in circumstances no one, then, could have imagined.

Bauby was born on April 23, 1952, in the 14th arrondissement of Paris. He grows up on rue du Mont-Thabor, near the Tuileries, in a building that once housed the writer Alfred de Musset. After his beginnings at Combat and then Le Quotidien de Paris, he passes through Paris Match, where he handles the culture pages, before taking over Le Matin de Paris at 28. In 1994, he even publishes a book, a biography of the film producer Raoul Lévy. His career then takes him to the head of the newsroom at Elle magazine, alongside Marie-Françoise Colombani: a high-profile post, in a Paris media world where he is said to be quick, elegant, fond of a good line.

On December 8, 1995, at 43, everything changes. Bauby suffers a massive stroke, which affects the brainstem. He sinks into a coma and emerges from it some twenty days later: conscious, perfectly lucid, but entirely paralyzed. He can no longer speak, swallow, or move a limb. Only his left eyelid still answers to his will. Doctors have a name for this imprisonment of the body: locked-in syndrome.

A language has to be reinvented. A speech therapist devises for him an alphabet in which the letters are no longer arranged in the usual order but according to how often they are used in French: E, S, A, R, I, N, T, U, L… The letters are recited aloud to him; he blinks his left eye when he hears the right one. Letter after letter, word after word, this patient gymnastics becomes his only voice, and soon the instrument with which he will write a book.

It is the publisher Robert Laffont who proposes that he tell the story of his new existence. Every night, before dawn, Bauby composes his sentences in his head, memorizes them, corrects them. Every day, he dictates them to Claude Mendibil, a colleague from the Laffont house who runs through the alphabet until the blink that confirms the right letter; a few chapters are taken down by Michel Abécassis, his deputy at Elle. The work lasts about two months, several hours a day, for a short, dense text in which humor, against all expectation, keeps surfacing: "Wheelchairs are as commonplace at Berck as Ferraris at Monte Carlo," he writes of the seaside hospital where he is being treated.

Le Scaphandre et le Papillon — The Diving Bell and the Butterfly — is published on March 7, 1997, by Robert Laffont. The success is immediate: more than 25,000 copies sell on the first day, more than 150,000 in a week. The press discovers a text without pathos or complaint, in which the journalist describes, from his hospital bed, what the illness has not taken from him: his memory, his humor, his eye on the world.

Jean-Dominique Bauby would know only the very first days of that success. On March 9, 1997, two days after his book came out, he dies of pneumonia at the Raymond-Poincaré hospital in Garches. He was 44. He is buried in Père-Lachaise cemetery, in the same section as his grandfather, a military doctor.

The book, for its part, goes on without him. It becomes an enormous bestseller across Europe and will be translated in some thirty countries, giving the general public a word for a reality that had until then remained in medical shadow: locked-in syndrome. Ten years later, in 2007, the director Julian Schnabel makes a film of it, with Mathieu Amalric as Bauby, alongside Emmanuelle Seigner and Max von Sydow. Shown at the Cannes Film Festival, the film wins Schnabel the directing prize; it goes on to take the Golden Globe for best foreign-language film and six Césars, including best film and best director.

What remains of this story is the image of a man who, reduced to total immobility, refused to give up his last space of freedom: his thought. Toward the end of the book, Bauby asks himself: "Does the cosmos contain keys for opening up my diving bell?" He would never know the answer. But by blinking his left eye, tens of thousands of times, he left his own all the same: a book people go on reading, nearly thirty years after his death, as a testimony to dignity and the power of the mind.
