On Easter Sunday 2005, in St. Peter's Square, tens of thousands of the faithful look up toward the central loggia of the basilica, as they do every year for the Easter blessing. The pope appears at the window. He opens his mouth to speak. No sound comes out. John Paul II is 84; his last public words date back two weeks. Cardinal Angelo Sodano has to read in his place the message the pontiff can no longer deliver himself. Millions of television viewers around the world watch, live, a man who refuses to leave the stage even as his body no longer obeys him.

For nearly a decade, the tremors in his left hand, the rigidity of his face and his increasingly hesitant gait have been visible at every one of his public appearances, broadcast across the planet. Yet the man who leads both a state and the Catholic Church has never seriously considered giving up his office. Understanding this obstinacy means going back over the path of a man shaped, long before illness, by occupation and dictatorship.

Karol Józef Wojtyła is born on May 18, 1920, in Wadowice, a small town in southern Poland. He is nineteen when Nazi Germany invades his country. To escape deportation to the Reich, he works in a limestone quarry and then in a chemical plant, while secretly pursuing theology studies in a clandestine seminary in Kraków. Ordained a priest in 1946, he then rises through the ranks of the Polish Church under a communist regime that keeps the clergy under close surveillance, becoming bishop and then archbishop of Kraków, before being made a cardinal.

On October 16, 1978, at 58, he is elected pope and takes the name John Paul II. He becomes the first non-Italian pontiff since 1523 and the first Pole to occupy the throne of St. Peter. In the middle of the Cold War, the election of a pope from a country of the communist bloc carries symbolic weight. The following year, his triumphant return to Poland draws enormous crowds and helps galvanize opposition to the regime; the Solidarność (Solidarity) trade union is born a few months later. His role in the collapse of communism in Eastern Europe, alongside other actors of that period, is today widely recognized by historians.

It is during the 1990s that the first signs of Parkinson's disease become perceptible: a trembling left hand, an increasingly frozen face, a shuffling gait. The Vatican remains evasive for a long time. In October 1996, his spokesman Joaquín Navarro-Valls speaks of a vague "extrapyramidal syndrome," without ever uttering the word Parkinson. It is not until the very beginning of January 2001 that a diagnosis is finally confirmed publicly: in an interview with the Italian magazine Oggi, the orthopedic surgeon Gianfranco Fineschi, who had operated on the pope in 1994, refers to the Parkinson's disease from which the pontiff suffers.

For more than a decade, as the tremors worsen and speech becomes increasingly difficult, John Paul II continues to travel, to preside over the great ceremonies, to receive heads of state. He gives up none of his duties, even as the possibility of a resignation begins to be raised in the international press. The pope himself had developed, in his writings, a reflection on the meaning of human suffering; many saw in his remaining in office to the very end a way of putting that conviction into practice — a deliberate witness of dignity in the face of illness and old age rather than mere stubbornness.

His pontificate will have lasted nearly twenty-seven years, one of the longest in the history of the Catholic Church. That record is also darkened by his criticized handling of sexual abuse committed by members of the clergy under his authority. The most documented case is that of the Mexican priest Marcial Maciel Degollado, the subject of a Vatican investigation as early as 1956: the accusations that resurfaced against him in the 1980s went nowhere within the hierarchy, which even approved internal statutes forbidding any criticism of him, before he was found, much later, to be guilty of abusing seminarians and of having fathered children in violation of his vows. The practice of moving accused priests rather than reporting them to the civil authorities is documented as relatively widespread during this period, and the reforms imposing background checks and mandatory reporting came only late in the pontificate.

Three days before his death, on March 30, 2005, John Paul II appears one last time at the window of his apartment. Silent, he can no longer say a word; he blesses the crowd gathered in St. Peter's Square with a sign of the cross. He dies at the Vatican on the evening of April 2, 2005, having exercised his office until his final days, without ever having publicly relinquished his role as head of the Church and head of state.
