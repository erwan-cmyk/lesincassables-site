In her grandparents' pool, somewhere in Maryland, a ten-year-old girl dives in every Sunday after Mass. She takes to it, soon joins a club, then the national team. Two years later, that same girl is standing on the podium at the Athens Paralympic Games with three gold medals around her neck. Her name is Jessica Long. Twenty years later, at the end of six Paralympic Games, she has become one of the most decorated swimmers in the history of the Paralympic movement.

Jessica Long was not, however, born in Maryland. On February 29, 1992 — strictly speaking, she only has a birthday every four years — a baby girl is born in Bratsk, in eastern Siberia, under the name Tatiana Olegovna Kirillova. Her parents, barely out of their teens, cannot raise her and place her in an orphanage. Her legs, affected by fibular hemimelia, a congenital malformation of the long bones, have not formed normally.

At thirteen months, she is adopted by Steve and Beth Long, a couple from Baltimore, Maryland, who at the same time adopt her brother Joshua, from the same Siberian orphanage. A few months later, her legs are amputated below the knee so that she can walk on prosthetics, the first step in a long series of operations. Her parents sign her up for gymnastics, skating and climbing, before she finds her sport in the family pool, at ten, then joins her first competitive club in 2002.

Years later, talking to the specialist podcast SwimSwam about what she feels in the water, she would say that she does not experience herself as someone without legs. At twelve, she makes the U.S. team for the Athens Games, in 2004, and comes away with three gold medals, in the 100-meter and 400-meter freestyle and in the 4x100-meter relay. An extraordinary career is beginning.

She does not stop there. Singled out in the mid-2000s by the prestigious James E. Sullivan Award, she becomes the first Paralympic athlete to receive that honor, usually reserved for the finest prospects in American amateur sport. In the pool, Beijing, in 2008, brings her four more titles, a silver medal and a bronze. In London, in 2012, she climbs to the top of the podium five times, flanked by two silvers and a bronze. Rio, in 2016, marks a relative halt: a single title, in the 200-meter individual medley, rounded out by three silver medals and two bronzes. She bounces back in Tokyo — the 2020 Games, held in 2021 — where she wears the captain's armband for the U.S. women's team and adds new gold medals to her tally, before winning two more titles in Paris, in 2024, in the 100-meter butterfly and the 400-meter freestyle. At the end of six Paralympic Games, the counter stops at 18 titles and 31 medals in all.

That early success has a flip side. After her first bronze medal, in Beijing, she asks her mother one day: "do you still love me?" — a question she recalls, in a profile devoted to her by NBC Olympics, as the sign of a relationship to winning already out of all proportion for a teenager.

The real vertigo comes after Rio. Long carried along by the honors, Jessica Long goes through a severe depression. On the official Paralympic Games website, she would speak of the many dark days that followed her return from Rio. She gradually comes through it, never hiding it publicly afterward, doing her part to open up the conversation about mental health in elite sport.

In the meantime, in December 2013, she returns to Siberia for the first time and meets her biological parents, an episode followed by NBC's cameras for the documentary Long Way Home, broadcast during the Sochi Winter Olympics, in 2014. Facing her birth mother, she makes a point of saying that she does not hold the choice of adoption against her. "I think that was really brave," she says of that decision, taken when her parents were still teenagers themselves.

Now married to Lucas Winter, a soccer coach, Jessica Long published her autobiography, Unsinkable, co-written with her sister Hannah, in 2024. She is aiming for a seventh Paralympic appearance, in Los Angeles in 2028, while already taking on more and more work as a public speaker and sports commentator. Her story, made widely known in the United States by a Toyota commercial aired during the Super Bowl in 2021, has turned her into a point of reference for families who adopt children with disabilities.
