In the hallways of Fine Hall, the mathematics building at Princeton, students in the 1970s sometimes cross paths, at night, with a solitary figure covering the blackboards with symbols, dates, and messages that add up to nothing, before vanishing before dawn. The staff eventually let him be; some professors, who had known him in another life, quietly make sure no one chases him off. The newcomers call him the "Phantom of Fine Hall." Few know who he had been.

This man's name is John Forbes Nash Jr. Born on June 13, 1928, in Bluefield, a small town in West Virginia, he enters the Carnegie Institute of Technology in Pittsburgh on a scholarship, first in chemical engineering before switching to pure mathematics. Arriving at Princeton for his doctorate in 1948, he writes a twenty-eight-page thesis on non-cooperative games, supervised by Albert W. Tucker. In it, he introduces a concept of strategic equilibrium that will soon bear his name, the Nash equilibrium, and that will become one of the most widely used tools in game theory, with applications reaching far beyond economics.

What follows looks like an unbroken rise. An instructor at MIT starting in 1951, he earns tenure there in 1958. In February 1957, he marries Alicia Lardé, a physicist from El Salvador and an MIT graduate. He also works as a consultant for the RAND Corporation, at the heart of American strategic research. Nothing, on the surface, hints at what comes next.

In late 1958, Nash's behavior changes: he starts seeing coded messages in the press, believing himself entrusted with a mission of supreme importance, hearing voices. In April 1959, he's admitted to McLean Hospital, near Boston, where he's diagnosed with paranoid schizophrenia. He is thirty. That spring, he resigns from his tenured position at MIT, just months before the birth of his second son, John Charles Martin Nash, who will himself be diagnosed with schizophrenia as a teenager, before nonetheless earning a PhD in mathematics from Rutgers University.

More than ten years follow, marked by repeated involuntary hospitalizations, notably at Trenton State Hospital in New Jersey, where he receives insulin coma therapy for a time, a common psychiatric practice at the time. Worn out, Alicia divorces him in 1963. She doesn't abandon him, though: after his last hospital discharge, in 1970, she takes him in as a boarder in her home. In the autobiography he writes in 1995 for the Nobel Foundation, Nash looks back on this period with unusual restraint for a text meant to accompany a prize: "I spent times of the order of five to eight months in hospitals in New Jersey, always on an involuntary basis and always attempting a legal argument for release. And it did happen that when I had been long enough hospitalized that I would finally renounce my delusional hypotheses and revert to thinking of myself as a human of more conventional circumstances and return to mathematical research."

It's in this in-between state, out of the hospital but not quite recovered, that Nash becomes the Phantom of Fine Hall — tolerated, quietly protected in his own way by a department that never forgot who he had once been.

From the mid-1980s on, improvement takes hold, slow and non-linear, but real: the episodes grow rarer, more stable thinking regains the upper hand. Nash would later place in these years the lasting shift that let him return to sustained mathematical work.

In 1994, the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences awards him, together with John Harsanyi and Reinhard Selten, the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences, "for their pioneering analysis of equilibria in the theory of non-cooperative games." The prize rewards work then forty-four years old, completed well before the illness: that single early thesis had been enough to transform the field.

The biography journalist Sylvia Nasar devotes to him in 1998, A Beautiful Mind, followed by Ron Howard's film of the same name in 2001, starring Russell Crowe and Jennifer Connelly and winner of four Academy Awards including Best Picture, make him known far beyond scientific circles. That same year, 2001, he and Alicia remarry. In 2015, Nash receives a second major honor, this time for his work on nonlinear partial differential equations: the Abel Prize, mathematics' highest honor, shared with Louis Nirenberg.

He doesn't have long to enjoy this final tribute. On May 23, 2015, on the way back from the ceremony held in Norway, the taxi carrying John and Alicia Nash from Newark Airport loses control and hits a guardrail on the New Jersey Turnpike. Neither is wearing a seatbelt; both are thrown from the vehicle and die in the crash. Nash was 86. He leaves behind, beyond the equilibrium that bears his name, a rare document: a first-person account, plain and precise, of what the road back can look like from an illness that, in 1959, was said to have no way back.
