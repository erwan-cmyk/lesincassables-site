She's eighteen, without a penny to spare, and without a guitar. In a shop in Saskatoon, Saskatchewan, Joni Mitchell's eyes land on a $36 baritone ukulele, the only stringed instrument she can afford. At the time, she has no serious musical ambitions: the instrument is only meant to accompany bawdy songs sung among friends. "I took up the guitar, or rather the ukulele, at eighteen, with no ambition of a career in music, just to accompany some bawdy songs," she would later say. No one, at the time, imagines that this left hand would become one of the most singular harmonic tools in popular song.

That hand had already been through an ordeal. Nine years earlier, around 1952, just as the Anderson family settles in Saskatoon, young Roberta Joan — the future Joni — contracts polio, in the midst of recurring epidemics in Canada, before the first vaccines arrive. She is nine. "I had polio when I was nine years old. My spine was twisted like a train wreck. I couldn't walk. I was paralyzed," she would tell the press decades later. She's hospitalized for several weeks, watched over by a mother who refuses to leave her alone, one of the children that year who escapes the most severe forms of the disease, while others don't come back from it at all.

She learns to walk again, and quickly regains the energy of her age: "When I got my legs back, I rock 'n' rolled all through my teens," she would tell Mojo magazine. But her left hand keeps a stiffness that never fully disappears. Years later, when she finally takes up the guitar, that hand can't form the standard chords her ear demands. Rather than give up, she works around the obstacle: she changes the tuning of the strings so that rich harmonies become reachable for fingers that struggle to press down on a neck by the usual rules. Out of that constraint comes a method. Over the course of her career, Joni Mitchell will use close to fifty different tunings, a harmonic language all her own that first unsettles the people around her. "My style was very different. I kept being told I played wrong," she would recall. She draws a lesson from it that she likes to repeat: "It's only through error that discovery is made."

From Saskatoon, she leaves for Toronto, then the folk clubs of Detroit and New York. Her first album comes out in 1968, produced by David Crosby. Clouds and Ladies of the Canyon follow, then Blue, in 1971, a record of rupture and introspection that becomes a landmark of the singer-songwriter genre. Court and Spark, in 1974, sends her to the top of the charts; Hejira and Mingus, at the end of the decade, pull her toward jazz, culminating in a 1979 collaboration with bassist Charles Mingus himself. This catalog, full of stylistic turns her audience doesn't always follow, earns her eleven Grammy Awards, a Grammy Lifetime Achievement Award in 2002, and a double induction, in 1997, into both the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame and the Songwriters Hall of Fame.

In the 1990s, polio comes back to haunt her in another form: post-polio syndrome, extreme fatigue, joint pain, muscle weakness, which sometimes affects former patients decades after the original illness. Faced with how little conventional medicine had to offer at the time on this poorly understood syndrome, she remarked, with her characteristic irony, that as far as she could tell, the medical establishment's answer was little more than: lie down and wait it out. She turns to alternative approaches to keep living and working.

In March 2015, a brain aneurysm strikes her down at her home in Los Angeles, where she's found unconscious. She loses the ability to speak and to use her legs. Rehabilitation is long, made of small daily gains. "The aneurysm took a lot more. It took my speech, and it took my ability to walk," she would say. Starting in 2017, she gathers musician friends at her house for informal sessions, the "Joni Jams," which help her reconnect with music without the pressure of performing live. In 2021, accepting the Kennedy Center Honors, she sums up the throughline of a life twice marked by having to relearn everything: polio, she has said, was a kind of dress rehearsal for the rest of her life — she'd had to come back from major setbacks more than once, and this last one, she added dryly, was a doozy, but she was walking, limping, and doing fine.

In July 2022, at the Newport Folk Festival, the festival where she first performed as a young folk singer in the 1960s, she returns to the stage as a surprise guest during Brandi Carlile's set, for a thirteen-song set. She shares there what the aneurysm taught her about the body's memory — how much it can erase, how to get out of a chair, how to get out of bed, things you no longer know how to do and have to relearn one movement at a time. Seventy years after first learning to walk again in a room in Saskatoon, Joni Mitchell had just done it a second time, in front of an audience on its feet.
