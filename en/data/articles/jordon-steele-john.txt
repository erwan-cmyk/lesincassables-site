One day in March 2018, on the lawns of Parliament House in Canberra, a senator hurries his wheelchair toward the chamber so as not to miss a vote. The wheels sink into the grass and jam. By the time he works himself free, the bells have stopped ringing and the vote is closed. Jordon Steele-John arrives a few seconds too late, in a building opened thirty years earlier without anyone, back then, having really thought about him.

The episode is far from isolated. In his own office, the door frames, too narrow for his wheelchair, regularly scrape his knuckles. The toilets are not accessible to him; he has to go elsewhere in the building to find one. On the Senate floor, he cannot wheel over to his colleagues to greet them and remains assigned to an isolated seat at the back of the chamber. The symbol is stark: the man who will become one of the most determined voices for the rights of people with disabilities sits in an institution that, quite literally, had not planned for his presence.

Jordon Steele-John is born in October 1994 in the United Kingdom with congenital cerebral palsy, which affects his mobility and requires the use of a wheelchair. As a child, he emigrates to Western Australia with his parents and grandparents; there he is homeschooled alongside his brother. He joins the Australian Greens at just 16 and, still a teenager, runs in a series of elections he loses every time: a state election in 2013, a federal election the same year, then a special Senate election in 2014. Nothing in this early path of successive defeats hints at what is to come.

The turning point comes in 2017. Senator Scott Ludlam, who led the Greens' ticket in Western Australia, resigns after discovering that he also holds New Zealand citizenship — a dual nationality that makes him ineligible under the Australian Constitution. A recount of the votes designates Steele-John, next on the ticket, to replace him. He is declared elected in late October 2017 and sworn in on November 10, becoming at 23 the youngest senator in Australian history and the first parliamentarian with a physical disability to sit in the upper house of the Australian Parliament. His entry into the Senate also cuts short the political science studies he was pursuing by distance learning at Macquarie University.

Reelected in 2019 with 11.8 percent of his state's vote, he now holds his party's portfolios for health, disability rights and foreign affairs, after having been responsible for youth, defense and digital rights. It is on the terrain of disability, however, that he carries the most weight. In 2019, his campaign leads to the creation of a Royal Commission into Violence, Abuse, Neglect and Exploitation of People with Disability — one of the largest public inquiries ever conducted in Australia on the subject. The following year, he secures the establishment of a compensation program for Australians affected by birth defects linked to thalidomide, a drug prescribed to pregnant women in the 1950s and 1960s. In 2021, his pressure helps push the government to abandon the "independent assessments" for the National Disability Insurance Scheme (NDIS), a mechanism that advocacy groups considered too rigid and disconnected from the real needs of participants.

His first speech in the Senate, in November 2017, is not confined to his own situation: he argues that every Australian should be able to sleep in their own bed, denounces tax dodging by large corporations, and defends the welcoming of refugees, presenting himself as the spokesman for a generation in a hurry to act. He later returns, in an interview with an Australian disability services provider, to what it means to have grown up with a disability in his country: "Over the course of my life I've learned that to be a young person with a disability in contemporary Australia is to occupy the intersection of some of our society's most ingrained myths. At every opportunity I've worked hard to bust these myths and be a strong voice for the issues that matter."

Named One Young World Global Youth Politician of the Year in 2018 and then McKinnon Emerging Political Leader of the Year in 2019, Jordon Steele-John remains, nearly a decade after his surprise entry into Parliament, one of the few elected officials in the world to legislate from a wheelchair. He keeps reminding his colleagues, in a chamber designed without him in mind, that accessibility is not a secondary accommodation but a condition of democracy itself.
