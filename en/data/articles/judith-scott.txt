In the studio at Creative Growth Art Center, in Oakland, a woman sits on the floor, surrounded by balls of multicolored yarn, scraps of fabric, and spools of twine. For hours, without a word, she winds thread after thread around a shopping cart, a bicycle wheel, or a plain piece of wood, until the original shape disappears completely beneath the layers of fiber. Five days a week, for eighteen years, she comes in to work this way, with an intensity those close to her would describe as unstoppable. Out of that silent ritual come nearly two hundred sculptures, now held at MoMA and the Brooklyn Museum, or shown at the Venice Biennale. The woman's name is Judith Scott.

On May 1, 1943, in Cincinnati, Ohio, Judith and her twin sister Joyce are born. Judith has Down syndrome. In the family home, the two girls are raised side by side, inseparable. "We played in the same space, slept in the same bed, we did absolutely everything together," Joyce would tell the BBC decades later.

But scarlet fever contracted in early childhood has left Judith completely deaf — a fact no one, at the time, diagnoses. Her unrecognized deafness is taken for a more severe intellectual disability than the one she actually has: she will never be taught sign language, lip-reading, or speech. On medical advice, her parents resign themselves to placing her, on October 18, 1950, in an Ohio institution, the Columbus State Institution, then in a smaller facility in Gallipolis. She is seven years old. A note added to her file years later sums up how the institution sees her: "Her presence on the ward is a disturbing influence." Judith will remain there for thirty-five years, cut off from her family and her twin.

Grown up and settled in California, Joyce never gave up on her sister. After a long and complicated process with the Ohio authorities, she obtains legal guardianship in 1985 and brings Judith to live alongside her, in the San Francisco Bay Area — a state where, unlike the Ohio of the 1950s, people with intellectual disabilities are entitled to ongoing educational support. Judith is 42. She has never spoken a word.

In April 1987, Joyce enrolls her at the Creative Growth Art Center, an art studio founded in Oakland in 1974 for adults with disabilities. For nearly two years, Judith shows no interest in painting, drawing, or ceramics. The turning point comes at the end of 1987, during a workshop led by visiting textile artist Sylvia Seventy: Judith discovers yarn and twine, and never lets go of them again. She begins wrapping found objects — keys, plastic pipes, bicycle wheels, shopping carts — in successive layers of thread and fabric, until they take on organic forms, cocoons or totems, whose core stays hidden forever from anyone looking at them.

Recognition comes gradually, driven in particular by the writings of art historian John MacGregor, who follows and documents her work for several years. Her sculptures enter the collections of the Museum of Modern Art in New York, the San Francisco Museum of Modern Art, and the American Folk Art Museum, along with several European collections, including the Collection de l'Art Brut in Lausanne. In 2014-2015, the Brooklyn Museum gives her her first major American retrospective, "Judith Scott: Bound and Unbound," organized by curators Catherine J. Morris and Matthew Higgs, and later shown at other American museums. Three years later, in 2017, her work appears at the 57th Venice Biennale, "Viva Arte Viva," curated by Christine Macel, alongside the most celebrated artists of the international contemporary scene — one of the rare occasions on which outsider art is invited at that level into the art world's great global gathering.

Judith Scott dies on March 15, 2005, in Dutch Flat, California, at the age of 61. In the memoir she would devote to her sister, Joyce writes of those eighteen years in the studio: "Through her art, Judy found a way to create beauty from what others discarded and, most importantly, she found her voice." A woman no one had ever taught a language ended up inventing one, made of wool, thread, and patience, that museums around the world go on exhibiting today.
