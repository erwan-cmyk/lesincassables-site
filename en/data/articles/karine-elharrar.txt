On November 1, 2021, Karine Elharrar's car pulls up outside the Glasgow conference center where COP26 is opening. Israel's Minister of National Infrastructure, Energy and Water Resources is expected inside to meet her counterparts from around the world and negotiate on climate. But none of the site's entrances is accessible to her wheelchair. Summit staff make her wait, move her from one access point to another, then offer her a shuttle: it is not adapted either. The hours pass, one attempt follows another, nothing works. Karine Elharrar ends up turning back to her hotel in Edinburgh, without having been able to attend the first day of the planet's biggest climate gathering.

She recounts the episode that same day on Twitter, in English: "I came to COP26 to meet my world counterparts and to advance a common battle against climate change. It is sad that the UN that advances accessibility to people with disabilities, in the year 2021, does not ensure accessibility to its events." The message travels through newsrooms around the world and reopens, at the very heart of climate diplomacy, the debate over the accessibility of major international summits.

Israeli Prime Minister Naftali Bennett calls the situation "unacceptable" and has the minister's car added to his own official motorcade the next day, to guarantee her entry. Foreign Minister Yair Lapid publicly criticizes the organizers. On the British side, Ambassador Neil Wigan offers his apologies. Karine Elharrar finally enters the summit on November 2, in the prime minister's car, the day after being barred from taking part by the very people who were organizing it.

Born on October 9, 1977, in Holon, Israel, to Jewish parents who immigrated from Morocco, Moti and Colette Elharrar, Karine Elharrar lives with muscular dystrophy, a disease that causes muscle weakness, and uses a wheelchair. She attends Kugel High School and then chooses law: a bachelor's degree at the College of Management Academic Studies, in Israel, followed by an LLM from American University in Washington. Now settled in Rishon LeZion, married and the mother of two children, she has built her career on that legal foundation, with a specialty that will never leave her: between 2008 and 2013, she runs a legal clinic at Bar-Ilan University devoted to the rights of Holocaust survivors, people with disabilities and retirees, three categories of citizens the system often struggles to hear.

In 2012, she joins Yesh Atid, the young centrist party founded by the journalist turned politician Yair Lapid. She is elected to the Knesset in the January 2013 elections, tenth on the list of a party that enters parliament with many newcomers, then reelected without interruption: in 2015, twice in 2019, in 2020 within the Blue and White alliance, then in March 2021. In parliament, she chairs the State Control Committee before being appointed, in May 2021, to head the powerful Knesset Arrangements Committee, the body that organizes parliamentary business in the absence of a government, at the most uncertain moment in recent Israeli political life, between two attempts to form a coalition without Benjamin Netanyahu.

In June 2021, with the formation of the Bennett-Lapid government, she becomes Minister of National Infrastructure, Energy and Water Resources, a strategic portfolio for a country betting on its energy independence, and gives up her Knesset seat under the so-called Norwegian Law. She holds the post until 2022. Four years earlier, in 2017, the Israel Democracy Institute had already awarded her, together with her fellow lawmaker Roi Folkmann, its Outstanding Parliamentarian Award, in recognition of work built, among other things, on the defense of the rights of people with disabilities.

The Glasgow episode changed nothing about her political trajectory, but it did change, at least for a time, the way an international summit thinks about accessibility. That an energy minister should find herself, for want of a ramp or an adapted vehicle, shut out of discussions on the future of the planet laid bare how much accessibility is still treated as a logistical detail rather than a condition of equal participation. Karine Elharrar, for her part, turned it into one more political argument, in keeping with a career spent defending, first as a lawyer and then as a legislator, those whom the architecture of institutions forgets: the right to take part in decisions that bind everyone should never depend on the ability to climb stairs.
