On August 3, 2012, in the Olympic pool in London, a 15-year-old no one was expecting touches the wall first in the 800-meter freestyle. Katie Ledecky finishes ahead of Spain's Mireia Belmonte García and the defending Olympic champion, Britain's Rebecca Adlington, with a time of 8:14.63 that shatters Janet Evans's American record. Facing reporters, she sums up the scene with disarming simplicity: "I didn't really expect gold, but I'll take it." That evening, no one yet knows that this unknown will become the most decorated Olympic swimmer in history — or that she will do it, from 2015 on, while managing an invisible chronic illness.

Kathleen Genevieve Ledecky is born on March 17, 1997, in Bethesda, in the suburbs of Washington, D.C., to a lawyer father and a mother who swam in college before becoming a hospital administrator. At 6, she follows her older brother Michael to the neighborhood club, the Palisades Swim & Tennis Club, before joining the Nation's Capital Swim Club, where coach Yuri Suguiyama shapes her powerful stroke and her very particular kick. Even before entering Stone Ridge School of the Sacred Heart, she is already dominating the American junior championships over 400, 800 and 1,500 meters — an early sign.

After London, Ledecky piles up titles under her new coach, Bruce Gemmell: four gold medals at the 2013 world championships in Barcelona, then an unprecedented peak in Kazan, Russia, in the summer of 2015. There she becomes the first person, man or woman, to win the 200, 400, 800 and 1,500-meter freestyle at a single major international meet, breaking several world records along the way, including the 800-meter mark, which she lowers by more than three and a half seconds.

It is precisely in Kazan that everything shifts, quietly. One evening, after dinner, Ledecky feels abnormally feverish and dizzy; in the days that follow, episodes of extreme fatigue and lightheadedness repeat every time she gets out of the pool. On her coach's advice, she sees a specialist at Johns Hopkins, who makes a diagnosis: postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome, or POTS, a disorder of the autonomic nervous system that causes an abnormal rise in heart rate on standing up. In her memoir, she would describe the mechanism precisely: "I pool blood in the vessels below my heart when I stand."

For nearly a decade, Ledecky chooses to keep that information largely private, simply adjusting her daily routine: more salt, more hydration, compression gear and extra vigilance in heavy heat or illness. "I really just had to add salt to my diet and wear compression gear," she would sum up in her memoir. The syndrome does not stop her from crushing the field in Rio in 2016, where she becomes only the second woman in history to win three individual freestyle events at the same Games (200, 400 and 800 meters, the last in a world record of 8:04.79), nor from going toe to toe in Tokyo in 2021 with Australia's Ariarne Titmus, taking gold in the very first Olympic final of the women's 1,500 meters.

In May 2024, a few weeks before the Paris Games, Ledecky receives the Presidential Medal of Freedom from President Joe Biden, the highest American civilian honor — a first for a swimmer. The following month, in her memoir Just Add Water: My Swimming Life, she makes her diagnosis public, with a message addressed to other patients: "The biggest thing is to trust the health professionals you're working with."

In Paris, she reaches the podium in all four events she swims — bronze in the 400 meters, gold in the 1,500 meters, silver in the 4x200-meter relay, and a ninth career Olympic title in the 800 meters that puts her level with the Soviet gymnast Larisa Latynina for the record number of women's Olympic gold medals. With fourteen Olympic medals in all, she officially becomes the most decorated Olympic swimmer in history. "It was kind of just a feeling of relief," she says after the race.

Far from easing off, Ledecky keeps harvesting: a seventh consecutive world title in the 800 meters at the 2025 world championships in Singapore, a first world record in seven years at that distance (8:04.12), and another win in the 1,500 meters at the 2026 Pan Pacific Championships. Training in Gainesville, Florida, under coach Anthony Nesty, she already has Los Angeles 2028 in her sights.

More than ten years after Kazan, POTS is part of Ledecky's daily life without having dictated the story of her career. She has made it neither an obstacle she brandishes nor a shameful secret: just one more thing to manage, on the same footing as training, nutrition or sleep, in the life of an athlete who, at 29, keeps pushing the limits of her sport.
