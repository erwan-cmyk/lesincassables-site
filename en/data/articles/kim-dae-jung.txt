On August 8, 1973, in the early afternoon, a group of men bursts into room 2211 of the Grand Palace Hotel in Tokyo, where Kim Dae-jung has just finished a political meeting. He is seized, dragged out of the hotel, then taken aboard a boat that puts out to sea toward Korea. On board, his hands and feet are tied and weights are attached to them: everything indicates that he is to be thrown overboard. The plan fails by a hair. The Japanese coast guard spots the vessel, gives chase and fires a flare. The kidnappers then release their hostage near Busan. Five days after his disappearance, Kim Dae-jung is found alive at his home in Seoul. In 2007, South Korea's intelligence services will acknowledge that the abduction had been carried out by their agency, the KCIA, with at least the tacit approval of the president at the time, Park Chung-hee.

Born in 1924 in the island county of Sinan, in the southwest of the Korean peninsula, Kim Dae-jung first runs a shipping company before entering politics in the 1950s and winning election to the National Assembly in 1961. There he builds a reputation as an orator, notably with a speech of more than five hours delivered to delay a vote, in 1964. But it is his candidacy in the 1971 presidential election, against General Park Chung-hee — who had come to power ten years earlier in a coup d'état — that changes the course of his life. In a vote marked by massive fraud, Kim still collects 44 percent of the ballots. Shortly after his defeat, a heavily loaded truck slams into his car. He and two of his aides are seriously injured. Kim will be left with a lasting after-effect: an injury to the hip and leg that will still trouble him decades later. Convinced that this "accident" is in reality a disguised assassination attempt, he chooses to flee to Japan — where, two years later, the KCIA's agents will catch up with him.

The following years give him little respite. Under surveillance, placed under house arrest, he remains a target for the military regime. In May 1980, as South Korea falls under the martial law imposed by General Chun Doo-hwan, Kim Dae-jung is arrested and accused of having instigated the unrest that has just broken out in Gwangju. A military court sentences him to death. Under pressure from several foreign governments, the sentence is commuted to life imprisonment, then reduced to twenty years in prison. He will later go into exile in the United States, before returning to Korea to resume his political fight.

He will still have to wait nearly two more decades, and through several unsuccessful presidential bids. On December 18, 1997, with 40.3 percent of the vote against 38.7 percent for his main rival, Kim Dae-jung finally wins the presidential election, by a margin of just over a point and a half. On February 25, 1998, he becomes the eighth president of the Republic of Korea — the first in the country's history to come to power through a peaceful transfer to the opposition.

His presidency is marked by an unprecedented policy of openness toward North Korea, remembered in history as the "Sunshine Policy." It culminates in June 2000 with the very first inter-Korean summit since the partition of the peninsula in 1945: in Pyongyang, Kim Dae-jung meets the North Korean leader Kim Jong-il, with whom he signs, on June 15, a joint declaration opening a brief window of dialogue between the two Koreas. A few months later, the Nobel Committee awards him the Nobel Peace Prize, for his work for democracy and human rights in South Korea and, more broadly, in East Asia, as well as for his efforts for peace and reconciliation with North Korea.

This diplomatic breakthrough will later be tarnished by a controversy revealed after the end of his term. In 2003, it emerges that large payments had been secretly made to Pyongyang by major South Korean conglomerates, including Hyundai, in the days leading up to the 2000 summit. The affair fuels a lasting political controversy in South Korea, without any evidence establishing personal enrichment on the part of Kim Dae-jung.

Kim Dae-jung dies on August 18, 2009, in Seoul. Of the man they tried to silence with a truck, and then on the open sea, what will remain is the image of a head of state who carried, to the end of his life, a leg injury that had never entirely left him — and the proof that the determination of a single man could, in South Korea, finally get the better of several decades of dictatorship.
