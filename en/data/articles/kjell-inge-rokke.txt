In 1979, a young Norwegian lands in Seattle with nothing to his name but experience as a fisherman and a firm intention to put money aside. Next stop, Alaska, where factory trawlers crisscross the Bering Sea fishing for Alaska pollock, in the cold and on night watches. While his crewmates spend their pay on cars and nights out, Kjell Inge Røkke saves every dollar. Three years later, in 1982, he puts together the $75,000 down payment needed to buy his first boat, a 21-meter trawler. Others follow: the fleet grows fast and gives rise, in the late 1980s, to American Seafoods Group, which will become one of the largest industrial fishing companies in the world.

That discipline, the man says, was forged early, in a modest childhood in Molde, a small port town on Norway's west coast where he is born in 1958. Dyslexic, he struggles at school and leaves at fourteen, with no diploma and no clear prospects in a town where the horizon seems to end at the docks and the canneries. At eighteen, he ships out for the first time on a Norwegian fishing boat, before leaving to try his luck in the United States a few years later, in 1979. Røkke has never made a secret of his reading difficulties: he will later explain that they forced him, from a very young age, to learn differently, by observing and remembering rather than reading reports — a method he says he has kept all the way into the boardroom.

Under his leadership, American Seafoods brings aboard its vessels processing and freezing equipment that sets the standard in the industry. By the late 1990s, the company carries real weight in Alaskan waters, enough to weigh on the sector's regulation as well: it takes an active part in setting up conservation cooperatives and supports the American Fisheries Act of 1998, even at the cost of sacrificing part of its own fleet to curb overfishing.

The return to Norway, in the mid-1990s, is a change of scale. Through Resource Group International (RGI), the company he built across the Atlantic with the businessman Bjørn Rune Gjelsten, Røkke buys up stakes in Aker, one of the country's oldest industrial groups, founded in 1841 around the Oslo shipyards. RGI becomes its main shareholder in 1996; the merger of the two entities is completed in January 1997, and Røkke takes over as chairman of the board. The newcomer, who never set foot in a business school, quickly makes his mark: swift restructurings, hard-edged decisions, a reputation as a ruthless businessman, described in the business press as a formidable corporate raider. Under his influence, Aker ASA turns into a sprawling conglomerate: offshore oil and gas with Aker BP, oil engineering with Aker Solutions, marine biotechnology with Aker BioMarine, industrial software with Cognite. Three decades later, he remains its chairman and controlling shareholder, holding more than two-thirds of the capital through his holding company TRG AS.

The path is not free of a brush with the courts. In 2001, Røkke's yacht runs aground near Strömstad, in Sweden, an incident that casts doubt on the validity of his pleasure-boat license. The affair, revealed by the business daily Dagens Næringsliv, leads to a bribery investigation: Røkke is accused of having paid a Swedish official to obtain valid skipper's licenses for himself, his valet, and a Norwegian shipbroker. In June 2005, an Oslo court finds him guilty of bribery and license fraud, and sentences him to 120 days in prison, 90 of them suspended. He begins serving his sentence in 2007 and is released on parole after about twenty days.

The episode tarnishes his image without loosening his hold on the Norwegian economy. Røkke keeps building, and redirecting his empire: in 2016, the Aker group exits the fishing sector by selling its stake in Havfisk, as if to close the chapter of its origins. He also funds the construction of the Rev Ocean, one of the largest oceanographic research vessels in the world, designed to study plastic pollution and climate change at sea. Married, a father of four, settled in Vettre not far from Oslo, he is today worth more than $10 billion, according to Forbes. From onetime deckhand on the Alaskan trawlers to the chairmanship of one of Scandinavia's largest industrial groups, with no diploma and no easy relationship with the written word, Kjell Inge Røkke has built, through hard work and instinct, one of Norway's most powerful industrial empires.
