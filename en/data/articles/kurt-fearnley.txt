In November 2009, on the Kokoda Track, in Papua New Guinea, a man has been moving on all fours through the mud for ten days. Around him, the jungle climbs and drops without respite, cut by rivers that must be crossed by arm strength alone. He has not brought his wheelchair. Kurt Fearnley, 28, already a three-time Paralympic champion, is covering 96 kilometers crawling on his hands and knees, protected by gloves, wrist guards and kneepads he had custom-designed over the previous year. Three weeks earlier, in New York, he was winning his fourth title in that city's wheelchair marathon.

The choice of route is no isolated publicity stunt. A steep military trail where Australian and Japanese troops fought during the Second World War, the Kokoda Track tests able-bodied walkers in peak physical condition. Fearnley takes it on to draw attention to men's health, alongside the Movember campaign and the mental health organization Beyond Blue. Two brothers, several cousins and a handful of friends go with him, some fifteen people close to him in all. At the finish, at Owers' Corner, his parents are waiting: they have come in secret. He will later describe the crossing as the hardest physical test he has ever faced, tougher than any of his marathons.

Nothing at his birth hinted at such a path. Kurt Fearnley is born on March 23, 1981, in Cowra, New South Wales, and grows up in Carcoar, a small village in the hinterland. He has sacral agenesis, a rare congenital malformation that leaves his spine without its lowest vertebrae and without the sacrum entirely, resulting in severely underdeveloped legs. According to several accounts of his birth, doctors did not expect him to survive beyond his first week. He nonetheless grows up like the other children of the area, until he discovers wheelchair racing at 14. Three years later, he joins the elite training program at the NSW Institute of Sport, under coach Andrew Dawes.

His first Paralympic selection, at home in Sydney in 2000, earns him two silver medals at 19. It is only a beginning. Over five editions of the Paralympic Games, from Sydney to Rio de Janeiro in 2016, Fearnley builds one of the richest records in Australian athletics: thirteen medals in all, three gold, seven silver and three bronze. He becomes Paralympic champion in the 5000 meters and the marathon in Athens in 2004, then wins the marathon again in Beijing in 2008. He will stand on the marathon podium at four consecutive editions of the Games, from 2004 to 2016.

Outside the Games, Fearnley reigns over the world's major wheelchair marathons: four wins in New York, followed by a fifth in 2014, three straight titles in Chicago, and victories in London, Paris, Seoul and Sydney. At the Commonwealth Games, he adds gold in the 1500 meters in Delhi in 2010, then in the marathon on the Gold Coast in 2018 — his last competition, which he closes by carrying the Australian flag at the closing ceremony.

His life does not stop at the edge of the track. A teacher by profession, settled in Newcastle, married since 2010 to Sheridan Rosconi, whom he met at Charles Sturt University, he is the father of two children, Harry and Emilia. In 2014, he publishes an autobiography, Pushing the Limits: Life, Marathons & Kokoda, in which he returns at length to his crossing of Papua New Guinea. Since 2020, he has hosted the interview program One Plus One on the public broadcaster ABC, as well as a podcast.

His service has been recognized with two national honors: the Medal of the Order of Australia in 2005, then Officer of the Order of Australia in 2018. That same year, he becomes the first athlete with a disability to receive The Don Award, before induction into the Sport Australia Hall of Fame in 2023. Since 2020, a Sydney ferry has borne his name.

Since the end of his sporting career, Fearnley has turned his energy toward public roles: a member of the International Paralympic Committee's athletes' council, then of the Australian Sports Commission, he has chaired since September 2022 the board of the National Disability Insurance Agency (NDIA), the body that runs Australia's disability insurance scheme — a first for a person with a disability in that position. Reappointed for a further three-year term from January 2026, he continues, far from the athletics track, to shape the lives of hundreds of thousands of Australians with disabilities.
