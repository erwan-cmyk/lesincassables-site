On January 2, 2018, in the reception rooms of the presidential residence of Cheong Wa Dae, in Seoul, some two hundred and forty guests attend the New Year's reception given by President Moon Jae-in. On stage, Lee Hee-ah performs "Amazing Grace" at the piano, then sings in place of the singer Kang San-ae, who is ill and absent that day. The room sings along; at the end of the piece, the president comes over to embrace her. It is not the first time the pianist, then thirty-two, has performed before the country's highest authorities: fifteen years earlier, she was already sharing the stage of a New Year's concert with the soprano Sumi Jo.

In South Korea, she has been known since childhood as "the four-fingered pianist." Lee Hee-ah plays with two fingers on each hand — the left thumb, which has no bone, is of no use to her at the keyboard — a congenital condition combined with an impairment of the legs that required amputation below the knee at around the age of three. The exact origin of this malformation has never been confirmed by a medical source; her mother has mentioned taking motion-sickness medication during pregnancy, without any cause-and-effect link having been established with certainty.

Born on July 9, 1985, in Seoul, Hee-ah grows up alongside her mother, Woo Gap-seon, who very early becomes the mainstay of her journey, while part of the extended family openly questions whether she should be raised at all. From the age of six, Woo Gap-seon enrolls her daughter in piano lessons — less with the idea of making her a musician than to strengthen her hands. Several schools turn the child away before a teacher agrees to take her. Hee-ah's father, a former serviceman left disabled by an injury sustained in service, dies while she is still a teenager.

A year after her first steps at the keyboard, in 1992, the seven-year-old wins first prize at a national music competition for students; the following year, a national competition reserved for artists with disabilities is said to have honored her performance in turn, according to some sources. In the late 1990s, President Kim Dae-jung presents her with a presidential award for overcoming disability. Her working method becomes part of her legend: Lee Hee-ah holds herself to sessions of up to ten hours of piano a day, and devotes five whole years to mastering just one passage of Chopin's Fantaisie-Impromptu.

As an adult, she gives more than two hundred concerts, in South Korea and abroad — the United States, Japan, China, Taiwan, Hong Kong, Australia and Canada are among the countries where she has performed. She shares the stage with the soprano Sumi Jo and, in 2005, with the French pianist Richard Clayderman. That same year, and again in 2007, she releases two albums, marketed under the name "the four-fingered pianist."

The malformation of her hands is accompanied by a mild learning disability, which makes mathematics in particular difficult; she has learned to play much of her repertoire from memory rather than by reading the score. She herself speaks of it openly but without dwelling on it, preferring to stress what hard work has allowed her to build.

Since the mid-2000s, Lee Hee-ah has put her fame in the service of a larger project: reconciliation between the two Koreas. In 2007, she gives a charity concert in Seoul to fund medical equipment for people with disabilities in North Korea, with the ambition of performing in Pyongyang afterward. "North Koreans are kin and the closest neighbor to South Koreans, so it is natural that they remain close to my heart," she told the daily Korea Times at the time. In the years that follow, she becomes the spokesperson for an inter-Korean agricultural cooperative built around a symbolic project of exchanging strawberry seedlings between the two countries, organizes one donation drive after another — wheelchairs, food — for people with disabilities in the North, and in that capacity meets Presidents Kim Dae-jung and then Park Geun-hye. The Pyongyang concert she has dreamed of for more than a decade, however, appears never to have taken place.

Her last major, widely documented public appearance remains that 2018 New Year's reception at Cheong Wa Dae. An article published in 2021 by the Korean outlet World Korean, citing those close to her, indicates that she has reportedly since ceased all concert activity, her physical condition no longer allowing the exertion a full performance demands, and that her adaptive piano equipment has reportedly been put away — information that other sources have not confirmed since.

Asked by the American broadcaster Voice of America what keeps her going, she said, "I always live with a can-do spirit," adding that "even though your body may be challenged, it does not necessarily mean your thinking has to change."
