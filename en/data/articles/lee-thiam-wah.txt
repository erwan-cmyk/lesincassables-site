On the side of Jalan Kampar, in Klang, a teenager of barely fourteen sets up a stall of sweets in front of the family home every afternoon, in the late 1970s. Between customers, seated in his wheelchair, he reads — newspapers, magazines, books borrowed from the neighborhood, for want of money to buy them. The boy's name is Lee Thiam Wah. Nearly half a century later, he will run Malaysia's largest chain of mini-markets and rank among the country's richest men.

Lee Thiam Wah is born in 1964 in Klang, in the state of Selangor, into a family of eleven children. His father works on construction sites; his mother sells food in the street. At around eight months old, he contracts polio, which permanently paralyzes his legs: he will never walk again and has used a wheelchair ever since. The family can afford only six years of primary school — secondary schools, too far away and inaccessible to him, remain out of reach. That is the whole of his formal schooling.

He makes up for that lack of formal education by observing, reading, and listening to the adults around him. His paternal grandmother plays, during this period, a role he will later describe as decisive: she keeps telling him that he is diminished only physically, not mentally, and that he can still accomplish a great deal with his hands. As an adult, Lee Thiam Wah will also explain, more soberly, that few employers wanted to hire him given his disability, and that he had to learn to fend for himself. The sweets stall on Jalan Kampar is that first answer.

In 1987, at 23, he takes a step up: with RM17,000 in savings, he opens a small neighborhood grocery store in Klang, Pasaraya Hiap Hoe. There he learns the trade — customers' habits, negotiating with suppliers, managing inventory. Five years later, in 1992, he sells that first shop to launch a mini-market in Klang Utama named Pasar Mini 99. The name will stick: Lee Thiam Wah will explain that he chose the number because it evokes the idea of never being quite perfect, while always aiming for the best for the customer.

The chain grows slowly at first — eight outlets in 1998 — before picking up speed. In 1997, his wife, Ng Lee Tieng, joins the company as head of purchasing; she will remain a co-shareholder for decades, before sitting on the board of directors when the group goes public. Around 2000, all the stores are unified under the name 99 Speed Mart. The network passes the 100-store mark in 2008, reaches RM1 billion in revenue with 300 stores in 2011, then opens its thousandth outlet in 2017. In 2015, Lee Thiam Wah diversifies by acquiring the Malaysian and Singaporean Burger King franchise; in 2023, he becomes a major shareholder in Alliance Bank Malaysia.

On September 9, 2024, 99 Speed Mart lists on the Kuala Lumpur stock exchange. The offering, worth roughly $531 to $532 million, is Malaysia's largest IPO in seven years; the shares, priced at RM1.65, climb 14% on their first day of trading. Overnight, the fortune of Lee Thiam Wah, then 60, is estimated at about $3.3 billion by the Bloomberg Billionaires Index. The man whom, decades earlier, almost no employer was willing to give a chance now runs one of the country's largest retail companies — he remains its chief executive after the IPO.

Today, 99 Speed Mart claims more than 3,000 stores in Malaysia — about 3,086 at the end of March 2026, according to Forbes — or nearly 40% of the country's mini-market segment. The chain reached a new milestone in 2025 with the opening, in Fuzhou, China, of its very first store outside Malaysia. As for Lee Thiam Wah's personal fortune, it has continued to grow with the group's market valuation: Forbes put it at about $6.7 billion in mid-August 2026, which would make him the sixth-richest man in Malaysia. That figure, subject to market swings, should be treated with the caution that any wealth estimate based on a stock market valuation demands — but on its own, it speaks to the distance traveled.

Lee Thiam Wah continues to oversee his group's expansion himself, alongside his wife and their son, Lee Yan Zhong, now an alternate director of the family company. From the sweets stall on Jalan Kampar to the shelves of thousands of mini-markets across the country, his trajectory holds to one simple thread: that of a man deprived very early of walking and of school, who chose to build, store after store, what no one was offering him.
