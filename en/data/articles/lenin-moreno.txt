On May 24, 2017, in the reception rooms of the Carondelet presidential palace in Quito, a man wheels his chair up to the lectern to receive the presidential sash from the hands of his predecessor. Lenín Moreno becomes that day the head of state of Ecuador — and, for the four years that follow, the only sitting leader in the world to hold such an office from a wheelchair.

Nothing in his childhood foreshadowed a trajectory of this scale. Born on March 19, 1953, in Nuevo Rocafuerte, a village in the Ecuadorian Amazon, he is the son of a left-leaning schoolteacher who names him after Lenin out of conviction. In 1972, he marries Rocío González; the couple will have three daughters, Irina, Cristina and Carina. On January 3, 1998, while he is heading the national federation of tourism chambers, he is shot in the back by robbers outside a bakery in Quito. He is 44. The wound leaves him paraplegic and has confined him to a wheelchair ever since.

He gets through the following years by cultivating, alongside his institutional commitments, a deliberately lighthearted public image: several books of humor are attributed to him during this period. From 2001, he heads the national disability directorate, under two successive presidencies, before joining Rafael Correa's ticket as running mate in 2006.

Vice president from 2007 to 2013, Moreno places disability at the center of his term: an inclusive employment law imposing a 4 percent quota in companies, the widespread introduction of lowered curbs and accessible pedestrian crossings, the creation of a monthly allowance — the Joaquín Gallegos Lara bonus — for people with disabilities unable to work, the Manuela Espejo mission to identify people with disabilities and provide medical care nationwide, and an organic law on disability adopted in 2012. That same year, he is nominated for the Nobel Peace Prize by an official of Ecuador's commission for people with disabilities. From January 15 to February 14, 2013, he also serves as the country's acting president.

From 2013 to 2016, he serves as United Nations special envoy on disability and accessibility, appointed by Ban Ki-moon — a post that takes him to Geneva and extends, on an international scale, the work begun in Quito.

He returns to Ecuador to run for president and wins in the second round, on April 2, 2017, with 51.16 percent of the vote. His term, marked by a break with his mentor Correa and by austerity measures, ends in 2021 with an approval rating that has fallen to around 9 percent, against 77 percent at the start. He now lives in Paraguay, where he holds a post as commissioner within the Organization of American States.

It is from that country that Lenín Moreno must now answer corruption charges unrelated to his disability, but which weigh heavily on his political legacy. Ecuador's justice system suspects him of having favored, when he was vice president, the Chinese company Sinohydro in the award of the contract for the Coca Codo Sinclair hydroelectric dam, in exchange for bribes estimated at around $76 million paid between 2009 and 2018. The investigation opens in March 2019. On March 6, 2023, a judge authorizes the prosecution; on April 19 of that year, prosecutors request his pretrial detention after he fails to appear at a court summons. On December 8, 2025, Judge Olavo Hernandez orders him to stand trial, designating him as the "direct perpetrator of the crime of bribery." Several people close to him are also called to appear, including his wife and one of his daughters, suspected of complicity. The trial opens and stretches over more than a month of hearings; in early July 2026, prosecutors seek six and a half years in prison for Moreno and nineteen other defendants. Faced with these accusations, the former president defends himself publicly: "I have presented all the evidence to the attorney general's office, and they have not been able to prove that I received a single cent. Let's not allow such a great injustice to be committed," he declared, denouncing political persecution. A few days later, the court retires to deliberate; in mid-August, prosecutors were still asking for the hearing to be reconvened for the announcement of its decision. At the time of writing, no verdict has been handed down, and Lenín Moreno, like any defendant, is entitled to the presumption of innocence.

Whatever the outcome of the trial, the laws he had adopted — on employment, on the accessibility of Ecuador's streets, on support for the most dependent people — remain in force long after his departure from power. For four years, a country saw its president chair the council of ministers, receive heads of state and sign its laws from a wheelchair, without this ever being presented as an obstacle to the exercise of the office.
