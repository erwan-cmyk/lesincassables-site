Vienna, May 7, 1824. At the Kärntnertortheater, the hall is packed for the premiere of a symphony most of the musicians present have never heard in full: the Ninth. On the podium, in the middle of the orchestra, a fifty-three-year-old man with wild grey hair beats time with broad gestures. The conductor actually leading the performance, Michael Umlauf, has quietly asked the musicians and singers to ignore his cues and keep their eyes on him instead. Because the man beating time, standing before his own score all the way through the choral finale on Schiller's "Ode to Joy," can hear almost nothing of what he has just written. When the last note fades, the hall rises and erupts in applause. Beethoven, facing the musicians, doesn't react: he cannot hear the applause. It's the contralto Caroline Unger who takes him by the arm and turns him to face the audience, standing in the middle of the ovation. Ludwig van Beethoven discovers, by seeing it, the triumph he cannot hear.

Twenty-six years earlier, nothing hinted at this scene. Beethoven was born in Bonn — baptized on December 17, 1770, his exact birth date never firmly established for lack of a surviving record, but traditionally placed on December 16. He goes on to establish himself in Vienna as one of the most daring virtuoso pianists and composers of his generation. It's around 1798, when he's about twenty-seven, that the first signs appear of an affliction that will never leave him: severe tinnitus, growing difficulty following conversation, especially in noisy settings. He would later tell pianist Charles Neate that the trouble first appeared after a violent argument with a singer. The condition then worsens, slowly, year after year, never really stabilizing.

In 1801, in a letter to his childhood friend Franz Wegeler, he confides his growing isolation and his fear of being found out, before asserting his determination to carry on: he will, he writes, "seize Fate by the throat; it shall certainly not crush me completely." The following year, retreating to the village of Heiligenstadt on his doctor's advice, he writes a long text addressed to his two brothers, never sent, found only after his death: the Heiligenstadt Testament. In it, he describes the despair of a musician forced to hide his deafness for fear of judgment, and the pull of suicide, which he says he resisted so as not to leave the world before he had accomplished everything he felt within him.

On the exact nature of this deafness, historians and doctors still disagree. Otosclerosis, auditory nerve damage, Paget's disease, an autoimmune cause: several theories have been put forward over two centuries, none reaching consensus. An older theory, lead poisoning, relied for years on the analysis of a lock of hair attributed to the composer. In 2023, a genetic study conducted on a different, authenticated lock reopened the debate without settling it: it established mainly that the lock used for the lead-poisoning theory actually came from a woman with no family relation to Beethoven. The study, published in the journal Current Biology, uncovered genetic risk factors for liver disease and traces of a hepatitis B infection, without offering a definitive answer on the origin of his hearing loss.

Beethoven never goes completely deaf: to the end of his life, he can still perceive certain low sounds and sudden, powerful noises. But by the mid-1810s, ordinary conversation has become largely inaccessible to him. Those close to him then communicate with him in writing, in conversation books, several hundred of which survive today. It's during these years of growing sonic isolation that he composes much of the work for which he's remembered today: the final piano sonatas, the late string quartets, the Missa Solemnis, and this Ninth Symphony, written and rehearsed while he could follow an orchestra only by watching the movement of the bows.

He dies in Vienna on March 26, 1827, barely three years after that silent triumph at the Kärntnertortheater. Two centuries later, the "Ode to Joy" that closes his Ninth Symphony remains one of the most performed pieces of music in the world — composed note by note by a man who, on the night of its premiere, had to be turned around to discover, on the faces of the crowd, the ovation he could not hear.
