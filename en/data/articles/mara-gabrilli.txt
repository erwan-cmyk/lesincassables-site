In late August 1994, a car winds its way down a mountain road between Paraty, on the coast of Rio de Janeiro state, and Taubaté, in the interior of the state of São Paulo. On board, Mara Gabrilli, 26, is returning from a weekend by the ocean. On a stretch that locals already call the curve of death, the car leaves the road. The impact breaks her neck. Years later, she will recount having come, that day, very close to dying.

Nothing, until then, had marked out this young São Paulo native for public life. A psychology graduate of the Universidade Paulista, trained in advertising and marketing at the Escola Superior de Propaganda e Marketing, she works in advertising in São Paulo, one professional trajectory among so many in the Brazilian megacity. The accident changes everything in a fraction of a second. Mara Gabrilli becomes quadriplegic, paralyzed from the neck down. She spends five months in the hospital, two of them on a ventilator, before she can so much as breathe and speak again without assistance.

The years that follow are devoted to relearning how to live with a body that no longer responds as before, in a wheelchair, with the constant help of others for everyday tasks. Rather than withdrawing, she gradually settles into a role she will never leave: that of spokesperson for people with disabilities. In 1997, three years after the accident, she launches the Projeto Próximo Passo, an initiative dedicated to accessibility and the universal design of public spaces. Ten years later, the project becomes the Instituto Mara Gabrilli, today a Brazilian reference in scientific research on paralysis, accessibility consulting and support for Paralympic athletes.

Her growing profile and her fieldwork draw the attention of São Paulo's city hall, which in 2005 appoints her the city's first municipal secretary for people with disabilities. She is then elected to the city council, a seat she holds from 2009 to 2011, a period during which she introduces several dozen bills on accessibility and inclusion. The local level does not satisfy her for long: in 2010 she wins a seat as a federal deputy for the state of São Paulo, which she holds from 2011 to 2019, with reelection in 2014.

It is in the Chamber of Deputies that Mara Gabrilli puts her name to her most significant text. Appointed rapporteur of the Brazilian Inclusion Law for Persons with Disabilities, she steers the adjustment of the text to the demands of advocacy movements and to the United Nations Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities. Passed and then brought into force in January 2016, the law directly benefits around 45 million Brazilians with disabilities, nearly one Brazilian in five: it mandates inclusive schooling, penalizes discrimination, recognizes the right of people with disabilities to marry and to a reproductive life, and builds accessibility into the standards of the entire public administration.

In 2018, she is elected senator for São Paulo with more than 6.5 million votes, and takes office on February 1, 2019. Her commitment then extends beyond Brazil's borders: she is elected a member of the United Nations Committee on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities for a first term in 2019, then appointed again in 2025 for the 2025-2028 period, at the Conference of States Parties meeting in New York. There she sits alongside experts from around the world, tasked with examining, country by country, the implementation of the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities.

Nearly thirty years after the accident that broke her neck, Mara Gabrilli gets back, quite literally, on her feet. In April 2023, in New York, she straps into a French exoskeleton called Atalante and stands upright, takes a few steps, arms free, under the gaze of Linamara Battistella, the Brazilian physician who coordinates the Lucy Montoro public rehabilitation network and is evaluating with her the device's therapeutic potential. From that experience a new project is born: creating accessible walking centers in Brazil, where other paralyzed people could, like her, experience what technology makes possible again. From the seat of a car on a mountain road to the corridors of the Brazilian Senate, Mara Gabrilli has turned every stage of her reconstruction into a collective undertaking, for the benefit of millions of compatriots she has never met.
