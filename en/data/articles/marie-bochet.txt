At the Paralympic village at Fiames, in the Dolomites, in March 2026, Marie Bochet watches a course inspection without taking part in it. "I was there for the inspection, and I was thinking it made you want to put skis on," she says. For the first time since her Paralympic debut in Vancouver, sixteen years earlier, she is not skiing the course herself: as chef de mission of the French Paralympic delegation for the Milano Cortina 2026 Winter Paralympics, she has traded the race bib for responsibility over a team competing across three venues, from Milan to Cortina d'Ampezzo.

To understand what that shift represents, you have to go back to February 9, 1994, and the maternity ward in Chambéry. Marie Bochet then grows up in the Beaufortain, in Arêches-Beaufort, where her parents make Beaufort cheese. She is born with agenesis of the left forearm, a congenital malformation: the forearm simply did not form. She talks about it plainly and without pathos: "I was born with agenesis of the left forearm. Meaning? My left forearm didn't develop. I do have a little finger, all the same!"

She puts on her first skis at five, at Les Saisies, before joining the Albertville para-sports club and then a sport-study program built for elite skiers. Her family does not overprotect her. "When you know disability, it doesn't frighten you — quite the opposite," she would later sum up, adding that difference pushes you "to adapt" and makes you "much more creative."

At 16, she skis her first Paralympic Games in Vancouver, where she finishes fourth twice — close enough to the podium to understand that it is within her reach. She skis standing, in the LW6/8-2 category, one pole in her good hand, while preparing an economics and social sciences baccalauréat that she passes with honors, then studying at Sciences Po Paris through an arrangement for elite athletes. The Sochi Games, in 2014, then leave no room for doubt: downhill, super-G, super-combined, giant slalom — four gold medals in four races. Four years later, at Pyeongchang, she takes four more titles, in the downhill, super-G, giant slalom and slalom. Eight Paralympic crowns in two editions, 22 world championship titles, nine overall crystal globes: Marie Bochet becomes the most decorated skier in the history of her category at the Winter Games. In 2018, the Republic makes her an Officer of the Légion d'honneur, four years after making her a Knight.

Beijing 2022 marks a harsher turning point. In the downhill, she loses a ski mid-course and does not finish; she makes up for it with silver in the super-G, but comes home to France with her back in bad shape. In December 2022, she has surgery for a herniated disc and misses the whole of the following season. As early as June 2023, she puts into words what she already sensed, ahead of a 2023-2024 season deliberately left without a major championship: "It's never simple for an athlete to end their career, but this will be my last season. I need to make it official so that I can enjoy it." And so, in the spring of 2024, she closes fifteen years of top-level competition, with this conviction as her summary: "The work is done, I've helped this world grow, and today we have a talented next generation."

What follows looks nothing like a withdrawal. Co-chair of the elite athletes' commission of the French Paralympic and Sports Committee, she is named in February 2025 chef de mission of the French Paralympic delegation for the Milano Cortina Games. "I'm really very honored to have been offered the role of chef de mission, which is an important role," she says as the Games approach. On whether she misses competing, her answer is clear: "I really feel like I'm in the right place."

From March 6 to 15, 2026, the delegation she leads fields thirteen French athletes. The results are mixed: sixth in the medal table with twelve medals, four of them gold, France falls short of the top-four goal set before the competition. But the skier Arthur Bauchet, double Paralympic champion in the super-combined and giant slalom, and the young Aurélie Richard, whom she advises by virtue of a shared impairment and discipline, each come away with several medals — the next generation Marie Bochet spoke about, at work in front of her. On this Cortina course she no longer skis down, she watches a story she largely wrote go on being written without her. And that seems to suit her very well.
