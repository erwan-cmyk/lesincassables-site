Late August 2021, in the main pool of the Tokyo Aquatics Centre, Mark Malyar touches the wall in the 400-meter freestyle and looks up at the scoreboard. The clock reads 4:31.06: his own world record, improved by two seconds, won barely three days after a first Paralympic title. Exhausted, he tells Israeli media: "I'm happy that I succeeded in getting the result I want, I'm very tired. I didn't feel that I was even going that fast, but it worked and my body is just about done."

Mark Malyar is born on March 5, 2000, at the Bnei Zion Medical Center in Haifa, with a twin brother, Ariel. Both boys come into the world with cerebral palsy, which affects their motor skills to different degrees. They grow up in Kiryat Motzkin, in the suburbs of Haifa, the sons of Alex and Diana Malyar. At 5, the twins are enrolled at the swimming pool: less for competition than for rehabilitation, as part of an aquatic therapy program offered by the ILAN Haifa club.

Under coach Yaakov Benenson, the rehabilitation sessions gradually turn into full-fledged athletic training. Between the two brothers, a childhood rivalry — which Mark would later describe as keener than it is today — accompanies this shared progress. Both boys join the Israeli national para swimming team, Mark in the S7/SM7 class, Ariel in the S4 class, corresponding to a more severe impairment. In the Paralympic swimming classification system, these codes designate the swimmers' degree of functional mobility: the lower the number, the greater the physical impairment. The two brothers, born the same day with the same cerebral palsy, thus find themselves in separate classes, each facing opponents whose disability is as close as possible to his own.

At 17, Mark Malyar climbs onto his first international podium: bronze in the 100m backstroke at the 2017 World Para Swimming Championships in Mexico City. The Israeli Paralympic Committee names him athlete of the year. The following year, at the European Championships in Dublin, he adds silver in the 400m freestyle and two bronze medals, in the 100m breaststroke and the 100m backstroke.

In 2019, in London, he crosses a threshold: gold in the 400m freestyle, in a world record (4:33.64), and silver in the 200m individual medley. At 19, he is no longer merely a prospect of Israeli para swimming; he has become one of its faces.

The Tokyo Games, held in the summer of 2021 after a one-year postponement due to the pandemic, are his first Paralympic appearance. There he wins gold in the 200m individual medley SM7 in 2:29.01, a new world record, before the 400m freestyle title mentioned above. Such a double in three days had not been achieved by Israeli Paralympic swimming since 2004, and makes him, at 21, one of the standard-bearers of the country's entire Paralympic delegation. He rounds off his haul with a bronze medal in the 100m backstroke, bringing to three the number of podiums won at a single Games. Israeli President Isaac Herzog congratulates him publicly: "Mark, this is so exciting. You are unstoppable. Both a gold medal and a Made-in-Israel world record!"

Three years later, at the Paris Games, Mark Malyar adds a bronze medal in the 100m backstroke to his record. At the poolside, he dedicates his medal to Israeli soldiers, whom he thanks for allowing athletes to keep training — a statement widely picked up by the Israeli press.

In the summer of 2024, Mark Malyar simultaneously holds the world records in the 400m, 800m and 1,500m freestyle, as well as the 200m individual medley. A rare dominance in Paralympic swimming, where it is not common for the same swimmer to reign over both the distance events and the most complete event on the program.

Two decades after his first laps in Kiryat Haim, where he still trains under Yaakov Benenson, Mark Malyar remains attached to this pool which, for him as for his brother, was first meant to serve as rehabilitation. Ariel, too, continues to swim in Israel's colors, in his own class, far from his brother's records but with the same dedication in training. Born the same day, with the same cerebral palsy, the two twins have each charted their course in Paralympic waters — one the holder of a handful of world records, the other committed to his own races, on his own terms. What the rehabilitation pool of their childhood was simply meant to help them recover, mobility, it will have ended up turning, for one of the two brothers, into one of the most precisely timed sports in the world.
