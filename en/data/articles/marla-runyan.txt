Stadium Australia, Sydney, September 2000. On the starting line of the Olympic 1,500-meter final, Marla Runyan can make out neither the faces of her eleven rivals nor the scoreboard hanging above the track: she picks out the white lines of her lane and the shapes moving at the edge of her field of vision. The gun goes off. Four minutes and eight seconds later, she crosses the line in eighth place, ahead of the other American in the race, Suzy Favor Hamilton, who is left in last. That day, Marla Runyan becomes the first visually impaired athlete to have run an Olympic final against sighted competitors.

Nothing in her childhood pointed to such a trajectory. Born on January 4, 1969, in Santa Maria, California, the daughter of Valerie and Gary Runyan, she grows up passionate about gymnastics and soccer, fascinated, as the story goes, by the Romanian gymnast Nadia Comăneci, discovered on television during the Montreal Games. At nine, a diagnosis upends that sporting childhood: Stargardt disease, a hereditary macular degeneration, gradually destroys her central vision. She becomes legally blind, but keeps a peripheral vision that lets her perceive shapes, contrasts and movement off to the sides, never what lies straight ahead of her. Her mother fights to get her large-print textbooks and, with the support of the Lions Club, a closed-circuit reading system that allows her to stay in mainstream school.

A graduate of Adolfo Camarillo High School in 1987, she goes on to San Diego State University, where she earns a first master's degree in communicative disorders and then a second in special education, the very fields in which she would teach much later. On college tracks, she proves an accomplished heptathlete, competing in sprints, hurdles, jumps and throws. In 1996, she even qualifies for the U.S. Olympic trials in that discipline and finishes tenth, without securing a place for Atlanta. The disappointment is short-lived: that same year, she stands on the top step of the Paralympic podium in Atlanta, four years after already winning several sprint titles at the Barcelona Paralympic Games. In all, her Paralympic career would bring five gold medals.

Marla Runyan then leaves the combined events behind to concentrate on middle distance. In 1999, she wins the 1,500 meters at the Pan American Games. The following year, she qualifies for the Sydney Olympic Games over that same distance, becoming the first officially visually impaired athlete to make a U.S. Olympic team, in an event open to every competitor, sighted or not.

In Sydney, she first gets through the qualifying rounds, posting in the semifinal one of the fastest times among the non-automatic qualifiers, 4:06.14. In the final, the race is tight: the first eight finish within three seconds of one another. Algeria's Nouria Merah-Benida wins in 4:05.10; Britain's Kelly Holmes, a future double Olympic champion in Athens, takes seventh. Marla Runyan crosses the line just behind her, eighth, in 4:08.30.

Selected a second time for the Olympic Games in 2004, in Athens, over 5,000 meters, she also explores the marathon distance. On her debut, in New York in 2002, she covers the course in 2:27:10, the fastest American in the race, which at the time represented the second-best debut performance ever recorded by a U.S. woman at that distance. She confirms it next in Boston and then Chicago, before winning the Twin Cities Marathon in 2006 and being crowned USATF national marathon champion the same year. Between 2001 and 2003, she also strings together three consecutive national titles over 5,000 meters.

In 2001, she publishes her autobiography, No Finish Line: My Life As I See It, in which she recounts her double life as a competitor, first Paralympic, then Olympic. The following year, she marries her coach, Matt Lonergan; the couple would have a daughter in 2005. Back surgery puts an end, in 2008, to her hopes of returning to the Olympic Games. She turns then to teaching: since 2013, she has been a teacher and ambassador at the Perkins School for the Blind, in Massachusetts, an institution specializing in the education of blind and deafblind children, the very field in which she had trained in San Diego. From 2014, she also joins her husband as a coach at Northeastern University.

On her official website, she sums up her philosophy in a single sentence: the way we face hardship, she writes, reveals our character, our courage and our integrity. It is a line that could just as easily sum up her own trajectory: that of a competitor who never presented her sight as a disability set apart, but as one fact among others in the building of one of the most singular careers in American track and field.

Twenty-six years after Sydney, Marla Runyan has changed course once again. In 2025, she earns a doctorate in law at Syracuse University College of Law and, the same year, enters the U.S. Olympic and Paralympic Hall of Fame. At 57, the woman who remains the only American athlete to have competed at both the Paralympic Games and the Olympic Games goes on, far from the track, passing on what she learned running without ever seeing the finish line straight ahead of her.
