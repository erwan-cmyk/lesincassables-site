On September 6, 2024, on the mat at the Arena Champ-de-Mars in Paris, everything is decided in five seconds. Marta Arce lands a makikomi and tips over her Brazilian opponent Lucía da Silva. The referee first calls a waza-ari; the video review upgrades the decision to ippon. Immediate victory. The Spanish judoka takes bronze in the -57 kg J2 category at the Paris Paralympic Games — her first medal in twelve years.

Marta Arce Payno is born in 1977 in Valladolid, in the Castile and León region. She has complete oculocutaneous albinism, which causes a progressive loss of vision. In the Spain of the late 1970s and the 1980s, the educational and social inclusion of visually impaired children is not yet a priority: her childhood and adolescence are marked by difficulties she faces without any real institutional support. Not until she is sixteen does she join ONCE, the Spanish organization for the blind and visually impaired, and discover that structured support exists.

The turning point comes in Madrid, where she moves to study physiotherapy at the ONCE school attached to the Autonomous University of Madrid. Her older brother already practices judo; she decides to follow him and joins a club in the capital where other visually impaired judoka train. The mat becomes for her a space where competition is finally an even contest. The titles come quickly: European champion in 1997 and 1999, world champion in 2006, World Cup winner in Rio de Janeiro in 2001, then twenty-one Spanish national titles over the course of her career.

Her first Paralympic appearance, in Athens in 2004, brings her silver at -57 kg right away. She confirms it four years later in Beijing with another silver medal, this time at -63 kg. In London, in 2012, it is bronze she takes home — the same year her compatriot Maria Monica Merenciano also steps onto the Spanish Paralympic judo podium.

Between Olympic cycles, Marta Arce becomes a mother three times. Each birth means a spell away from the mat, then a return to competition — an ebb and flow that Olympics.com would sum up in a formula: "three children, three retirements, three comebacks." At the Tokyo Games, held in 2021, she finishes fifth: close to the podium, but without a medal. Twelve years have gone by since her London bronze.

For the Paris 2024 Games, the Spanish delegation gives her the honor of carrying the national flag at the Paralympic opening ceremony. A few days later, on the mat, she finds her way back to the podium: a first-round win by ippon in a little over a minute against Turkey's Dondu Yesilyurt, the 2022 world champion and 2023 European champion; a semifinal loss by waza-ari to Uzbekistan's Kumushkhon Khodjaeva, the tournament's top seed; then that bronze snatched in five seconds against Lucía da Silva. It is the 38th medal for the Spanish delegation at these Games.

"Ha sido la competición con más flow que he hecho nunca," she says in the moments after her bronze-medal bout — literally, the most fluid competition of her whole career. Asked about a possible appearance at the Los Angeles Games in 2028, she refuses to settle the question: "Me acabará retirando la vida, pero ahora no voy a decir nada de retirarme" — life will end up retiring her one day, but for now she is saying nothing about retiring. She dedicates her medal to her husband, at her side throughout her sporting career.

A sixth-dan black belt, Marta Arce now divides her time between physiotherapy — the profession she has practiced since earning her degree, which was also recognized in Italy, where she has worked — speaking engagements, and her role as a trainer in the ONCE Foundation's Trainers Paralímpicos program, where she puts her own experience at the service of other people with disabilities. She has also completed a master's degree in sports communication and journalism. On her professional website, she sums up her relationship with competition in a single sentence: "Nunca doy nada por sentado" — I never take anything for granted.

Five Paralympic Games in twenty years, four medals, three children raised alongside the mat: Marta Arce's trajectory has been built in successive restarts rather than in a straight line. After Paris, she has closed no doors on what comes next — not even the one to Los Angeles, in 2028.
