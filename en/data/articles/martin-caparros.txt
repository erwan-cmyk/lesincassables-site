In the mid-1970s, in the newsroom of the Argentine daily Noticias, funded by the Peronist Montoneros movement, a teenager serves the coffee and sorts wire copy between desks. Martín Caparrós has just entered the profession through the back door. On the Policiales y Vida Cotidiana desk, he watches and admires his first section editor, Rodolfo Walsh, a writer and journalist who would be murdered by the military dictatorship a few years later. Out of that first job as an errand boy comes a calling that will never leave him: to tell, from the ground, what others would rather not see.

Born on May 29, 1957, in Buenos Aires, Martín Caparrós grows up in an Argentina shot through with political instability. A student at the prestigious Colegio Nacional de Buenos Aires, he commits early to the militant currents of his generation. The 1976 coup forces him into exile: first Paris, where he rents a maid's room and begins a history degree, then Madrid, where he lives until 1983 and works with El País, among others. Those years of exile shape his eye, the eye of a chronicler who will never stop crossing borders.

Back in Buenos Aires, he writes for the daily Tiempo Argentino, before taking over the magazine El Porteño in 1987 and co-founding, with Jorge Lanata, the newspaper Página/12, which would become a landmark of the Argentine press. He then founds the magazine Babel, and from 1991 publishes, in the Página/30 supplement, long travel narratives that earn him the Rey de España journalism prize in 1992. In between, in 1984, his first novel appears, Ansay o los infortunios de la gloria — the start of an already considerable body of work, which today runs to several dozen titles.

Caparrós establishes himself as one of the great figures of the Latin American crónica, the first-person narrative journalism that blends reporting and literature. He publishes La Voluntad (1997, with Eduardo Anguita), a sweeping account of Argentina's militant generation of the 1970s, then the novels Valfierno (2004, Premio Planeta) and Los Living (2011, Premio Herralde). In 2014 comes El Hambre, a vast investigation carried out across several continents and one of his most translated books; in 2021, Ñamérica draws an ambitious portrait of contemporary Latin America.

It is shortly afterward that the first symptoms appear, in his legs at first. The diagnosis of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis comes around 2022. For more than two years, Caparrós confides it only to his partner, the Spanish journalist Marta Nebot. "No quise que los amigos me vierais como un moribundo," he would explain — he did not want his friends to see him as a dying man. But when the disease reaches his arms, in the fall of 2024, concealment becomes untenable. "Iba a salir del armario, quisiera o no quisiera" — I was going to come out of the closet, whether I wanted to or not — he says then in an interview with the Spanish daily La Vanguardia, adding soberly: "La esperanza de vida es de tres a cinco años" — life expectancy is three to five years.

That same month, he publishes Antes que nada (Random House), a voluminous memoir written with no certainty that it would ever appear, retracing his path as a great reporter and novelist while folding in, without pathos, the announcement of his illness. His partner, for her part, publishes an open letter, "Salir del armario," in which she writes: "Lo que más me duele no es la ELA" — what hurts her most is not the ALS itself.

Far from giving up writing, Caparrós publishes again in 2025, La verdadera vida de José Hernández and then the essay Sindiós, and keeps up a regular column in El País. In July of that year, the University of Buenos Aires awards him an honorary doctorate: he enters the lecture hall in a wheelchair to deliver a speech of biting lucidity in which he sums up, in a line that has stayed famous, his relationship with his native country: "Fracasamos la Argentina y yo, en ese orden" — we failed, Argentina and I, in that order.

In early June 2026, at the Madrid Book Fair, he sums up his situation with the same economy of words that made his reputation as a chronicler: "Hago todo en silla de ruedas y con poquita fuerza, pero sigo" — I do everything in a wheelchair and with very little strength, but I keep going. Fifty years after serving the coffee in the Noticias newsroom, Martín Caparrós is still writing.
