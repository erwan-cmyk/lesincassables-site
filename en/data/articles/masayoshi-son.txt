One day in 1981, in a cramped office in Fukuoka, a twenty-four-year-old climbs onto a wooden crate to address his only two employees, two students hired part-time. He announces to them that their fledgling company, no bigger than a back room, will one day become a giant worth trillions of yen. The two employees, aghast, quit within the week, convinced they are dealing with a crank. Forty-five years later, that company has become SoftBank Group, one of the largest technology investment conglomerates in the world, and the man on the crate, Masayoshi Son, one of the boldest — and most closely watched — gamblers in global capitalism.

The son of a Korean family of pig and poultry farmers settled on the island of Kyushu, Son grows up in postwar Japan enduring the second-class status reserved for the zainichi, Koreans born on Japanese soil. He leaves high school at sixteen for California, where he earns the equivalent of an American high school diploma in a matter of weeks, before studying economics at Berkeley. Already an entrepreneur as a student, he sells Sharp a prototype electronic translator for $1.7 million and imports secondhand Japanese arcade machines into the United States. Back in Japan, he founds SoftBank in 1981 to distribute software — with, from day one, ambitions out of all proportion to the company's actual size.

What follows proves him right at first: within two years, the headcount goes from about thirty to one hundred twenty-five employees and revenue climbs from ¥2 billion to ¥4.5 billion. Then, in 1983, the ascent stops dead. Doctors at Toranomon Hospital, in Tokyo, diagnose Son with chronic hepatitis B and speak of a prognosis of three to five years to live. The fact is documented both by Gambling Man, the 2024 biography by Lionel Barber, former editor of the Financial Times, and by the Japanese business press, which was already recounting the episode in the 2010s — well before the book came out.

Married, a young father, at the head of a fast-growing company, Son finds himself hospitalized and partly sidelined from the day-to-day running of SoftBank for several years. Rather than passively rely on the available treatments, he immerses himself, one of his biographers recounts, in the specialized medical literature on hepatology. In the end it is his father who spots, in a newspaper article, the work of a Toranomon hepatologist experimenting with a novel steroid-withdrawal protocol. Son bets on this still-uncertain treatment. He pulls through.

This episode, long kept in the background of his public story, comes at the precise moment when SoftBank could have collapsed for lack of a leader, shortly before some of the most decisive choices of his career. Recovered, Son takes the company back in hand in the late 1980s and lists it on the stock market in 1994. In 1995, he invests in a nascent American search engine, Yahoo, before launching its Japanese version the following year. In 2000, he stakes $20 million on a young Chinese e-commerce platform founded by a former English teacher, Jack Ma: Alibaba. Two decades later, that investment will become one of the most profitable in the history of finance.

The contrarian bets follow one another: the purchase of the carrier Vodafone Japan in 2006, the exclusive deal with Apple to sell the iPhone, then, in 2016, the acquisition of the British chip designer Arm for about $32 billion — successfully taken public in 2023. In 2017, Son launches the Vision Fund, endowed with $100 billion, the largest technology investment fund ever assembled at the time, even at the cost of suffering, with the collapse of WeWork, one of the most talked-about losses in the history of private equity. None of it stops him: since 2025, he has been committing tens of billions of dollars to artificial intelligence, partnered with OpenAI in the Stargate infrastructure project, convinced that this technology will transform the global economy more profoundly than any before it.

Masayoshi Son has never built his persona around the illness that, in 1983, should have carried him off before the decade was out. He rarely speaks of it, and without emphasis when he does. But the episode casts its light, in negative, on everything that followed: a man who had been handed a countdown spent the next four decades investing as though he no longer had one.
