She was twenty-three when she entered a swimming pool intending, for the first time, to swim seriously. In a wheelchair since her teens, she had tried sit-skiing a year earlier; that day, friends who swam relays had drawn her toward the pool. In the water, her body weighed nothing, a feeling of lightness she found nowhere else. She saw in it at once more than a pastime: a goal, and very soon a daily discipline.

Mayumi Narita was born on August 27, 1970, in Kawasaki, in Kanagawa Prefecture. She was thirteen when transverse myelitis attacked her spinal cord and took away the use of her legs. Her junior high school years were marked by some twenty hospital stays, for heart problems and high blood pressure linked to her condition. She nonetheless grew up with no particular connection to sport; ten years went by before that discovery of swimming, and one more year was all she needed to enter her first competition.

The journey home nearly cost her that budding career. In 1994, the car in which she was returning from that event was in an accident: a cervical spine injury was added to her disability, her left arm lost mobility, and her body, from then on, struggled to regulate its own temperature. Five months in the hospital followed, with no certainty that she would ever return to the pool. Two years later, though, she was on the deck of the Olympic pool in Atlanta for her first Paralympic Games: two gold medals, two silver, one bronze.

What followed was a steady ascent, across a range of events in the S4 and S5 classifications: freestyle, backstroke, breaststroke, individual medley, with a particular fondness for the 50 meters freestyle. In Sydney, in 2000, she collected six titles and set five world records; the Japanese team entrusted her with the national flag at the closing ceremony. Four years later, in Athens, she brought her haul to eight medals at that single edition, a national record, seven of them gold, along with six world records and seven Paralympic records. In 2005, the International Paralympic Committee named her best female athlete of the year. In a Japan where para sport remained little seen at the time, she helped bring it public recognition.

The Beijing Games, in 2008, were more modest and closed a run of four consecutive editions crowned with medals: in all, 15 Paralympic titles and 20 podium finishes, a tally that to this day ranks her twelfth in the world among the athletes with the most gold medals in the history of the Games. She then withdrew from competition without leaving the world of sport or that of the Games: a contract employee of the broadcaster Nippon Television, a research associate at an institute of Nihon University, she joined the bid committee and then the organizing committee of the Tokyo Games, where she served as vice-chair of the athletes' commission.

The retirement did not last. Carried by the approach of those Tokyo Games she had helped bring to her country, and convinced that determination mattered more than age, she dove back into the big pool at forty-six, in Rio, in 2016, then one last time in Tokyo, in 2021, at fifty-one. Between her debut in Atlanta and that final lap, twenty-five years and six editions of the Paralympic Games had gone by, a rare span in elite sport, para or otherwise. In Japan, where she was nicknamed the queen of the water, she became after that final retirement a figure of mentorship: talks in schools, outreach to the youngest generations, plans to stay active as a coach at the Yokohama Sakura Swimming School.

Mayumi Narita died on September 5, 2025, in Kawasaki, the city where she had been born fifty-five years earlier, from an illness that several Japanese and international media outlets identified as bile duct cancer. The Japanese para swimming federation, in announcing her death, paid tribute to a legendary figure of the sport. Her funeral was held in mid-September, in that same city where she had grown up in a wheelchair before entering, one day at twenty-three, a swimming pool she never really left.
