By the edge of a swimming pool in Georgia, in the mid-2000s, Teresa Coan takes the flotation vest off her daughter and watches her swim alone toward the deep end. McKenzie is five. Her two brothers, Grant and Eli, already train with the local Habersham Rapids team, which she wants to join. In the water, she discovers a body that no longer needs protecting with every move. "I didn't feel different from them," she would say years later. "I didn't have to worry about my wheelchair."

McKenzie Coan is born on June 14, 1996, in Toccoa, Georgia, with a femur already fractured. The diagnosis comes almost immediately: osteogenesis imperfecta, a spontaneous genetic mutation with no family history, which weakens collagen and makes bones brittle. Doctors warn Marc and Teresa Coan that their daughter will likely never walk, may not sit up on her own, may not even speak. The couple refuses to raise their daughter by that prognosis. What follows are hospital stays every three months, half a dozen body casts, seven to eight surgeries, and more than a hundred fractures throughout her childhood.

The pool quickly becomes a world of its own. Around age seven or eight, McKenzie is recruited by BlazeSports Georgia, a nonprofit that introduces her to the Paralympic Games. She starts following the careers of swimmers like Jessica Long and Erin Popovich and decides she'll be one of them. At eleven, she's already the youngest competitor at the U.S. national trials — but it will take five more years before she makes a Paralympic team.

In June 2012, on her sixteenth birthday, she secures qualification for the London Games, beating the required time in the 400m freestyle by eleven hundredths of a second. On August 31, she finishes sixth in the S8 final. Not a podium, but a start.

She then enrolls at Loyola University Maryland, where she swims NCAA Division I alongside able-bodied athletes under coach Brian Loeffler, while earning a degree in political science. Reclassified into the S7 category, she reaches her first peak at the Rio Games in 2016: three individual gold medals in the 50m, 100m, and 400m freestyle, a Paralympic record in the 50m, and a silver medal in the 4x100m relay. But the image that lingers from Rio isn't an official medal ceremony. Poolside, Coan takes off one of her gold medals and places it around the neck of an eight-year-old girl who came to watch her swim — a girl who, like her, has osteogenesis imperfecta. "Rio changed a lot of things for me," she would later say.

The following years confirm her dominance over the distance. World champion in the 50m, 100m, and 400m freestyle in Mexico City in 2017, she breaks two world records in 2018: the 800m freestyle S7 record on June 8 in Berlin, improving the previous mark by thirty-seven seconds, then the 1500m freestyle S7 record at the U.S. national championships in Arizona. In 2019, in London, she successfully defends her world titles in the 100m and 400m.

The pandemic pushes the Tokyo Games back a year; Coan trains in a pool built in her parents' garage before joining the training center in Colorado Springs. On August 29, 2021, she retains her 400m freestyle S7 title in 5:05.84. "It's been five long years, with quite a few hard things in them," she says after the race. Two days later, she adds a silver medal in the 100m freestyle, behind Italy's Giulia Terzi. Back home, she publishes her autobiography, Breaking Free, and founds Kenzie Kares, which organizes visits for hospitalized children with rare bone conditions.

The most unexpected obstacle of her career, though, has nothing to do with her bones. In June 2023, a poorly treated ear, nose, and throat infection develops into facial paralysis on her left side: she can't close her eyelid, can't wear swim goggles, can't even keep water out of her mouth on every lap. The diagnosis: Bell's palsy. Her coach tells her something that convinces her to skip that year's World Championships: "you're a human being before you're a swimmer." Still, she's back in a pool two days later, cycling through acupuncture and rehab three times a week, and returns to competition a few months later at the Para Pan American Games in Santiago.

In Paris, in September 2024, she competes in her fourth Paralympic Games. In the 400m freestyle, her signature event since Rio, she finishes second behind her own teammate Morgan Stickney, who sets a new world record. It's her seventh career Paralympic medal, after four golds and two silvers won in Rio and Tokyo — earned at the end of a year when she wasn't even sure she'd make it to the start line.

Coan has since begun law school, intending to work on disability and equality issues in American collegiate sport. She doesn't rule out a fifth Games, in Los Angeles in 2028. Twenty-five years after a Georgia swimming pool gave her the feeling of a body without limits, she keeps telling that story — in the hospitals she visits, on the stages where she speaks, and soon, perhaps, in courtrooms.
