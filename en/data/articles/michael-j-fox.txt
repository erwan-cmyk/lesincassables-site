On June 29, 2024, on the Pyramid Stage at Glastonbury Festival in England, Chris Martin stops Coldplay's set to introduce a guest. A man walks out, an electric guitar slung over his shoulder, his movements stiffer than in his glory days. In front of tens of thousands of spectators, he plays "Humankind" and then "Fix You" alongside the band. "Back to the Future is the main reason we became a band," Chris Martin tells the crowd before introducing him. The man on stage, past sixty, is Michael J. Fox — the actor who played Marty McFly forty years earlier, and who has lived with Parkinson's disease for more than three decades.

Michael Andrew Fox is born on June 9, 1961, in Edmonton, Alberta, Canada. The role that makes him a household name comes in the 1980s: from 1982 to 1989, he plays Alex P. Keaton, a wisecracking young conservative, in the NBC sitcom Family Ties — a performance that earns him three consecutive Emmy Awards, in 1986, 1987, and 1988, followed by a Golden Globe in 1989.

In the meantime, in 1985, he becomes Marty McFly in Back to the Future, alongside Christopher Lloyd. The film spends eight straight weeks atop the American box office and earns more than $380 million worldwide; two sequels, in 1989 and 1990, extend the success. Before he even turns thirty, Michael J. Fox is one of the biggest stars in American popular cinema.

While filming Doc Hollywood, in early 1991, he notices a tremor in his little finger and a persistent ache in his shoulder. The diagnosis comes that same year: early-onset Parkinson's disease, a form that usually affects much older patients. Michael J. Fox is on the edge of his thirties at the time; doctors suggest he has only a few years before the disease keeps him from working.

He chooses to keep it quiet. For seven years, he keeps making films and shows without revealing anything, going on to land the role of Mike Flaherty, deputy mayor of New York, in the series Spin City in 1996 — another hit that earns him an Emmy Award in 2000 and three consecutive Golden Globes between 1998 and 2000. But by the third season, hiding the tremors becomes untenable. In 1998, he publicly reveals his diagnosis to the press.

The following year, he takes his story to the U.S. Congress. In 1999, invited to testify before the Senate Appropriations subcommittee to advocate for Parkinson's research funding, he deliberately chooses not to take his medication before the hearing. "I had made a deliberate choice to appear before the subcommittee without medication," he writes in his memoir Lucky Man, published in 2002. "It seemed to me that this occasion demanded that my testimony about the effects of the disease, and the urgency we as a community were feeling, be seen as well as heard."

In 2000, as his symptoms progress, he leaves Spin City and steps back from acting full-time. That same year, with Deborah W. Brooks, he founds the Michael J. Fox Foundation for Parkinson's Research, with a stated goal: accelerate research until his own existence becomes unnecessary. "We're in business to go out of business," he later sums up on the foundation's website.

The bet pays off. According to Biography.com, the foundation becomes the world's largest private funder of research into Parkinson's treatments, having raised close to two billion dollars since its founding. In 2010, it launches the Parkinson's Progression Markers Initiative, a cohort study whose data has since been downloaded more than 1.7 million times by researchers, followed in 2017 by Fox Insight, a participatory research platform that now counts more than 48,000 volunteers.

On July 16, 1988, Michael J. Fox marries actress Tracy Pollan; the couple will have four children, one son and three daughters. Over the years he publishes several bestselling memoirs, including Lucky Man in 2002 and No Time Like the Future in 2020, and receives the Jean Hersholt Humanitarian Award at the Academy's Governors Awards in 2022 for his fight against Parkinson's disease.

He never sugarcoats the reality of the illness. "I'm not gonna lie. It's getting harder. Every day it's tougher," he tells journalist Jane Pauley on CBS Sunday Morning in 2023, also describing spinal surgery and several fall-related fractures. That same year sees the release of Still: A Michael J. Fox Movie, a documentary directed by Davis Guggenheim for Apple TV+, which wins four Emmy Awards.

Since then, he hasn't stopped showing up: on stage at Glastonbury in 2024, then on screen in 2025 in the series Shrinking. In October 2025, he publishes a new book, Future Boy, looking back on forty years of Back to the Future. Thirty-five years after a tremor in his little finger on a film set, Michael J. Fox keeps finding, in his own way, a way to keep pace.
