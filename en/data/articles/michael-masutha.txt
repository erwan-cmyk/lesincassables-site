On May 26, 2014, in Pretoria, Michael Masutha is sworn in as Minister of Justice and Correctional Services of the Republic of South Africa. In the room where President Jacob Zuma presents his new government, few people dwell on a detail that, elsewhere, would have made headlines: this 49-year-old man who has just inherited one of the heaviest portfolios in the state has been blind since birth. For Masutha, that day does not mark a victory over a disability. It is the culmination of a quarter century of law, advocacy work and patience.

Tshililo Michael Masutha is born in 1965 in Valdezia, a small village in the northern Transvaal — today's Limpopo province — in a South Africa still locked down by apartheid. He comes into the world with a visual impairment; the available sources do not allow its precise medical cause to be established. What is documented, on the other hand, is the trajectory he builds despite, and through, that blindness, in a country where being Black and disabled under the regime of racial segregation already amounted to a double obstacle.

Masutha studies law and becomes a lawyer specializing in human rights, at a time when that specialty is never neutral in South Africa. But it is outside the courtroom that he lays, in 1989, the most significant stone of his path: he founds the Northern Transvaal Association for the Blind. He is 24 at the time. Ten years before entering politics, Masutha thus chooses to build an institution rather than wait for one to be entrusted to him.

That decade of advocacy bears fruit in 1999, when Masutha is elected to the South African Parliament under the banner of the African National Congress (ANC), Nelson Mandela's party, which has become the country's dominant force five years after the end of apartheid. He sits there for more than a decade, until his career gets its first acceleration: in July 2013, he is appointed Deputy Minister of Science and Technology.

The real turning point, then, comes in May 2014. By appointing him to Justice, Zuma makes Masutha the second minister with a disability in his cabinet — a fact rare enough at the time to be noted by the South African press. The portfolio is an unusual pairing: Masutha must oversee both the country's judicial system and its entire prison administration, two worlds whose logics are largely foreign to each other, brought together in a single ministry.

The five years that follow are no smooth ride. Masutha has to contend with a prosecuting body, the National Prosecuting Authority, over which he has only limited authority, while the institution goes through a period of severe internal turbulence. In 2018, at the height of the storm around the country's chief prosecutor, Shaun Abrahams, accused by part of the political class and civil society of selective prosecutions, Masutha publicly states that he retains confidence in him, while denying that Jacob Zuma ever asked him to act improperly in the matter. Those years in power unfold as the ANC is shaken by sweeping accusations of state capture, without any inquiry ever implicating Masutha's personal conduct in those scandals.

The prisons side of his job exposes him just as much. In 2015, he authorizes, on medical grounds, the parole of Clive Derby-Lewis, a former far-right member of parliament convicted of the 1993 assassination of the communist leader and anti-apartheid figure Chris Hani. The decision sparks fierce controversy, and the South African High Court rebukes his handling of the matter — a reminder that the job of justice minister, in post-apartheid South Africa, also means arbitrating the still-open wounds of the country's history.

Masutha leaves the government in May 2019, when Cyril Ramaphosa, now president, forms his own team after the general elections, bringing to a close five years spent at the head of two of the country's most sensitive administrations.

What history retains of Michael Masutha is not the way he supposedly "overcame" his blindness — a reading that would reduce an entire career to a single trait. It is rather the constancy of a method: build an institution first, before claiming power, then exercise that power, once obtained, with the same instruments as anyone else — the law, analysis, arbitration. Born blind in a village in Limpopo under apartheid, he ran one of the most strategic ministries of Africa's most industrialized country for five years. Few trajectories illustrate so soberly that a disability from birth says nothing about the heights a life can reach.
