On the evening of November 18, 1997, 6.9 million viewers turn on TF1 to discover "Le Miroir aux enfants," the first episode of a series that no one, at the time, imagines will run for close to thirty years. In the title role, a 40-year-old actress who came up through the Lyon café-théâtre scene plays Joséphine Delamarre, a guardian angel sent to rescue souls in distress. The audience share climbs to 28.4 percent. That evening, without knowing it yet, Mimie Mathy has just met the role of her life.

Michèle Mathy — Mimie would be a nickname first, then a stage name — is born on July 8, 1957, in the 7th arrondissement of Lyon. Her father, Marcel, head of the transport department at a Lyon company and an amateur saxophonist in his spare time, and her mother, Roberte, raise three daughters: Michèle is the eldest, ahead of Frédérique and Marie. She is born with achondroplasia, a form of dwarfism that will set her adult height at 1.32 meters — one fact of her childhood among others, in a family that encourages her without ever making it a subject apart.

With a baccalauréat in série B, she begins a degree in economics at the university in Lyon that she will never finish. At 18, she becomes an activity leader at a Villages Vacances Familles holiday center: it is there that she is nicknamed "Mimie."

In 1979, she is picked by the singer Michel Fugain for his musical-theater workshop at the Victorine studios in Nice. "I started in 1979," she would sum it up decades later on Sud Radio. The workshop takes her to Paris, where in 1981 she appears on the TF1 program Fugues à Fugain. In 1982, she writes and performs her first one-woman show, Elle voit des géants partout, at the Théâtre du Point-Virgule, before joining the troupe of Le Petit Théâtre de Bouvard on Antenne 2, where she works alongside Muriel Robin, Michèle Bernier and Isabelle de Botton.

With the last two, she forms the trio Les Filles, which tours for seven years, until 1993, before a cumulative audience estimated at between 10 and 15 million, with shows such as Existe en trois tailles (1988-1990) and then Le Gros N'avion, created at the Michodière in 1991.

The wider television audience discovers her with the TV movies Une nounou pas comme les autres (1994) and Une nana pas comme les autres (1995), each of which draws more than 11 million viewers. But it is Joséphine, ange gardien, launched in 1997, that establishes her for good in the broadcasting landscape: more than a hundred episodes filmed in close to thirty years, three 7 d'Or awards for best actress (1998, 2000, 2003), and a character she goes on writing herself. "I'd rather do 6 million on a night on TV" than movies, she explained on Sud Radio, owning a career choice uncommon for an actress of her generation.

Her personal life turns on April 22, 2003, in Saint-Brieuc, where she brings a spectator up on stage for a sketch. Watching him come toward her, she thinks: "That one must be either gay or a priest," she would tell Paris Match. That man, Benoist Gérard, a cook and restaurateur, becomes her husband on August 27, 2005, in Neuilly. Mimie Mathy becomes stepmother to his four children, then step-grandmother to a granddaughter, Jade.

Her charitable commitments keep pace with her fame: she has sung with the Enfoirés since 1994, is a patron of the association Docteur Clown, and has served as a UNICEF goodwill ambassador in France. In recognition of her career, she is named a Knight of the Legion of Honor on January 1, 2015, then decorated on May 4 of that year at the Pavillon Dauphine, in Paris. Polls by the Journal du Dimanche long ranked her among the French public's favorite personalities.

Close to thirty years after her debut on Joséphine, ange gardien, Mimie Mathy is still filming, even as her mobility has narrowed: she now gets around most often in an electric wheelchair. "I don't walk anymore," she told the Belgian press in late June 2026, specifying that "it's linked to my configuration." She added, without pathos: "Now it's becoming a mechanical difference," before giving notice: "That won't stop me from filming, or writing, or traveling." The series, back on air in late 2025 with new episodes, has written that wheelchair into Joséphine's world. On Sud Radio, she mentions a new project: "It's about the next part of my life."

More than forty years after she walked into the workshop at the Victorine, the former Villages Vacances Familles activity leader turned one of the most watched actresses in France remains faithful to the rule she was already applying in the café-théâtres of the 1980s: stay present, show after show, and never leave the stage.
