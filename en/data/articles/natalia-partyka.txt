At the table in the table tennis club a few hundred meters from her home, in Poland, Natalia Partyka tirelessly returns the balls sent over by her older sister, Sandra. She is seven years old. She has been missing her right hand and forearm since birth, but she has learned to hold her paddle in her left hand with a precision that sharpens match after match. The club is so close to the family home that she goes there almost every day after school. She would later describe, soberly, the luck she sees in it: "I always underline how lucky I was, as the club was very close to my home, so I had favourable conditions to grow from the start."

At the club, she rubs shoulders with more experienced para athletes who draw her toward competition. She would later remember having had, in her words, "great role models": athletes who show her, as she puts it, that "I can do whatever I want and do it well," and that she can aim for the top level like any other player in the club. Born on July 27, 1989, she wins her first international medal as early as 1999, at the world championships in her class. The following year, in Sydney, at 11 she becomes the youngest Paralympic athlete in the history of table tennis. Four years later, in Athens, she wins her first Paralympic singles title — becoming the youngest Paralympic champion in the sport — rounded out by a team silver medal. In 2006, she sweeps three titles at the European Paralympic championships.

It is in Beijing, in 2008, that her trajectory takes on a dimension never seen before. Competing in class 10 — the class that groups, in para table tennis, the standing athletes with the mildest impairment — she retains her Paralympic singles title there, beating China's Fan Lei three sets to none, and adds a team silver medal. But it is another performance that writes her into sporting history: the same year, she becomes the first table tennis player to also compete at the Olympic Games. There she faces, with no adapted class and no adjustment to the rules, the best able-bodied players on the planet, on the international circuit where she is already a regular entrant between Paralympic events. That year, she and South African swimmer Natalie du Toit are the only two Paralympic athletes to also compete at the Beijing Olympics.

She repeats the double in London in 2012: she plays the women's singles at the Olympic Games while retaining, at the Paralympic Games, her singles title — wrested in five tight sets, three to two, from China's Yang Qian, already her great rival. Her gold medal is rounded out by a team bronze, also won at the end of a seesaw match against France (3-2). Two years later, at the world championships in Beijing, the same Yang Qian pushes her to match point without managing to bring her down. Then, in Rio in 2016, the two players meet once again in the singles final: this time, Partyka's victory is clear-cut, three sets to none. It is her fourth consecutive Paralympic singles title, an unbroken reign since 2004, won while also competing, at the Olympic Games, in the team event.

Over these Games, her Paralympic collection grows to six titles, three silver medals and three bronze. But the figures say little about what makes her path singular: year after year, Natalia Partyka runs two careers in parallel that almost nothing in the organization of world sport is designed to let coexist — that of a dominant Paralympic player and that of a competitor on the conventional Olympic circuit, with no intermediate category and no adapted rule between the two.

Of this double sporting life, she speaks with restraint rather than emphasis. Asked what sport has given her, she summed it up: "I have achieved almost everything in my life through sports. I am grateful that I have lived this beautiful adventure for so many years." Far from stopping at Rio, she then continues her career on the international circuit, competing notably at the 2018 European Championships. A quarter of a century after her beginnings on a table a stone's throw from home, Natalia Partyka's story remains one of the few to have abolished, in fact rather than in words, the border between Paralympic and Olympic sport.
