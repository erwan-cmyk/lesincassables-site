On August 30, 1951, a father and son spend the afternoon swimming in the Pigeon River, near Omemee, a small town in Ontario. The next morning, the boy wakes up feverish, with a sharp pain in his shoulder. Within hours, he can barely stand. His parents rush the child to the Hospital for Sick Children in Toronto, where a spinal tap confirms what the family doctor already feared: polio, part of what would remain Ontario's last major epidemic of the disease.

Isolated for several days in the contagious disease ward while his parents are quarantined at home, then back in Omemee for a long and difficult recovery, Neil Young — not yet six — carries away a hazy memory of the episode and a lasting mark: a partial paralysis that never fully resolves, leaving him with a slight limp. "I just remember lying there, partially paralyzed," he would recall decades later in Shakey, the biography written by journalist Jimmy McDonough. That winter, the family heads to Florida to warm up, convinced, like many at the time, that a milder climate would help his recovery.

The small Ontario town nonetheless becomes the backdrop for an entire strand of the work to come: its streets and its river haunt songs written much later, once the child has become one of the most singular musicians of his generation. After his parents divorce in 1960, Neil Young follows his mother to Winnipeg, where he forms his first bands — the Squires, first — before heading to Los Angeles in 1966 behind the wheel of a hearse. It's on Sunset Strip, stuck in traffic, that he runs by chance into Stephen Stills, with whom he forms Buffalo Springfield that same year.

It's also during these early years, as the band's star is rising, that his first seizures occur. At a Teen Fair in Hollywood in 1967, he collapses amid the crowd's commotion. "I felt kind of sick to my stomach, and then I started to feel all weird and echoey, and then I fell down," he would later recount. What doctors eventually identify as epilepsy accompanies him on every stage he plays afterward. Sensitive to the powerful lighting used at concerts, Young learns to recognize the warning signs of a seizure and to leave the stage in time; he has said he was never struck by a seizure during an actual performance. In his own words, the seizures grew rarer over the years, without him ever settling on a definitive medical explanation.

Neither polio nor epilepsy slows his trajectory. Buffalo Springfield breaks up in 1968, but Young has already launched a solo career and formed Crazy Horse, the raw guitar band he will record with for more than half a century. After the Gold Rush (1970) and then Harvest (1972) — carried by the hit "Heart of Gold," a U.S. number one — cement his reputation for good, as does his time with Crosby, Stills, Nash & Young. In 1979, the album Rust Never Sleeps and its now-iconic line, "it's better to burn out than to fade away," sum up a permanent embrace of risk that never leaves him. Two decades later, a new generation of rock musicians — Nirvana, Pearl Jam, Sonic Youth — claims him as an influence; the music press nicknames him the "Godfather of Grunge."

That influence earns him a double induction into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame, in 1995 as a solo artist and again in 1997 with Buffalo Springfield. He's made an Officer of the Order of Canada in 2010. In 1985, he co-founds Farm Aid with Willie Nelson and John Mellencamp, a series of benefit concerts supporting family farms, still active forty years later. His activism, environmental as much as political, continues through albums like Greendale (2003) and Living with War (2006), and through repeated public stands, including against the use of his music in political campaigns.

At eighty, Neil Young keeps touring and recording. In 2025, he forms a new band, the Chrome Hearts, releases the album Talkin to the Trees, and headlines the Glastonbury Festival. On stage as in the studio, the man who grew up with one leg weaker than the other, and seizures he learned to feel coming, has never stopped moving forward — not always in a straight line, perhaps, but never at a standstill.
