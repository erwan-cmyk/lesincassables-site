Melbourne, December 1982. In the days after giving birth, Dushka Vujicic cannot bring herself to hold her son. It will take her four months. Doctors have no explanation: the baby born to this couple of Serbian immigrants, Boris and Dushka, has tetra-amelia syndrome, a malformation so rare that no family history helps explain it. Boris, of Serbian Orthodox origin but converted to Protestantism and now a pastor, goes through a crisis of faith. The couple nonetheless chooses to keep and raise this child, whom they name Nicholas.

In the Victoria of the early 1980s, a law still bars children with disabilities from mainstream schools. When the legislation changes, Nick becomes one of the first such students admitted into the standard Australian school system. He gets around using a single small foot, with two toes that surgery has separated so they can work like fingers — he will later call it, with humor, his "chicken drumstick." That foot lets him write, type, swim, and later surf and skateboard. But at school, it mostly draws stares and mockery.

From eight to twelve, Nick goes through severe depression. At ten, he attempts to drown himself in the family bathtub. Years later, in a television interview, he explains why he chose to stay that day: from eight to twelve, he says, he was depressed, and at ten he tried to drown himself — the only reason he decided to stay was that he didn't want to leave that pain to his parents, who had only ever loved him and didn't deserve it.

The turning point comes gradually, not as a sudden miracle. His mother shows him a newspaper article one day about a severely disabled man who still managed to inspire others. Around fifteen, reading a passage from the Gospel of John, where Jesus refuses to blame a man's blindness from birth on his parents' sin, permanently shifts his question: no longer "why me," but "what for." At seventeen, he speaks in public for the first time, in front of a handful of friends from his prayer group. Two years later, these talks become regular. In the meantime, out of caution as much as discipline, he earns a Bachelor of Commerce at Griffith University at twenty-one, with a double major in accounting and financial planning — a backup plan he will never use.

On January 1, 2005, he founds Life Without Limbs, a nonprofit set up to organize his speaking engagements; the first event is held two months later. In 2007, he moves to California to grow it from the United States. A sprawling career follows: more than sixty countries visited across six continents, several thousand talks, several million people met face to face, according to his organization's own count. In 2013, at Hanoi's My Dinh Stadium, 25,000 people gather to hear him speak — an event funded by the Vietnamese industrial group Hoa Sen and broadcast to millions of viewers by state television. Three years earlier, in Oregon, he had set a more unexpected world record, certified by Guinness World Records: 1,749 hugs given in sixty minutes.

In 2009, The Butterfly Circus is released, a short film in which he plays the lead role; his performance earns him the Best Actor award at Method Fest 2010. That same year, he publishes Life Without Limits: Inspiration for a Ridiculously Good Life with Random House, a bestseller translated into some thirty languages. In 2013, Oprah Winfrey invites him onto her show Lifeclass, alongside pastor Rick Warren: the clip, posted online, passes a million views and introduces Nick Vujicic to audiences well beyond the religious circles where his career began.

His personal life follows its own, quieter course. On February 12, 2012, he marries Kanae Miyahara. The couple has four children between 2013 and 2017 — two sons, Kiyoshi and Dejan, then twin daughters, Ellie Laurel and Olivia Lei.

He still says it today, on the stages he continues to tour more than four decades after his birth: that he thanks God for not answering his prayer, the one he made at eight years old, begging to be given arms and legs. It isn't a rhetorical flourish. It's the question he leaves, quietly, for anyone listening: what would you do with an answered prayer.
