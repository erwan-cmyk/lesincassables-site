In the long jump pit at Tokyo's Olympic Stadium, on August 28, 2021, Ntando Mahlangu begins his run-up on his carbon-fiber blade and takes off. The measurement reads 7.17 meters: a new world record in the T63 category, and a gold medal for the South African athlete, barely nineteen. Six days later, on September 3, he adds gold in the 200 meters T61, in 23.59 seconds — a time slower, though, than his own world record over the distance, set a few months earlier, in April 2021 in Gqeberha: 22.94 seconds, at the South African national para athletics championships.

Ntando Mahlangu is born on January 26, 2002, in Mpumalanga province, in the northeast of South Africa, near KwaMhlanga, in the former KwaNdebele region, about an hour's drive from Pretoria. He comes into the world with bilateral fibular hemimelia: a congenital condition that leaves him without a fibula in either leg and keeps them from developing normally below the knee. He grows up with his grandmother and spends his first decade in a wheelchair.

In 2012, at ten, his legs are amputated at the knee. That same year, thanks to the South African organization Jumping Kids, he receives his first prosthetics at an event held with Avis South Africa. Five years later, he sums up that turning point, on the International Paralympic Committee's website, in a sentence that never once asks for pity: "In 2012 my life changed – I'm not saying my life changed because I was not disabled anymore, but my life changed because I was tall enough to look at someone in the eye and say hello to them; those small things."

It is those prosthetics that, very quickly, get him running. Barely four years after his amputation, in 2016, he competes at fourteen in his first Paralympic Games, in Rio de Janeiro, and takes silver in the 200 meters T42, with an African record. The following year, he confirms it with another silver medal at the World Championships in London. In 2019, in Dubai, reclassified into the T61 (200 m) and T63 (long jump) categories, he wins his first world title over 200 meters and finishes fourth in the long jump — the event in which he will break out two years later in Tokyo.

His philosophy of running he states plainly to the South African daily Daily Maverick, in 2023: "When I started running, I was running to feel the wind. I was running for the fun of it. It was never for the glamour and all those other things that come with it." Already in 2020, he was among the nine athletes followed by the Netflix documentary Rising Phoenix, about the Paralympic movement.

In late 2023, Ntando Mahlangu joins Loughborough University, in England, to train under new coaches with Paris 2024 in view. With the 200 m T61 gone from the Games program, he turns all his efforts to the long jump, with the declared ambition of defending his title: "Paris is a funny one as we won't have the 200m event there. Now we're only focusing on the long jump so we're changing a lot of things," he says at the time, quoted by the university.

But Paris 2024 will be played out without him. In late August 2024, a few days before the Games open, ESPN Africa reports that Ntando Mahlangu has just been involved in a road accident that left him with a fractured neck, and that he will not be able to defend his title. The two-time Paralympic champion, presented a few months earlier as one of the faces of South African para athletics, appears in none of the results of the South African delegation at those Games: his teammates compete without him.

Still: since adolescence, alongside his career, Ntando Mahlangu has given part of his time to supporting other child amputees, marked as he was by the way others look at them. From the Tokyo long jump pit to Loughborough University, the story he keeps writing has never been that of a missing fibula, but that of a kid from Mpumalanga who became, jump after jump and stride after stride, one of the fastest sprinters and jumpers in world Paralympic sport.
