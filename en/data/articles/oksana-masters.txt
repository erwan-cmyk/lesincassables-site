Milano Cortina, March 7, 2026. At the finish of the para biathlon sitting sprint, Oksana Masters raises her arms, in shock: she has just defended her Paralympic title and, in doing so, collected the twentieth Paralympic medal of her career. "Oh my gosh, my emotions are just pure shock. I did not expect this," she tells the reporters on hand, still out of breath. She is 36. In the days that follow, at Milano Cortina, she adds four more, bringing her total to twenty-four Paralympic medals — thirteen gold, seven silver, four bronze — gathered in four different sports over fourteen years of competition. She becomes, along the way, the most decorated winter Paralympian in Team USA history.

Oksana Masters is born on June 19, 1989, in Khmelnytskyi, Ukraine, under the name Oksana Alexandrovna Bondarchuk. She has bilateral tibial hemimelia — the absence, on both sides, of the tibia, the weight-bearing bone of the leg — which leaves her left leg shorter than her right and her left knee malformed. She is also born with six toes on each foot and webbed fingers, without thumbs. She comes into the world three years after the Chernobyl disaster, in a part of Ukraine that received its radioactive fallout — a coincidence that the press, and Oksana Masters herself, widely associate with her malformations, though no published genetic test has ever established that cause-and-effect link. "Possibly because of the Chernobyl nuclear accident, I was born with a long list of radiation-induced defects," she would later write, with that same caution, in a piece published by The Players' Tribune.

Abandoned at birth, she spends seven years in three different Ukrainian orphanages, marked by hunger and mistreatment — at seven, she is less than a meter tall and weighs about fifteen kilos. Gay Masters, an American professor specializing in communication disorders, spots her in an adoption agency photograph and waits two years before she can bring her to the United States, in January 1997. The little girl recognizes her at once: "I know you. You're my Mom. I have your picture, see?" she says at their meeting, as Oksana Masters would later recount. In the United States, two amputations follow: the left leg, above the knee, at 9; the right, at the same level, at 14. In between, the family settles in Louisville, Kentucky, where Oksana discovers adaptive rowing at 13.

She finds her place there immediately. In 2012, in London, she and her teammate Rob Jones take bronze in the mixed double sculls, Trunk & Arms category — her first Paralympic medal, at her first Games. A back injury, in 2013, pushes her out of elite rowing: she turns instead to cross-country skiing and biathlon, which she competes in sitting. In Sochi, in 2014, she comes home with a silver and a bronze. Four years later, in Pyeongchang, she breaks out: two golds, two silvers, a bronze. She then adds handcycling to her repertoire, winning gold in road cycling at the Tokyo Games (2021) and then in Paris (2024), while continuing to race in winter: more golds and silvers in Beijing in 2022, before the five additional medals of Milano Cortina 2026.

Four sports, eight consecutive Paralympic Games since 2012, twenty-four medals: few athletes, disabled or not, can show a record of such consistency. Asked one day about wounded Ukrainian soldiers she had met, she had summed up her own philosophy in a sentence, reported by the American public radio station WBUR: "It's not about what you don't have. It's just about adjusting to your new normal."

"I have a very strong 'why,' where every single stroke for me counts. It's for every Paralympian, every athlete with a disability that was overlooked," she explained to the International Paralympic Committee's team after her medal of March 7, 2026.

Masters lives today in Louisville, where she keeps training between seasons, and shares her life with para athlete Aaron Pike, himself a multiple Paralympian. In 2022, she publishes her memoir, The Hard Parts, with journalist Cassidy Randall, looking back across the whole of that journey — orphanage, amputations, doubt, victories. "To look back now and it's my eighth games and I have 24 medals that I've brought home for Team USA … It's just incredible," she said again as she came off the biathlon course at Milano Cortina, in March 2026 — already, by then, thinking about what comes next.
