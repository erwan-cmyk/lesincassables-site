From Kharkiv, in the early 1930s, a young woman corresponds with the writer Maxim Gorky. She describes to him how she imagines the colors she has never seen, the sounds she no longer hears, the way her hands rebuild, object by object, a world to which hearing and sight no longer give her direct access. Gorky encourages her to keep up this work of painstaking observation. Nothing in this exchange of letters is enough to measure what Olga Skorokhodova had to overcome in order to write it: deaf and blind since the age of five, she had, as a child, to relearn everything, down to language itself.

Olga Skorokhodova is born on May 24, 1911, in Bilozerka, a village in Kherson oblast that then belongs to the Russian Empire and lies today in Ukraine. Her family is poor: her father is mobilized in 1914 and never returns from the front, and her mother works as a domestic servant to raise her daughter alone. The little girl's entire scientific career, for its part, will later unfold in the Soviet Union, between Kharkiv and Moscow.

At five, meningitis strikes her and gradually takes away her hearing, then her sight. Within a few years, the child loses all direct auditory and visual access to the world around her. Her mother dies in 1922; Olga is then placed in a school for the blind in Odesa, where she spends three difficult years. Her classmates, blind but hearing, have no way of communicating with a child who has become deaf and blind: it is the isolation, more than the loss of the senses itself, that marks this period of her life most harshly.

The turning point comes in 1925. She is admitted to the school-clinic for deafblind children in Kharkiv, run by the educator Ivan Sokolyansky. Under his guidance, she relearns to speak thanks to dactylology, a manual alphabet traced with the fingertips into the palm of the hand, and she learns braille. She also develops a fine tactile memory, learning to recognize those close to her by touch and to find her way alone through space, without a cane and without sight. Sokolyansky encourages her to keep a notebook in which she records, day after day, what she perceives of the world and how she pictures it without seeing or hearing it. This notebook, kept for years, will become the matrix of all her scientific work to come.

She lives through World War II in Kharkiv before settling in Moscow in the mid-1940s. In 1947, she publishes her first book, How I Perceive the World, drawn from her observation notebooks. The work receives the K. D. Ushinsky Prize, one of the Soviet literary and scientific distinctions. The following year, in 1948, she joins the Institute for the Handicapped of the Soviet Academy of Pedagogical Sciences as a researcher.

There she becomes, according to several biographers, the only deafblind researcher in the world conducting scientific work on the education and perception of children with the same double sensory deprivation as her own. Rather than remaining merely the subject of other scientists' observation, the position usually reserved for deafblind people of her era, she occupies the opposite position: she observes and theorizes her own condition, with the rigor of a researcher and the precision of a hand that has learned to read the world differently. This methodological choice, making her own perception the very object of her science, precedes by several decades the recognition of this field of research in the West.

In 1954 a second work appears, How I Perceive and Imagine the World, which extends and refines her initial observations. In 1961, she defends a thesis and obtains the degree of Candidate of Sciences, the Soviet equivalent of a doctorate. In 1972, she publishes a third and final book, How I Perceive, Imagine and Understand the World Around Me, the sum of twenty-five years of notes and research. Two years later, she receives the Order of the Red Banner of Labour. Her work also begins to circulate outside the USSR: in March 1974, the UNESCO Courier devotes an article to her work on her own perception of the world.

Olga Skorokhodova remains a senior researcher at the institute until her death, on May 7, 1982, at the age of seventy, after more than thirty years spent describing, from the inside, an experience that Western research would not begin to document seriously until much later. In 2016, for the 105th anniversary of her birth, Google devotes a doodle to her, shown in several countries: a belated tribute to a body of work that remained largely unknown outside specialist circles, but which was the first to open a path that other deafblind researchers would follow after her.
