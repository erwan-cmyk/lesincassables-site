On October 8, 2018, five French telecom operators — Orange, SFR, Bouygues Telecom, La Poste Mobile, and BTBD — bring into service France's first national telephone relay center, a system that allows any deaf or hard-of-hearing person to make an ordinary phone call, assisted in real time by a sign language interpreter, a French cued speech coder, or a written transcription, joined in 2021 by a braille-compatible mode. Three years after its launch, according to the Fédération française des télécoms, the service will have carried more than 450,000 calls and 1.8 million minutes, with 30% growth in the third year alone and a rating of 4.8 out of 5 on the App Store. The company charged with operating it is called RogerVoice. Its founder, Olivier Jeannel, has been profoundly deaf since the age of two.

Born in Los Angeles to a French expatriate family, Olivier Jeannel grows up in California, between hearing aids and speech therapy sessions, with a dual anchoring — French culture at home, American beach and campus life day to day — that makes him bilingual from childhood. He then studies at the University of California, Berkeley, one of the pioneering American universities in the inclusion of students with disabilities, where the accommodations he receives seem to him a matter of course: "I didn't realize it was exceptional," he will later tell the website French Morning. The contrast becomes stark when he crosses the Atlantic to continue his studies at Sciences Po, in Paris: there he finds a disability support system far less structured than California's, and, unable to find one, creates a resource page himself for the school's students with disabilities.

After Sciences Po, Olivier Jeannel joins the telecom operator Orange, where he works as an executive for about eight years. It is there, at the very heart of the telephone industry, that he goes on living every day with what his degrees have not solved: the impossibility of making a simple phone call on his own. In 2011, the rise of consumer automatic speech recognition technologies gives him the intuition that a technical solution now exists to transcribe a phone conversation in real time, the way a film is subtitled.

He then lays the groundwork for RogerVoice. A crowdfunding campaign on Kickstarter raises about €35,000; the MakeSense accelerator and several interest-free honor loans shore up the first months; Bastien Le Rest joins the project as chief technology officer and co-founder. The app, which turns a phone conversation into instant subtitles, officially launches in 2015. In September 2016, RogerVoice raises €500,000 from a pool of investors — including Femmes Business Angels, Business Angels des Grandes Écoles, Sophia Business Angels, 1001Pact, Comptoir de l'Innovation, and Aviva Impact Investing — betting on a global market then estimated at some 70 million deaf people, while the app claims more than 10,000 downloads and availability in about twenty languages. The funds go toward hiring and forging partnerships with major accounts — among them Orange, the SNCF, Aéroports de Paris, GRDF, and Mondial Assistance — to make their customer services accessible.

The next turning point plays out on regulatory ground. Article 105 of the 2016 Digital Republic Act requires operators to make their services accessible to deaf and hard-of-hearing people: it is this law that leads, in 2018, to the creation of the telephone relay center entrusted to RogerVoice. The system expands in October 2021, with the free monthly allowance raised to three hours and extended hours in the evening and on Saturday mornings. Olivier Jeannel, who still runs the company, responds: "Extending the hours and the allowance is a logical next step, one that we at RogerVoice are the first to champion." In the same statement, he points to a shortage of qualified interpreters in France and to the wait for a development plan for that profession, provided for by law but still postponed — a fight he continues to wage publicly, beyond the product he created.

Now a mission-driven company under French law, RogerVoice employs more than 120 people today and offers its service, according to the company, in around a hundred languages and some fifty countries. The young Orange executive who could not pick up a phone on his own ended up building the tool that today lets tens of thousands of others do so — not as a personal vindication, but as the repair of a broader oversight: a century of telephony designed without anyone asking whether it worked for everyone.
