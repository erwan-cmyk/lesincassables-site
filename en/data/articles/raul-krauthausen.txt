Berlin, 2004. In his daily life, Raúl Krauthausen needs the help of a young man assigned to Germany's compulsory civilian service, at the time the equivalent of military service for conscientious objectors. Rather than go through the usual administrative channels, he launches, with his cousin Jan Mörsch, a public casting call modeled on the show Deutschland sucht den Superstar and titled Die Suche nach dem SuperZivi, literally the search for the super civilian-service worker. More than a hundred and fifty candidates turn up. One of them does indeed become his assistant. The other result, the lasting one, is the founding of an association that will never stop growing: Sozialhelden e.V.

Born on July 15, 1980, in Lima, Peru, to a German mother, a doctor, and a Peruvian father, Raúl Krauthausen is born with osteogenesis imperfecta, the so-called brittle bone disease, which weakens his skeleton and leads him, as a child, to get around in a wheelchair. A year later, his parents settle in Germany, where they judge the system of care and support better suited to his needs. A few years after that, the family spends a year in Colombia; what he would keep from it is the memory not of an obstacle tied to his body, but of a language challenge. "That was the biggest challenge of my life," he would tell the German daily Neue Westfälische years later, speaking of learning Spanish on the fly.

Back in Berlin, he attends, from kindergarten on, one of Germany's very first integrative primary schools, alongside other children with disabilities. He does run into very concrete limits there: in physical education, he starts out assisting the teacher before being shut out altogether. He earns his school-leaving certificate, the Abitur, in 2001, at a school that is still integrated.

As a teenager, he is already getting familiar with the media: at seventeen, he works as a co-host for the association Aktion Mensch and is invited onto the set of the journalist Roger Willemsen, before landing an internship at the Berlin station Radio Fritz. In 2002, he begins studying social and economic communication at the Berlin University of the Arts (UdK), while working at an advertising agency and then as head of programming at Radio Fritz, a stable job he chooses to leave in order to devote himself fully to advocacy.

It is out of that casting experience that Sozialhelden e.V. is born, in 2004, founded with Jan Mörsch as a pragmatic laboratory of ideas rather than a classic charity. The name, Sozialhelden, literally "social heroes," is nothing like a modest choice: "We called ourselves Sozialhelden, in all megalomania," Krauthausen would explain years later, again in the pages of the Neue Westfälische.

The following year brings Pfandtastisch Helfen, a system for collecting bottle deposits for the benefit of charities, dreamed up one evening among friends and financed thanks to the prize from a competition run by the magazine NEON: a car that neither of the two cousins, having no driver's license, could use, and which they resell to get the project started. In 2008, the initiative receives the startsocial prize, worth 5,000 euros and presented by Chancellor Angela Merkel. But it is in 2010 that Krauthausen launches the project that will give him international reach: Wheelmap.org, a crowdsourced map, built on OpenStreetMap's open data, which records the wheelchair accessibility of public places around the world. Unable to know in advance whether a café, a train station or a movie theater will be accessible to him, he chooses to map the information himself, with the help of thousands of contributors. Within a few years, the tool lists hundreds of thousands of places on five continents and receives the United Nations World Summit Award in 2012.

On April 10, 2013, Krauthausen receives the Order of Merit of the Federal Republic of Germany from Ursula von der Leyen, then federal minister of labor and social affairs. Two days later, without waiting for the honor to settle, he publishes on his blog an open letter to the social services administration and to the minister herself, denouncing rules that cap the savings of people dependent on assistance at 2,600 euros and that regulate even their life as a couple. In it he puts a blunt question to the welfare state: does it want to make these citizens full participants, or only a line of spending to be watched?

Today, Sozialhelden employs some thirty people in Berlin, funded by a mix of grants, donations and accessibility consulting work for companies and public bodies. Krauthausen, for his part, has taken on many roles: host of the show KRAUTHAUSEN – face to face on Sport1 since 2015, podcaster with Im Aufzug, columnist for the public broadcaster MDR, and author of several books, among them the bestseller Wie kann ich was bewegen? (2021), which made the Spiegel bestseller list, and the essay Wer Inklusion will, findet einen Weg (2023), co-written with Martin Kulik. "Heroes have no excuses," he sums up in the pages of the magazine Human Resources Manager, a line that could serve as a subtitle for his whole career.

On his personal website, where he introduces himself plainly as an activist and author, one sentence sums up twenty years of work more surely than any list of prizes: "Non-disabled people also have the right to live with disabled people."
