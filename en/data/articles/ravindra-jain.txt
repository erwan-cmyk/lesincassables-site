A Sunday morning in 1987, in India, and the streets empty out. Shops pull down their shutters, official meetings are postponed, whole families crowd around the neighborhood's only television set, sometimes garlanded with flowers for the occasion. On the public channel Doordarshan, at the same hour every week, a serial holds an entire country in suspense: Ramanand Sagar's Ramayan, an adaptation of the great Hindu epic poem, which at the time draws, according to the BBC, up to 82 percent of available viewers, a world audience record for the era. Its music, as famous as the images, bears the signature of a man the public will never see in a recording studio: Ravindra Jain, a composer blind since birth.

Ravindra Jain is born on February 28, 1944, in Aligarh, in present-day Uttar Pradesh, the third of eight children. His father, Pandit Indramani Jain, a Sanskrit scholar and practitioner of Ayurvedic medicine, notices his son's exceptional ear very early. The available sources do not specify the medical cause of this blindness from birth, which prevents the child from following a conventional education beyond his first years of schooling. Ravindra turns instead to singing: he performs bhajans, devotional songs, in the temples of his region, before receiving rigorous musical training from three masters, Pandit G. L. Jain, Pandit Janardhan Sharma and Pandit Nathu Ram, who pass on to him the foundations of Hindustani classical music.

This training takes him to Calcutta, where he teaches singing, becomes familiar with the repertoire of Rabindranath Tagore and records his first songs, notably for All India Radio. It is only at the very beginning of the 1970s that he moves to Bombay, then the undisputed capital of Hindi cinema, to try his luck as a composer. His first compositions, entrusted in particular to the voice of Mohammed Rafi, already a legend of Indian playback singing, initially go unnoticed. Recognition comes in 1974 with Chor Machaye Shor, for which Ravindra Jain combines three roles rarely held together in the Hindi film industry: composer, lyricist and, on some tracks, singer himself.

The film's success truly launches him. Commissions follow one after another: Geet Gaata Chal (1975), then Chitchor (1976), whose song "Gori Tera Gaon," built on the raga Dhani, earns the South Indian singer K. J. Yesudas the Filmfare Award for Best Male Playback Singer. Thus begins one of the most enduring collaborations in Hindi cinema: for more than a decade, Ravindra Jain will compose for Yesudas's voice, alongside another faithful performer, Hemlata. Over the years he becomes the house composer of Rajshri Productions, a studio specializing in family films, for which he notably writes the music of Nadiya Ke Paar (1982), shot on a modest budget and one of the biggest popular hits of the year.

The director and producer Raj Kapoor then entrusts him with the music of Ram Teri Ganga Maili (1985): the film, a huge box-office success, earns Ravindra Jain the Filmfare Award for Best Music Director, the most notable distinction of his film career. After Kapoor's death in 1988, Jain goes on to compose the music of Henna, released in 1991 under the RK banner.

But it is television that gives his work its widest reach. For Ramanand Sagar's Ramayan, Ravindra Jain composes the entire soundtrack of a series that becomes an unprecedented social phenomenon: power cuts during the broadcast are reported to have sometimes brought threats against local electricity authorities. He follows it with other major televised religious epics, including Shri Krishna and Jai Hanuman. In all, over more than four decades of work, Ravindra Jain will have written the music for more than a hundred films, continuing to compose until the mid-2010s.

Asked about his disability, he would readily explain that being blind had helped him concentrate better and become more deeply involved in his work. He was keen to point out, in remarks he made in an interview, that one should never let such difficulties get the upper hand, nor ever give up.

In 2015, India awards him the Padma Shri, the country's fourth-highest civilian honor, for his contribution to the arts. A few months later, on October 9, 2015, Ravindra Jain dies in Mumbai of multiple organ failure — a urinary infection is said, according to some sources, to have been the cause. Prime Minister Narendra Modi pays tribute to him in a few words that sum up the musician's trajectory: he will be remembered, he writes, for the versatility of his music and his fighting spirit. Ravindra Jain leaves behind his wife Divya Jain and their son Aayushman, as well as melodies that generations of Indian viewers still hum today, without always knowing they were composed by a man who never saw a single screen.
