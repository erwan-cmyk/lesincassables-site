In the headmaster's office at Stowe School, a select boarding school in Buckinghamshire, a teenager listens as his headmaster, Robert Drayson, sums up his future in a single sentence: he'll either end up in prison or become a millionaire. This sixteen-year-old has just spent several years struggling on the school benches — mediocre results, laborious reading, harsh report cards — without anyone, at the time, putting a precise name to his difficulties. That day, in the mid-1960s, Richard Branson leaves Stowe for good.

He is born on July 18, 1950, in Blackheath, southeast London, into a family where academic failure was hardly a given. His father, Edward Branson, is a barrister; his paternal grandfather, Sir George Arthur Harwin Branson, served as a judge on the High Court of Justice. His mother, Eve, a former ballet dancer and flight attendant, has an entrepreneurial streak of her own — she would spend a period earning a living making and selling wooden tissue boxes. Two younger sisters, Lindy and Vanessa, complete the family. As a child, Richard Branson attends Scaitcliffe School, a preparatory school in Surrey, before moving on to Stowe at thirteen.

His difficulties would later be named dyslexia — a specific learning disorder that complicates decoding text when reading and writing, without diminishing intelligence in any way. But in the English school system of the 1950s and 60s, the word is barely recognized, let alone accommodated: a child who stumbles over letters and turns in essays full of mistakes is simply judged slow, or even lazy. Decades later, Branson would write an op-ed for The Times about a schooling spent as a dyslexic child mistaken for a poor student.

At sixteen, he doesn't go back to school. With a group of friends, he launches Student, a magazine conceived as a platform for the voice of British youth at the time — with no five-year plan or carefully kept accounts, but with instinct, resourcefulness, and a knack for convincing people out loud of what the written word denied him. It's a method he never abandons.

From that first magazine comes, in 1970, a mail-order record business named Virgin, founded with his partner Nik Powell. The following year, the young company opens a record shop in London; in 1973, its label, Virgin Records, releases Mike Oldfield's Tubular Bells, an instant hit that establishes the label within the British music scene. Within a few years, Virgin becomes one of the independent labels that matter in the UK.

The name soon spills well beyond the world of records. In 1984, Branson turns it into an airline, Virgin Atlantic, a risky bet in an industry dominated by well-established legacy carriers. Other sectors follow over the decades: the brand comes to cover nearly a hundred companies by the 1990s, then a group present in more than thirty-five countries, today employing roughly seventy thousand people. The teenager his headmaster didn't see succeeding within a conventional framework is, by the early 1990s, running one of Britain's most diversified business groups.

In 2004, he founds Virgin Galactic, with the ambition of making space tourism accessible to private passengers. Seventeen years later, on July 11, 2021, he himself boards the VSS Unity spacecraft, on a mission named Unity 22, for a suborbital flight reaching an altitude of roughly 86 kilometers, alongside two pilots and three other crew members. He becomes the first founder of a space company to fly himself aboard a vehicle built by his own company.

Knighted in 1999 for services to entrepreneurship, the boy his school predicted for prison or fortune chose to tell his dyslexia as a starting point rather than as the central obstacle of his story. In his memoir, Losing My Virginity, he sums up the philosophy that carried him from Stowe to space: "I suppose the secret to bouncing back is not only to be unafraid of failures but to use them as motivational and learning tools... There's nothing wrong with making mistakes as long as you don't make the same ones over and over again."

Now at the head of a fortune Forbes estimates in the billions of dollars, Richard Branson continues to preside over Virgin Group's activities. From the classroom where he struggled to decode a page of text to the cockpit of a spacecraft, he turned every predicted failure into one more step forward.
