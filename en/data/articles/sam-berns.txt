One fall evening, on the football field of the high school in Foxborough, Massachusetts, the marching band moves into formation. Among the brass, a slight teenager carries a snare drum mounted on a custom-built harness: barely six pounds, compared to the roughly forty pounds of the standard model — far too heavy for a boy who himself weighs only about forty-five pounds. As drumline captain at his high school, Sam Berns had to fight, with his family's help, to get this lightweight instrument built so he could march like everyone else. That evening, on the field, nothing really sets him apart from the rest of the band — which is exactly what he wanted.

Sampson Gordon Berns — everyone calls him Sam — is born on October 23, 1996, in Providence, Rhode Island, and grows up in Foxborough, Massachusetts. His parents, Leslie Gordon and Scott Berns, are both physicians. Around nine months old, they notice his growth slowing abnormally; early hair loss and delayed teething follow. When he's just under two, a family friend, herself a doctor, raises an extremely rare possibility: progeria. The diagnosis is confirmed by one of the few American specialists in the disease.

Faced with the near-total absence of research into this disease, which accelerates aging in the children it affects, Leslie Gordon sets aside her hospital career. In 1999, with her husband and her sister, attorney Audrey Gordon, she founds the Progeria Research Foundation — at the time, the only organization in the world entirely devoted to the disease. "I could not live with myself if I didn't try," she would tell Brown Alumni Magazine, her alma mater's magazine, years later. In 2003, the foundation and the U.S. National Institutes of Health identify the gene responsible, LMNA. That discovery paves the way, in 2007, for a first clinical trial that Sam takes part in, alongside several dozen children from many countries, testing an experimental drug, lonafarnib.

An honor-roll student and member of the National Honor Society, Sam Berns grows up passionate about sports and science. For a time he dreams of becoming an engineer — "kind of like Einstein and Steve Jobs combined," he jokes in the same magazine — before falling in love with biology and genetics. A drummer since childhood, he plays snare in his high school marching band. As a Scout, he reaches the movement's highest rank, Eagle Scout.

His family catches the attention of filmmakers Sean Fine and Andrea Nix Fine, who follow Sam and his parents for the documentary Life According to Sam. Premiering at the Sundance Film Festival in January 2013 and airing on HBO that October, the film shows Sam's daily life as a teenager alongside the clinical trial his mother runs. It wins a Peabody Award and an Emmy Award, and is shortlisted among fifteen documentaries for the Academy Award.

A few weeks later, just seventeen years old, Sam Berns takes the stage at the TEDxMidAtlantic conference. In "My Philosophy for a Happy Life," he lays out the principles that shape his life: accepting what he can't do in order to focus on "there is so much I can do," surrounding himself with people he chooses, refusing self-pity — "I don't want people to feel bad for me." He also cites the family motto borrowed from Walt Disney: "we don't look backwards for very long. We keep moving forward." Posted online just months before his death, the video will be viewed more than 54 million times.

Sam Berns dies on January 10, 2014, in Foxborough, from complications related to progeria — four years past the average life expectancy for children with the disease, estimated at thirteen by the Progeria Research Foundation. He dies the day before the New England Patriots playoff game where he was to serve as honorary captain, an invitation he'd received just days earlier. Team owner Robert Kraft praises, in a statement, his "positive outlook on life." His funeral is held at Temple Israel in Sharon, Massachusetts.

His death doesn't close the work his parents began. The Progeria Research Foundation's annual scientific meetings, which drew about a dozen researchers in the early days, now bring together nearly a hundred. In November 2020, lonafarnib — the drug tested on Sam and several dozen other children starting in 2007 — becomes, under the name Zokinvy, the first progeria treatment approved by U.S. health authorities: clinical trials show it extends patients' life expectancy by several years. Sam Berns, who had himself received the drug in its experimental form, didn't live to see that milestone. But more than ten years after his death, his face and his way of telling his own story keep reaching audiences who, often, had never heard of progeria before.
