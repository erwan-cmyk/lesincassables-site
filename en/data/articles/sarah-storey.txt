In September 1992, in the Olympic pool in Barcelona, a fourteen-year-old dives in for her very first international competition. She is still called Sarah Bailey. She comes out of it with two gold medals. No one, that day, could guess that she will contest eight more editions of the Paralympic Games, in two different sports, until she becomes the most decorated para athlete in British sporting history, across every discipline.

Sarah Bailey is born on October 26, 1977, in Eccles, in the Manchester area. At birth, her left hand had not developed normally: the umbilical cord had wrapped around her arm during the pregnancy, restricting the blood supply. She grows up with that partially formed left hand, classified C5 once she becomes a cyclist — an upper-limb impairment that keeps her neither from swimming nor, later, from riding at the very top of the world. "Having a partially formed left hand is a circumstance that has never figured in any of my decisions," she would explain much later, in 2012, in an interview with the British magazine The Upcoming.

From 1992 to 2004, she contests four editions of the Paralympic Games as a swimmer — Barcelona, Atlanta, Sydney, Athens — and piles up the honors: five titles, eight silver medals, three bronzes, plus six world crowns and eighteen European titles in the pool. Then, in 2005, a chronic ear infection keeps her from training in the pool for several months. To avoid losing her fitness, she gets on a bike. Before the year is out, she is already breaking the world record in the 3-kilometer pursuit — a change of course that will become a full second career.

From Beijing 2008 to Paris 2024, the woman who in 2007 married Barney Storey — himself a tandem pilot for visually impaired cyclists and a Paralympic medalist — contests five more editions of the Games. At the London 2012 Games, held on home soil, she takes four gold medals and is made, in their wake, a Dame Commander of the Order of the British Empire. Her daughter Louisa is born the following year; she is back by 2016, at the Rio Games, to collect three more titles. After the birth of her son Charlie, in 2017, she reappears in Tokyo in 2021 and wins five more gold medals there, passing along the way the record of sixteen titles held until then by the swimmer Mike Kenny: she officially becomes the most decorated British Paralympian in history, across all sports. In Paris, in 2024, she adds two last gold medals to her collection, the final one won in a road race that came down to a sprint.

At the end of that career, the tally is staggering: thirty Paralympic medals, nineteen of them gold, gathered across nine editions of the Games between 1992 and 2024. To that come twenty-nine world championship titles, twenty-one European titles, and seventy-five world records broken, in swimming and then on the bike. Since 2022, alongside her competitive career, she has also held the post of Greater Manchester's active travel commissioner, appointed by mayor Andy Burnham to develop the region's cycling and walking infrastructure — a way of staying involved on the roads, but this time for everyone.

In early July 2026, at 48, Sarah Storey announces her retirement from competition and gives up, with it, a tenth Paralympic Games, due two years later in Los Angeles. "I am so privileged to have spent 35 years as an international athlete," she says. "I genuinely pinch myself that my childhood dream of being an athlete for as long as I possibly could has led to nine Paralympic Games and opportunities across so many sporting events." She maintains that she could physically have defended the two titles she won in Paris, but says she now wants to weigh on the future of her sport in another way: "There's also no doubt in my mind that, within Para sport, we have stalled somewhat since London 2012, and the years between each Games haven't been utilised well enough to create the momentum that I and others hoped to see."

Thirty-four years after diving into a Paralympic pool for the first time with a hand she never regarded as an obstacle, Sarah Storey therefore leaves competition not by fading away, but simply by changing the ground she works on — from the pool and the velodrome to the bodies that will, she hopes, shape the next generation of Paralympic athletes.
