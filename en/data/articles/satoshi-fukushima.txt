In a lecture hall at the Research Center for Advanced Science and Technology, at the University of Tokyo, a professor addresses his audience. Beside him, an interpreter rests her fingers on his: every question from the room reaches him as a series of rhythmic taps on his fingers, three fingers of each hand reproducing the six dots of braille. Satoshi Fukushima neither sees the room nor hears it. Yet he has been teaching there since 2001, at the head of one of the most singular research laboratories in Japan.

Born on December 25, 1962, in Kobe, in Hyōgo Prefecture, Fukushima is today described by several Japanese media outlets as the world's first deafblind university professor to hold a full-time post. In 2003, Time Asia magazine singled him out as one of its twenty-nine Asian Heroes of the year. Nothing in his childhood hinted at such a path.

An eye infection contracted when he is only five months old costs him the sight in his right eye at the age of three. Six years later, sympathetic ophthalmia strikes the remaining good eye: at nine, he is blind in both eyes. Yet the child adapts without his daily life being turned upside down — he still hears, goes to school, communicates normally. It is this intact hearing that allows him, for nearly a decade, to pursue a schooling almost like everyone else's.

But at fourteen, he loses the hearing in his right ear. Then, at the very start of 1981, a few days after celebrating his eighteenth birthday, the hearing in his left ear declines in turn, in barely a few weeks. In February, he writes to a friend that "if life has a meaning, there must also be a meaning to this suffering." Shortly afterward, he becomes totally deaf and blind.

The first weeks of total deafblindness are a brutal isolation. Fukushima would later describe them in these terms: "I felt like I was locked in an underground prison, and now and then someone would appear outside my tiny window to express their sympathy. We'd talk for a bit, then they'd leave again." With no way of receiving or sending the slightest message, he loses all access to the outside world.

It is his mother, Reiko, who invents the solution. He places the episode at the start of March 1981 — the 3rd, he thinks, without certainty, having not written it down in a diary. She has the idea of transmitting the dots of braille directly onto her son's fingers, tapping according to a precise code: three fingers of each hand corresponding to the six dots of the braille alphabet. This method, called finger braille — or yubitenji in Japanese — allows for the first time a real-time conversation, word by word, without paper or typewriter. With the help of interpreters trained in the technique, it will become his sole channel of communication.

Returned to school thanks to this tool, at the end of 1981 he joins a support group formed to prepare his entry into university — the forerunner of what will become the Japan Deafblind Association. In 1983, he is admitted to Tokyo Metropolitan University, becoming the first deafblind student enrolled at a Japanese university. He graduates in 1987, another national first.

His academic career is built over the long term. He teaches as an associate professor at Kanazawa University from 1996 to 2001, then joins the University of Tokyo — a first for a deafblind person in the institution's history — with the mission of developing a disability studies program there. He earns his doctorate there in 2008 and rises to the rank of professor, at the head of the Barrier-Free Laboratory, devoted to accessibility and disability sciences.

His research examines what it means to communicate, and to live, when two major senses are missing. For communication to occur, he insists, "it's not enough just to speak. Unless you can hear what the other person is saying, it's only one way." Day to day, he works with a portable device that converts emails, texts and dictionaries into braille — several thousand digitized books sit at his fingertips. He also heads the Japan Deafblind Association and represents Japan at the World Federation of the Deafblind. In 1996, he receives, together with his mother, the Yoshikawa Eiji Cultural Award; in 2015, the Kazuo Homma Prize, awarded by the Japan Braille Library.

In 2022, a feature film inspired by his story and that of Reiko, his mother, is released in Japanese theaters. Asked about the meaning of his own existence, Fukushima answers with the same sobriety that runs through his entire path: "first of all, to live, and next, to go on living." That, in his view, is what we are called on to do — he like anyone else.
