One day, a South African office solutions company gets in touch with Sebenzile Matsebula. Not out of admiration: the company is looking to improve its score on the Broad-Based Black Economic Empowerment (BBBEE) scorecard, the South African mechanism that rates companies on the diversity of their ownership. "Initially I was approached by a company that wanted to up its score points on the Broad Based Black Economic Empowerment (BEE) score card. I am black, a woman, and have a disability. So they scored 3 different points for the price of one individual," she told the website Lionesses of Africa in 2015. She could have left it there. She goes on: "I just couldn't resist the challenge. I thrive on challenge, so I convinced myself, I want to move into business."

That company is Motswako Office Solutions — a name meaning "mixture" — which specializes in office automation, printing, and telecom/VoIP solutions. It was not founded by Sebenzile Matsebula. Bought in 2010 by Jaco de Jager, when it was still called Gestetner Motswako, before he renamed it and grew it under its current name, the company today has that same Jaco de Jager as managing director and majority shareholder. Matsebula, for her part, is co-director and holds 26% of the capital, according to the South African trade website The GAPP. On the organizational chart published by Motswako, she carries the title of Executive Director, in charge of business development, governance, and policies for the inclusion of marginalized groups within the company.

This entrepreneurial turn comes late in an already long career. For several years, under the presidency of Thabo Mbeki, Matsebula heads the Office on the Status of Disabled Persons, attached to the South African Presidency. In June 2003, she presents her country's position before the United Nations ad hoc committee charged with negotiating what will become the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities, arguing for a broad definition of disability and an effective monitoring mechanism. She today chairs the Disability Workshop Development Enterprise and sits on several boards, including those of Action on Disability and Development, Cheshire Homes South Africa, and the FirstRand Foundation. Several biographies also mention her appointment, in 2012, to the United Nations Committee on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities — information we were unable to cross-check against a primary source.

Sebenzile Matsebula contracted polio at the age of ten months; the disease left her with paraplegia. For a long time she got around on crutches; today she increasingly uses a wheelchair, as post-polio syndrome wears down her stamina. "When you get older and you have post-polio syndrome, your energy levels are not as good as they were when you're younger. Sometimes I get tired, but I am still able to have a full day," she told Lionesses of Africa. In the same interview, she recounted having had to work harder than her non-disabled colleagues throughout her career in order to be taken seriously, against the received idea that a person with a disability would be less competent or less able to withstand pressure.

Under the joint leadership of De Jager and Matsebula, Motswako Office Solutions grows from 25 employees at the time of the 2010 buyout to 153 in 2018, with ten offices in South Africa and a branch in London, according to The GAPP. That year, the company claims 54% revenue growth and once again wins the title of Business Partner of the Year, awarded by the Japanese manufacturer Ricoh to its South African partners. "We've grown since I bought the company in 2010 and this year revenues have grown 54 percent," De Jager said at the time. More recently, the B2B database ZoomInfo put Motswako's revenue at about $22.8 million — a figure to be treated with caution, since estimates of this kind rest on methodologies that are rarely made public.

From this trajectory, Sebenzile Matsebula draws a doctrine without sentimentality. "In the entrepreneurial space there is no charity and there are no handouts; it's all about the bottom line and profits. Avoid negativity and self pity — no one is interested in that!" she summed up as early as 2015, addressing entrepreneurs with disabilities who might want to follow her path. A line that says a good deal about how she built her place: a full shareholder and executive of a company she did not create, but that she did much to help grow.
