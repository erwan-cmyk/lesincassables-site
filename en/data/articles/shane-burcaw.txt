One afternoon, in the gym of his school in Bethlehem, Pennsylvania, a boy of about ten is minding the net for his floor hockey team. He does not skate: sitting in his wheelchair, a stick taped to the armrest, he deflects the pucks that come flying at him. That day, as every time he has wanted to do something his disease seemed to forbid him, his parents have found a way to include him rather than keep him out. That boy is named Shane Burcaw.

Born on May 28, 1992, in Bethlehem, Shane Burcaw grows up in a family that refuses, very early on, to make his disease an excuse for withdrawing from the world. He has a brother, Andrew. In the Burcaw house, humor becomes a tool of daily life before becoming, much later, a profession: you learn to laugh at hard situations rather than run from them.

Shane Burcaw is diagnosed with type 2 spinal muscular atrophy before his first birthday. The disease progressively destroys the neurons that control the muscles; he will never walk, and he has used a motorized wheelchair since the age of two. At the time of his diagnosis, the prognosis attached to that form of the disease was bleak: the treatments that would change the picture for many patients with spinal muscular atrophy do not yet exist.

After school in Bethlehem, he goes to Moravian College, where in 2014 he earns a bachelor's degree in English. As early as 2012, still a student, he takes part in a university research program alongside professor Dana Dunn, devoted to the life stories of people with disabilities. "I love to write, and psychology has always been of great interest to me," he would say of that project, in which he draws for the first time on his own experience as working material rather than as a subject to keep quiet.

It is also during his college years that everything tips. One bored afternoon, in 2011, Shane Burcaw opens a blog on the Tumblr platform, which he names Laughing At My Nightmare. "I just wanted to make people laugh," he would tell the Young Adult Library Services Association about a project with no particular ambition. The tone — direct, funny, blunt about daily life with a neuromuscular disease — finds its audience: more than half a million people would soon follow his posts. Out of it comes a first book, Laughing at My Nightmare, published in 2014, followed in 2017 by a children's book, Not So Different, then in 2019 by Strangers Assume My Girlfriend Is My Nurse. With his cousin Sarah, he also turns the blog into a nonprofit: Laughing At My Nightmare, Inc. has since distributed more than a million dollars' worth of adapted equipment and more than $140,000 in scholarships to students with muscular dystrophy.

In 2016, a young woman from Minnesota, Hannah Aylward, comes across a mini-documentary that the director Rainn Wilson devoted to Shane Burcaw. She writes to him. The two exchange messages, meet, then build a long-distance relationship that would become, in 2018, a YouTube channel: Squirmy and Grubs. The premise, still rarely seen at the time, is simple: to show, unfiltered, what a romantic relationship between a non-disabled person and a person with a disability actually is. The channel grows fast; today it is approaching two million subscribers.

Shane proposes to Hannah at a family dinner, feigning a dizzy spell to draw her aside before producing the ring he had been hiding behind his back — not without having first asked Hannah's parents for their permission. The wedding takes place on September 4, 2020, in a garden, in the middle of the pandemic, before a small circle and with an officiant connected by Zoom. "I married my best friend," Shane would write the day after the ceremony. Their union also draws its share of hostile comments online, from people skeptical that a love story between a disabled person and a non-disabled person could be sincere. Hannah would answer them head-on: "No matter what you do, some people will just never come around."

As an adult, Shane Burcaw begins a treatment meant to slow the progression of spinal muscular atrophy — an option that did not exist at the time of his diagnosis. Long hesitant to commit to it, he now speaks of it in measured terms, saying the treatment has given him a steadiness and a strength he was not expecting. In 2025, he publishes with Hannah Interabled: True Stories About Love and Disability, a collection of accounts from other couples facing the same questions they do. The couple today divides its time between Minnesota and the Los Angeles area.

Fifteen years after his first blog posts, Shane Burcaw has built, with Hannah, one of the most-followed bodies of work on disability in the United States — more than 760 million cumulative views across all his content. A trajectory he goes on telling the way he began it: unfiltered, and without pathos.
