In the lecture halls of Cairo University, at the end of the 1960s, students crowd in by the thousands to hear him, even though no Egyptian radio or television station broadcasts his music. Yet they know every song by heart, learned by word of mouth or from cassettes copied in secret. This man with the oud is Sheikh Imam, and at his side stands the poet Ahmed Fouad Negm: together, without ever setting foot in an official studio, they have become one of the most popular and most closely watched voices of contemporary Egypt.

He was born Imam Mohammed Ahmed Eissa, on July 2, 1918, according to most sources, in the village of Abu al-Numrus, in the Giza governorate south of Cairo, into a modest family. He loses his sight in the very first months of his life; the exact circumstances of this early blindness are not established with certainty by the available biographical sources. Sent to the kuttab, the village Quranic school, he memorizes the entire Quran before the age of twelve, which earns him, very early, the title of sheikh.

He pursues religious studies at an institution attached to Al-Azhar, in Cairo, from which he is eventually expelled as a teenager: the authorities reproach him for having listened to the radio, considered at the time incompatible with the piety expected of a theology student. Without a roof or resources, he takes refuge for a while in the mosques of old Cairo and turns to music. Self-taught on the oud, he then refines his craft with established musicians, including Sheikh Darwish al-Hariri, who teaches him the basics of muwashshah singing, and the composer Zakariyya Ahmad, whose orchestra he joins for a time. He sings at weddings and circumcision celebrations, and builds a reputation in the working-class neighborhoods near Al-Azhar.

Everything changes in 1962, when a mutual friend introduces him to Ahmed Fouad Negm, a still little-known poet who writes in colloquial Egyptian Arabic rather than in classical Arabic. Negm leaves the room he was renting to move in with Sheikh Imam, in an old quarter of Cairo. Thus begins a collaboration of more than thirty years: Negm writes lyrics carried by and for the working class, Imam sets them to music and sings them in a stripped-down style, centered on the oud and sometimes a tambourine, meant to be sung along to in chorus rather than listened to as a spectator. The Syrian poet Nizar Qabbani would see in this duo an alliance placed in the service of a people whose voice had long been gagged.

Egypt's 1967 defeat by Israel marks a turning point: their songs become more directly political, denouncing corruption, social misery and the impotence of those in power. Sheikh Imam and Negm are banned from state radio and television. Their music circulates all the same, carried by cassettes copied clandestinely in universities, at demonstrations and in private homes, thereby escaping official censorship.

This popularity costs them dearly. In 1969, both are sentenced to life imprisonment for having supported student and worker protest movements; they are released in 1972. A new imprisonment follows as early as 1973, again because of their political songs — a repression aimed at their ideas and not at any personal wrongdoing, and one that is now an integral part of their shared legend. The following year, the pomp-filled visit of American President Richard Nixon to Cairo, on June 12, 1974, inspires "Sharraft ya Nixon Baba," a text of biting irony about the splendor laid on for the occasion while Egypt remains poor. The song, like "Guevara Mat," a tribute to Che Guevara, becomes one of the most widely covered titles in his repertoire. The arrests continue under Sadat, up to a final spell in prison in the early 1980s, from which he emerges only a few years later.

Once freed, Sheikh Imam tours internationally from the mid-1980s onward: France, the United Kingdom, Lebanon, Tunisia, Libya and Algeria welcome him before enthusiastic audiences, particularly among the Arab diasporas of Europe. The man no Egyptian radio station has ever broadcast now fills halls abroad.

His collaboration with Negm comes to an end in the mid-1980s, for reasons that remain poorly established — some point to tensions that arose with the fame gained during the tours. Sheikh Imam continues alone with a more low-key career, draws closer to religious practice, and goes on living modestly in old Cairo, surrounded by friends. He dies on June 6, 1995, at 76, after a long illness. The obituary the daily Al-Ahram first devotes to him is so brief that it reportedly provokes a reaction: a second text, signed by numerous figures from the cultural world, is said to have paid tribute to the man they called the artist of the people.

Sixteen years after his death, his songs resurface in Tahrir Square, during the Egyptian uprising of 2011: a new generation takes up "Sharraft ya Nixon Baba" and "Guevara Mat" as anthems of protest. Kept off the air for most of his career, Sheikh Imam will have ended up carrying his voice farther than any state radio would ever have allowed him.
