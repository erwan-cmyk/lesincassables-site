In June 1994, in the back room of the Tapas Bar on Rundle Street in Adelaide, an eighteen-year-old singer takes the stage to open for the British band Mother Earth. Journalist Paul Kitching, there that night for the magazine Rip It Up, remembers a performance that seizes the room immediately: "She had the ability to pull you in and you were transfixed." The singer's name is Sia Furler. The band she has just put together, Crisp, is only a month old. Nobody imagines that twenty years later she will be one of the most influential voices and pens in pop.

Sia Kate Isobelle Furler is born on December 18, 1975, in Adelaide, South Australia. Her father, Phil Colson, is a guitarist; her mother, Loene Furler, teaches art. She grows up among musicians and music lovers, raised on Aretha Franklin, Stevie Wonder and Sting. At twelve, she stays in New York with a family friend, the musician Colin Hay of the band Men at Work, during the Grammy Awards ceremony: watching the broadcast on television settles her vocation for good.

Back in Adelaide, the teenager works the local circuit with Crisp, the jazz-funk band she founds in the mid-1990s. The group releases an album, Word and the Deal, in 1996, then an EP, Delirium, the following year, and draws the attention of labels and artists, Jamiroquai among them. But Crisp breaks up in 1997. That same year Sia puts out a little-noticed first solo album, OnlySee, before leaving Australia for London.

In London, she sings first in Jamiroquai's backing vocals, then becomes one of the voices of the trip-hop group Zero 7, alongside Sophie Barker. She lends her voice to the single Destiny in 2001, then to Somersault and Speed Dial No. 2 in 2004, and takes part in the album The Garden in 2006. The period makes her known to an inner circle before her solo career, and lets her make a living from music — without yet being in the spotlight.

These London beginnings are darkened, though, by a tragedy. Sia is about to be reunited in London with her partner, Dan Pontifex, when she learns, from Thailand, that he has just been hit by a taxi on his twenty-fourth birthday. She is twenty-one; she goes to his funeral instead of their reunion. "I couldn't really feel anything," she would later tell The Sunday Times. A long spiral of addiction follows: "Unfortunately, that bender lasted six years for me," she acknowledges in the same interview, before joining Alcoholics Anonymous in 2010.

It is in this same tested body that Sia has always carried another reality, one she will make public only years later. In October 2019, on Twitter, she reveals that she has Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, a connective tissue disorder that causes chronic pain, accompanied, she says, by neurological problems. She addresses those going through something comparable: "I love you, keep going." Her career, meanwhile, never pauses: the disclosure arrives inside an international run already nearly twenty years old, and one that continues, album after album.

Five years earlier, in March 2014, the single Chandelier, from the album 1000 Forms of Fear, pushes Sia herself — rather than the artists she writes for — to the front of the stage for the first time: the song climbs to No. 8 on the Billboard Hot 100 and earns her four Grammy Award nominations. It is in this period that she chooses to perform with her face hidden beneath a blonde bob wig, letting the young dancer Maddie Ziegler embody her presence on screen. "I'm trying to have some control over my image," she would explain to Interview magazine.

This taste for self-effacement is rooted in another side of her career, begun a few years earlier: writing for other artists. A much sought-after songwriter in Los Angeles, she signs Diamonds for Rihanna — which she says she wrote in about fifteen minutes — Pretty Hurts for Beyoncé, Perfume for Britney Spears, and Titanium, conceived with Alicia Keys in mind before becoming, in her own voice, a hit credited to David Guetta. In 2016, she gathers on the album This Is Acting several tracks written for others and never used, among them Cheap Thrills, a number one in the United States.

Two episodes sum up the ambivalence of this fame held at arm's length. In April 2016, her concert at the Coachella festival, her first since 2011, makes an impression: she stays in the background while her dancers occupy the stage. Five years later, she steps behind the camera with Music, a musical film centered on an autistic teenager, which draws sharp controversy over its portrayal of autism and earns her the Razzie Award for worst director — a setback she owns, in a trajectory otherwise widely praised.

In 2024, Sia releases Reasonable Woman, her tenth studio album, with a much-noticed collaboration with Kylie Minogue. On the personal side, she has just reached a custody and child support settlement with Dan Bernad, her ex-husband and the father of her son Somersault Wonder — a name that picks up, surely not by chance, the title of the Zero 7 track she was singing twenty years earlier. She says she is sober again today. "I'm a sober working mom trying to buy peace," she wrote recently, according to E! News, summing up in a single sentence a life built, against all odds, on the stage.
