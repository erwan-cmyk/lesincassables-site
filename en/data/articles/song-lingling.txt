Rio de Janeiro, September 2016. In the pool of the Olympic Aquatics Stadium, a twenty-year-old Chinese swimmer touches the wall first in the 100-meter backstroke, S6 class. Earlier that day, in the heats of that same event, she had already erased the world record, in 1:24.66. In the final, she turns that performance into a Paralympic title. Song Lingling becomes Paralympic champion barely seven years after entering a swimming pool for the first time.

That first time goes back to 2009, in Shenyang, a large industrial city in northeastern China. Song Lingling is thirteen. She has grown up with her legs paralyzed as a result of poliomyelitis contracted in childhood. A local coach, Li Jianhui, suggests she try swimming. She accepts, without knowing that this municipal pool will become the starting point of one of the fastest trajectories in Chinese Paralympic sport.

In the water, Song Lingling finds a discipline in which her arms are enough to carry her. She progresses quickly, spotted and then brought into the regional para sport pathways that train young athletes across China, before joining the national team as a teenager. The sessions follow one another, with the rigor characteristic of Chinese sports structures, where promising profiles are identified early and enrolled in a high-performance training program. Her specialty, the backstroke, is swum in the S6 class, which groups swimmers with different physical impairments but of a comparable level of severity. Over the course of her career, she will count Ning Zetao, one of China's most popular able-bodied swimmers in the 2010s, among her inspirations.

The first international results come as early as 2010, at the para swimming world championships in Eindhoven, in the Netherlands. At fourteen, she returns to China with a silver and a bronze medal in freestyle, plus gold in the medley relay. Two years later, she discovers the Paralympic Games, in London: silver in the 100-meter breaststroke (SB5), bronze in the 400-meter freestyle (S6), fourth place in the 100-meter freestyle. A solid first tally for a teenager not yet twenty. Three years later, at the world championships in Glasgow, however, she leaves without a medal, fourth and fifth in her finals — a reminder that progress, at this level, is never linear.

It is in Rio, the following year, that her career changes dimension. Besides the gold and the world record in the 100-meter backstroke, she leaves the Games with a second medal, silver in the 400-meter freestyle, an event in which she had already posted the second-fastest time in qualifying. In a few days, Song Lingling establishes herself among the best para swimmers in the world, and becomes one of the leading figures of the Chinese Paralympic delegation, traditionally one of the most successful at the Summer Games. The path that has taken her from a municipal pool in Liaoning to the top of the Paralympic podium is contained, in a little under ten years, in this double won in Brazil.

The year 2017 confirms this rise. At the world championships in Mexico City, she wins two titles, in the 400-meter freestyle and the 100-meter breaststroke. Two years later, in London, the city where she had made her Paralympic debut in 2012, she takes silver in the 100-meter backstroke, beaten by Germany's Verena Schott while still holding, at that point, the world record in the event. A rare defeat, but one more medal in an already long record for a discipline so physically demanding, where the gap between the best often comes down to hundredths of a second.

At the Tokyo Games, held in 2021 after a one-year postponement due to the Covid-19 pandemic, Song Lingling reaches several finals, in the 100-meter breaststroke and the 400-meter freestyle, without leaving with a medal. She is then twenty-five, with a decade of international competition behind her, three Paralympic appearances and several world titles to her name — enough to remain, despite the absence of a podium in Tokyo, one of the leading names in her discipline in China.

This trajectory, begun in a Shenyang pool by a teenager whom polio had deprived of the use of her legs, has earned Song Lingling recognition that goes beyond sport: she has received the National May Day Labor Medal, a distinction awarded by the All-China Federation of Trade Unions to workers and public figures whose commitment is judged exemplary. An official tribute, far from the pools, for a career built in a decade from an almost trivial tryout, one day in 2009, in a municipal swimming pool in northeastern China.
