Andhra Pradesh, mid-2000s. In a state courtroom, a boy of about fifteen waits, alongside his family, for the outcome of an unusual hearing: at stake is whether he has the right to enroll in the science stream in high school. The rules of the time forbid it to blind students, deemed unable to follow physics or chemistry classes. After six months of legal proceedings brought against his own school, the ruling comes down in his favor. Srikanth Bolla becomes the first blind student allowed to study the sciences in the state of Andhra Pradesh.

He is born on July 7, 1991, in Seetharamapuram, near Machilipatnam, into a family of Telugu smallholder farmers. His parents discover, at his first bath, that his eyes do not open: their son is blind from birth. In the village, some relatives are said to have advised them to abandon him. They make the opposite choice: while still a child, Srikanth is sent to a school for the visually impaired in Hyderabad, where he learns Braille, English, and computing, and distinguishes himself in debate as well as in adapted chess. He earns a distinction in his Class 10 exams, then scores 98% in his Class 12 board exams — results that, by any logic, should have opened the doors of the country's best engineering schools.

It doesn't happen. India's elite institutions, the IITs first among them, turn down his application. Srikanth Bolla then applies abroad and receives offers from Stanford, Berkeley, and Carnegie Mellon. He finally chooses the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, entering the Sloan School of Management in 2009 — becoming the first blind international student in the institution's history. He will later sum up that choice in a line picked up by the Indian press: "If IIT didn't want me, I didn't want IIT either," he told the BBC, in remarks reported by Business Today.

Once he graduates, Srikanth Bolla returns to India rather than staying in the United States. In 2012, in Hyderabad, he co-founds Bollant Industries with Ravi Mantha, with starting capital of about $19,000. The company makes eco-friendly disposable packaging — products made from areca leaves, kraft paper recycled from municipal waste, reprocessed used plastic — under a model that aims to be zero-waste, going so far as to recycle the effluents of neighboring pharmaceutical plants. It makes hiring people with disabilities a founding principle rather than a side policy: a year earlier, in 2011, Srikanth Bolla had already helped create the Samanvai Center, a support organization for children with multiple disabilities, foreshadowing the social orientation of his later ventures.

Growth is fast: Bollant Industries claims average monthly growth on the order of 20% since its founding. In 2016, its revenue is close to Rs 10 crore and the company is aiming for Rs 100 crore by 2018. That same year, 2016, it raises funds from a group of investors that includes the industrialist Ratan Tata, alongside Srini Raju (Peepul Capital) and Satish Reddy (Dr Reddy's Laboratories). According to available data, revenue does indeed reach about Rs 150 crore (nearly $18 million) in 2018. More recent articles in the Indian press, published in 2025, mention a valuation of around Rs 500 crore and annual revenue above Rs 100 crore — figures that no independent source makes it possible to date or confirm with certainty. The company today claims several hundred employees, most of them people with disabilities.

Srikanth Bolla's story reaches beyond Indian business circles in May 2024, with the release of the film Srikanth, directed by Tushar Hiranandani. Rajkummar Rao plays the title role, alongside Jyothika and Alaya F; Sharad Kelkar plays Ravi Mantha, his business partner. The film takes in about Rs 63 crore worldwide and brings his journey to an audience well beyond the business and nonprofit circles that were already following him, from his selection for Forbes 30 Under 30 Asia in 2017 to the Surge Impact Foundation, which he set up in 2016 to support other Indian social entrepreneurs.

In 2025, Srikanth Bolla joins the panel of Shark Tank India for its fourth season, alongside investors such as Kunal Bahl and Viraj Bahl. Facing founders who have come to pitch their ideas, the man India's top engineering schools once rejected is now the one who weighs, numbers in mind, the viability of other people's businesses — a position he holds without ever dwelling on what, for so long, had counted against him before academic panels: his blindness.
