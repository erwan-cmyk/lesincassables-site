On the Isis, in Oxford, a young man sits at the helm of a rowing boat, barking orders at the crew. It's the early 1960s: Stephen Hawking doesn't row, he coxes — a position that calls for a strong voice and a cool head rather than physical strength. A college boatman remembered him as somewhere else entirely, working out equations in his head. The unexpected role turns the rather solitary student into a popular figure on campus. No one, at the time, imagines what his life will become.

Stephen William Hawking is born on January 8, 1942, in Oxford, where his parents had settled to escape the bombing of London. His father, Frank, is a tropical medicine researcher; his mother, Isobel, was one of the few women to study at Oxford in the 1930s. The eldest of four children, Stephen grows up in St Albans, where he tinkers with friends on a homemade computer built from spare parts. At his local school, he's already nicknamed "Einstein."

In 1959, at seventeen, he enters University College, Oxford. He would have preferred mathematics; his father saw him as a doctor; physics becomes the compromise. His Oxford years are marked by minimal effort — barely an hour of studying a day, he would later recall — and an intense social life, driven by rowing. He nonetheless earns a first-class degree in 1962, then heads to Cambridge for a PhD in cosmology, hoping to work with Fred Hoyle; he ends up under the supervision of Dennis Sciama instead.

It's at Cambridge, shortly after his twenty-first birthday, that everything changes. Growing clumsiness, then a fall on an ice rink, alarm his family: his mother takes him to Guy's Hospital in London. Two weeks of tests lead to a diagnosis of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), also known as motor neurone disease. Doctors give him only about two years to live. Hawking goes through a period of despair, then finds his footing again, buoyed by his budding relationship with Jane Wilde, whom he had just met. Decades later, he would look back on that moment: "My expectations were reduced to zero when I was 21," he told the New York Times in 2004.

He completes his thesis, Properties of Expanding Universes, in 1966. With Roger Penrose, he shows that general relativity implies the universe must have originated from a singularity, lending theoretical support to the Big Bang. In 1974, he's elected a Fellow of the Royal Society at 32; that same year, he publishes a discovery in Nature that upends theoretical physics: black holes aren't perfectly black — they emit radiation, now known as "Hawking radiation" — and could, over immense timescales, evaporate entirely. The disease, meanwhile, keeps advancing: crutches first, then a wheelchair by the late 1960s, and speech that grows increasingly hard to understand through the 1970s.

In 1979, Hawking is appointed Lucasian Professor of Mathematics at Cambridge, the chair once held by Isaac Newton. In 1985, pneumonia and the emergency tracheotomy that saves his life cost him the use of his voice. A California engineer supplies him with a word-selection system linked to a speech synthesizer; the American-accented voice he's given then remains his own for the rest of his life — he had come to identify with it, he would say. It's with this device, letter by letter, that he dictates A Brief History of Time, published in 1988: the book stays on bestseller lists for more than four years and sells millions of copies.

His private life follows a rockier course. His marriage to Jane Wilde, celebrated in July 1965, produces three children: Robert (1967), Lucy (1970), and Timothy (1979). Years of demanding caregiving eventually wear the couple down; they separate in 1990 and divorce in 1995. That same year, Hawking marries Elaine Mason, one of his nurses; this second marriage, marked by tension, ends in divorce in 2006, following allegations of mistreatment that he always denied. He later reconnects with Jane and their children, co-writing children's science novels with his daughter Lucy.

Two episodes capture his temperament well. Around the mid-1970s, he bets physicist Kip Thorne that the star Cygnus X-1 is not a black hole — a kind of insurance policy, in case his own work on black holes turned out to be for nothing; he eventually concedes defeat and pays up with the promised subscription to Penthouse. In April 2007, at sixty-five, he experiences a zero-gravity flight, freed for a few minutes from his wheelchair: "Space, here I come," he says afterward. Five years later, he opens the London Paralympic Games with these words: "Look up at the stars and not down at your feet."

Forced to step down from the Lucasian chair in 2009 due to age, he continues his research at the Centre for Theoretical Cosmology, founded two years earlier, and delivers his final public address at the Cambridge Union in November 2017. He dies on March 14, 2018, at his home in Cambridge, at 76 — the anniversary of Einstein's birth. His own birth, he liked to point out, had come exactly three hundred years after Galileo's death. His ashes now rest at Westminster Abbey, between the graves of Newton and Darwin. Diagnosed at 21 with two years to live, Stephen Hawking lived fifty-five more, during which he changed how we understand black holes and the origins of the universe.
