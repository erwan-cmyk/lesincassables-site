One morning in October 2009, a helicopter flies over Manhattan for twenty minutes. On board, a man looks at the city without writing anything down, without taking a single photograph. A few hours later, in a studio at the Pratt Institute in Brooklyn, he starts to draw. Over several days, watched by the public and by a webcam streaming the scene live, the towers of Manhattan reappear one by one on a canvas several meters long, to scale, down to the windows of the buildings. The man's name is Stephen Wiltshire. He has just redrawn from memory one of the densest cities in the world, after a single flyover.

Stephen Wiltshire was born on April 24, 1974, in London. His parents emigrated from the British West Indies: his father Colvin, from Barbados, and his mother Geneva, from Saint Lucia. The family settles in Maida Vale, in west London. Colvin Wiltshire dies in a motorcycle accident when Stephen is only three — the same year a diagnosis lands that will, for a long time, shape the way others see him: autism.

At the time, that diagnosis closes more doors than it opens. Young Stephen does not speak a word before the age of five. This total silence worries his family and his doctors, who struggle to gauge what is going on behind it.

Everything shifts at Queensmill, a special school in London, which he enters at five. His teachers notice his obsession with drawing — animals first, then cars — and use it as a bridge to him. One day, his drawing materials are taken away. For the first time, Stephen speaks: he asks for paper. "I needed some paper to draw on," he would explain years later. His speech still takes several more years to settle in fully; his drawing never leaves him again.

The child's talent explodes quickly. At eight, his sketch of Salisbury Cathedral becomes his first official commission, bought by the former British prime minister Edward Heath. Sir Hugh Casson, then president of the Royal Academy, describes him as "possibly the best child artist in Britain." At ten, his series of drawings "London Alphabet" details the capital's landmarks. In 1987, a BBC documentary brings him to public attention; the same year, at thirteen, his first book, Drawings, is published.

Three more books follow: Cities in 1989, during a stay in New York where he meets the neurologist Oliver Sacks, who would recount their relationship in An Anthropologist on Mars; Floating Cities in 1991, a number one bestseller in the United Kingdom; then American Dream in 1993. In barely a few years, Stephen Wiltshire becomes one of the most talked-about artists of his generation.

From 2005 on, he refines what becomes his signature: the urban panorama, drawn from memory after a single helicopter flight. Tokyo yields one of his largest canvases, completed over several days. In Rome, he renders the Vatican and St. Peter's Basilica from memory. In Hong Kong, a ten-meter panorama is finished in a week and given to the city's residents for Christmas. London, Dubai, Jerusalem, Sydney, Shanghai, Mexico City, Istanbul and Doha follow: dozens of cities captured after a single pass overhead.

The year 2006 brings a double recognition. Stephen Wiltshire opens his permanent gallery at the Royal Opera Arcade, a step from Piccadilly, in the heart of London. That same year, he is made a Member of the Order of the British Empire (MBE) for services to art, an honor presented by Prince Charles. The New York panorama born at the Pratt Institute would later find its place at the Empire State Building, where it is permanently on display.

Now 52, Stephen Wiltshire keeps drawing and traveling. In December 2024, he is the guest artist on the Space2Sea expedition to Antarctica. In April 2025, he draws a New York panorama live from the Ferris wheel at the American Dream mall in New Jersey, for a day devoted to autism awareness. His sister Annette, who has managed his career for decades, sums up their bond in a single sentence: "I wouldn't change him for anything," she told the American network CBS News.

Asked one day what he would change about his life, Stephen Wiltshire answered without hesitation: "Nothing really." Then he added what says who he is better than any diagnosis could: "I love strolling the streets, making up new ideas, buying my magazines, and drawing."
