That night in 1963, on the stage of the Regal Theater in Chicago, the band backing the Motortown Revue tour has already struck up the exit music. The musicians are packing up their instruments; a substitute, Joe Swift, has just picked up the bass. On the live recording that will be released a few weeks later, you can hear him shout "What key? What key?" as a thirteen-year-old bounds onto the stage for an encore nobody had planned. The improvised number, "Fingertips (Part 2)," climbs to number one on the Billboard Hot 100 in August 1963: no solo artist that young had ever reached the top spot. The boy's name is Stevland Judkins. On record sleeves, he's already known as Little Stevie Wonder.

Thirteen years earlier, on May 13, 1950, he was born in Saginaw, Michigan, about six weeks premature. As was standard practice at the time for very premature infants, the newborn is placed in an incubator with a high concentration of oxygen — a practice whose consequences for the retinas of premature babies medicine at the time did not yet fully grasp. Within a few weeks, this excess oxygen causes him retinopathy of prematurity: the still-immature blood vessels in his retina develop abnormally, leading to retinal detachment. Stevland Judkins goes blind before he's even two months old. Decades later, he would tell Oprah Winfrey about the brutality of that neonatal ward: "A girl who was born one minute before me actually died. She couldn't withstand that much oxygen."

His mother, Lula Mae Hardaway, separates from his father when he's four and moves with her three children to Detroit. Young Stevland sings in the choir at Whitestone Baptist Church, where he becomes a soloist at eight. He teaches himself piano, harmonica, and then drums, by ear. It's Ronnie White, of the Miracles, who discovers him and brings him, along with his mother, to Motown's headquarters. The label's boss, Berry Gordy, signs the eleven-year-old to the Tamla label; producer Clarence Paul renames him Little Stevie Wonder. Two years later comes the Regal Theater moment, and his career takes off.

In 1971, on the eve of his twenty-first birthday, Wonder renegotiates his contract with Motown — terms considered unprecedented for the label at the time — and, between 1972 and 1976, releases four albums that redefine American soul music: Talking Book, Innervisions, Fulfillingness' First Finale, and Songs in the Key of Life. He plays most of the keyboards himself, backed by Malcolm Cecil and Robert Margouleff, the duo behind TONTO, a giant modular synthesizer he discovers that year and brings on as associate producers. He wins the Grammy for Album of the Year three years running — a feat no artist has matched since — and becomes the first Black musician honored in that category.

He also puts his fame to work for causes beyond music. In 1980, his song "Happy Birthday" anchors a campaign to make Martin Luther King Jr.'s birthday a federal holiday in the United States; the following year, he organizes a rally in Washington that helps gather six million signatures. Ronald Reagan signs the law in November 1983. On the technology side, Wonder becomes, in 1976, the first user of the Kurzweil Reading Machine, capable of converting printed text into synthesized speech, then works with inventor Ray Kurzweil to develop a synthesizer, the Kurzweil K250, unveiled in 1984.

Asked by The Guardian in 2012 how he experiences his disability, he answers without hesitation: "Do you know, it's funny, but I never thought of being blind as a disadvantage, and I never thought of being black as a disadvantage. I am what I am. I love me!"

More than sixty years after the Regal Theater, Stevie Wonder remains the most awarded male solo artist in Grammy history, with twenty-five trophies and a Grammy Lifetime Achievement Award received in 1996. Add to that an Academy Award for Best Original Song in 1985, induction into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame in 1989, the Kennedy Center Honors in 1999, and the Presidential Medal of Freedom, presented by Barack Obama in 2014. More than one hundred million records sold worldwide, and still the same trajectory, traced from an incubator in Saginaw: a child who went blind within weeks of birth, who has spent his life making the world heard.
