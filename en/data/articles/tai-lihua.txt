On the evening of February 8, 2005, before several hundred million viewers gathered around the New Year's gala of Chinese state television CCTV, a curtain rises on twenty-one dancers dressed in gold, arranged in a single line. Arm against arm, they gradually compose the image of a thousand-handed deity, Guanyin, the figure of compassion in the Buddhist tradition. At their head, a young woman named Tai Lihua leads every movement with such precision that no one in the audience guesses that neither she nor her companions can hear a single note of the music accompanying them. Within a few minutes, "Thousand-Hand Bodhisattva" becomes one of the most talked-about acts in the history of Chinese television, and Tai Lihua, until then known only to dance enthusiasts, a national figure.

Tai Lihua was born in November 1976 in Yichang, in Hubei province, in central China. At the age of two, a high fever earns her an injection of streptomycin, an antibiotic with well-documented ototoxic effects: the treatment saves her, but destroys her hearing. Her parents notice only three years later, when the five-year-old stands motionless in the middle of a sound-based game the other neighborhood children are playing, unable to understand why she alone does not react to the signal everyone else hears.

She grows up in a silent world, attending a school for deaf children, where she discovers an unexpected taste for dance: she would later recount feeling a deep joy the day the rhythmic vibrations of a drum passed through her body for the first time. Spotted for her physical abilities, she is selected as a teenager by a Hubei association for people with disabilities, before joining the company that will become the China Disabled People's Performing Art Troupe, attached to the China Disabled Persons' Federation, a state body. The years of training are demanding: deprived of any sound cues, she learns to synchronize every gesture with the signals of her teachers stationed at the edge of the stage and with the vibrations of the floor transmitted by the percussion. At the same time, she pursues an academic education and, at the end of the 1990s, earns a degree in decoration and design from the Hubei Institute of Fine Arts.

Her international career begins early. As early as 1992, she performs on the stage of the Teatro alla Scala in Milan. Eight years later, in 2000, she dances at Carnegie Hall in New York before representatives of dozens of countries gathered for an international event. According to China Daily, she remains to this day the only Chinese dancer to have performed at both Carnegie Hall and La Scala, and the poster for her New York performance is said to be the only one from China ever displayed in the hall. Her training owes much to the dancer and choreographer Yang Liping, whose signature piece "Spirit of the Peacock" she performs, as well as to the choreographer Zhang Jigang. Yang Liping is said to have confided, after watching her dance, that she doubted she could herself execute the choreography with such accuracy if she had to contend with the same disability — a remark reported, in slightly varying terms, by several Chinese media outlets.

It is Zhang Jigang who conceives, at the turn of the 2000s, "Thousand-Hand Bodhisattva," designed for an ensemble of deaf dancers whose arms, through an interplay of positioning and light, suggest the multiplying hands of the deity. According to several Chinese sources, the piece is first presented in 2004, at the closing ceremony of the Athens Paralympic Games, before achieving immense fame when it is broadcast at the CCTV gala in February 2005. Rebroadcast and discussed for months across the country, the act makes Tai Lihua one of the most recognized faces of disability in China.

Since then, she has taken over the artistic direction of the troupe, which she leads today, and sits as a member of the National Committee of the Chinese People's Political Consultative Conference, an advisory body of the Chinese state, where she puts forward proposals on the education of deaf children and on access to culture for people with disabilities. Over the seasons, she has trained several generations of dancers called upon to take over the role she held in 2005: the company now speaks of a seventh generation of performers of the Thousand-Hand Bodhisattva, trained according to the method she herself received twenty years earlier — gestures counted in sign language, vibrations felt through the floor, and a discipline of rehearsal that leaves nothing to improvisation.
