In the salons of the Congress of Vienna, in the fall of 1814, a sound always precedes his arrival: an uneven, almost syncopated step on the parquet, recognizable among all others. He is sixty years old, represents a France defeated and humiliated by two decades of Napoleonic wars, and yet it is he who, in a few months, will establish his country as an indispensable player in the negotiations redrawing Europe. Charles-Maurice de Talleyrand-Périgord has never hidden his limp. It has been with him since childhood and has, against all expectations, shaped the whole of his trajectory.

Born in 1754 into one of the oldest families of the French nobility, he is the eldest son: the one who must, logically, inherit the name and the lands and take up a military career. But very early on, a deformity affects one of his feet — a club foot, turned inward, that makes him limp for the rest of his life. In his memoirs, written decades later, Talleyrand will recount having fallen from a chest of drawers at the age of four, while in the care of a nurse. Historians today largely doubt this version. Michel Poniatowski leans toward a deformity present from birth. Emmanuel de Waresquiel cautiously puts forward a hereditary lead — an uncle is said to have suffered from the same infirmity — mentioning, among other hypotheses, a possible Marfan syndrome, without anything allowing the question to be settled. Only the club foot itself, visible and documented by all his contemporaries, is beyond doubt.

What is certain is the consequence. Deemed unfit for a military career, the young Charles-Maurice is forced by his parents to renounce his birthright in favor of his younger brother, Archambaud. In its place, a future he has not chosen is mapped out for him: the Church. At twenty-one, on receiving the subdiaconate that commits him to holy orders, he is said to have confided: "They are forcing me to be a churchman; they will regret it!" History will prove him right, though not in the way anyone might have imagined.

Ordained a priest and then appointed bishop of Autun in 1788, Talleyrand exercises his religious ministry only briefly. The French Revolution overturns everything and offers him the chance to reinvent himself. Elected to the Constituent Assembly, he abandons the cassock for politics, then for diplomacy — the trade in which he will deploy, for nearly half a century, a gift for maneuvering that his contemporaries, admiring or frightened, never stopped commenting on. Minister of foreign relations under the Directory from 1797 to 1799, he holds the same post again under the Consulate and then the Empire of Napoleon Bonaparte, from 1799 to 1807, becoming one of the chief architects of French foreign policy.

He then distances himself from the emperor, senses his fall, and negotiates behind the scenes the return of the Bourbons. Under the Restoration, from 1814, he becomes minister of foreign affairs and president of the Council of Ministers, and it is in that capacity that he leads the French delegation to the Congress of Vienna, between 1814 and 1815, where he pulls off the feat of bringing a defeated France back into the concert of the great European powers. Later still, under the July Monarchy, he ends his career as French ambassador in London, from 1830 to 1834.

Few politicians have passed through so many contradictory regimes while remaining each time at the top: absolute monarchy, the Revolution, the Directory, the Empire, the Restoration. This ability to reinvent himself without ever disappearing earns him, already in his lifetime, a reputation as a traitor that will never leave him. It comes with another, just as firmly established: that of a venal man, who sold his position throughout his career. The best-known episode remains the so-called XYZ Affair, in 1797-1798: intermediaries acting on his behalf demand bribes from three American envoys before even opening any negotiation, provoking a near-rupture in diplomatic relations between France and the United States. Talleyrand himself will later put at sixty million the sums he reportedly received in total from foreign powers over the course of his career — a venality that, while it offends today's morality, also fits within diplomatic practices inherited from the Ancien Régime, where the mixing of public and private interests remained a widely tolerated norm.

He dies in 1838, at eighty-four, having held for more than forty years some of the most exposed posts in European diplomacy — a political longevity rare for the era, rarer still for a man whose infirmity had, in childhood, condemned him to a life he had not chosen. Pushed against his will off the path laid out for the eldest son of a great family, he will have made that constraint the starting point of one of the most singular trajectories in French political history.
