Chicago, October 2009. Tatyana McFadden lines up for the marathon almost on a whim: until then, she has only raced short distances, on the track. She crosses the finish line so fast that her mother does not have time to get her camera out to capture the win. That day, nobody imagines that this improvised success is opening the way to one of the most dominant careers in the history of Paralympic sport.

Tatyana McFadden was born on April 21, 1989, in Leningrad — today's Saint Petersburg — then in the Soviet Union. She comes into the world with spina bifida, a malformation that leaves her paralyzed from the waist down. Her biological mother, Nina Polevikova, places her in the city's Orphanage No. 3, an institution too poor to provide her with a wheelchair. For six years, to get around, she walks on her hands. "I scooted around or I walked on my hands," she told the BBC in 2018.

It is in that orphanage that Deborah McFadden meets her, in 1994. Herself paralyzed since the age of 23 by Guillain-Barré syndrome, Deborah is at the time the U.S. Commissioner of Disabilities under George H. W. Bush, a post from which she helped shape the Americans with Disabilities Act. Passing through post-Soviet Russia to distribute American aid, she comes upon a girl of about five kept apart from the other children: she remembers noticing a "little girl with a bow in her hair bigger than her head," she told the BBC. Deborah comes back regularly to visit her; in 1995, learning that the girl risks being transferred to an institution for disabled children, she decides to adopt her with her partner, Bridget O'Shaughnessy. Tatyana arrives in the United States in 1996, at the age of six, to grow up in Maryland.

Doctors warn Deborah at the time: for lack of care during her first six years, Tatyana would have only a limited life expectancy. To build her up, her mother has her try several sports — swimming, gymnastics, wheelchair basketball, sled hockey, track and field. At her first session in the pool, the girl propels herself through the water and cries out "Ya sama!", which the BBC translates as "yes, I can do this." She is eight when she takes up wheelchair racing; at fifteen, she makes the U.S. team and goes off to compete at the Athens 2004 Paralympic Games.

Back from Athens with a silver medal and a bronze, the teenager simply wants to run with the track team at her high school, Atholton, in Maryland. The school administration refuses to let her compete at the same time as able-bodied students, citing safety and competitive advantage. She races separately, alone on an empty track. "Every track meet that I went to they were always booing," she told the BBC. In 2005, Tatyana and Deborah McFadden sue Howard County; they win their case the following year. Their fight leads, in 2008, to Maryland's adoption of the Fitness and Athletics Equity for Students with Disabilities Act, which requires schools to offer equal sporting opportunities to students with disabilities. The law is nicknamed "Tatyana's Law."

On Paralympic tracks, McFadden's medal haul grows edition after edition: silver and bronze in Athens in 2004, three silver medals and a bronze in Beijing in 2008, gold in the 800 meters in London in 2012 — where she also shares the 100-meter final with her sister Hannah, adopted in Albania: the first siblings in history to compete at the same Paralympic Games. Rio, Tokyo and Paris follow: in all, 22 Paralympic medals — 8 gold, 9 silver, 5 bronze — the most recent won in Paris in 2024. In 2013, at the IPC World Championships in Lyon, she becomes the first athlete to win six consecutive titles, from the 100 meters to the 5,000 meters, then wins the Boston, Chicago, London and New York marathons in the same year: she becomes the first athlete in history, able-bodied or not, to win all four major marathons in a single season. She would go on to string together four consecutive titles in London, from 2013 to 2016.

In 2014, McFadden sets herself a new kind of challenge: the Sochi Winter Paralympic Games, in Russia, her country of birth. A novice in cross-country skiing, she takes silver in the 1 km sprint, sitting category, in the presence of her American family and of her biological mother, Nina Polevikova, found again thanks to Deborah in 2011. She would later call that moment "the cherry on top," she told the BBC.

More than ten years after her first Grand Slam, Tatyana McFadden is still racing at the highest level: she now has 24 Marathon Major victories. In April 2026, at 37, she finishes second at the London Marathon, five seconds behind Switzerland's Catherine Debrunner, after finishing third in Boston a few weeks earlier. The same month, she receives the 96th AAU Sullivan Award, given each year to the most outstanding amateur athlete in the United States: she becomes only the second Paralympic athlete in history, after Jessica Long, to receive the prize in its 96 years of existence. "Sports have such a big power and voice," she says that evening, according to USA Track & Field.
