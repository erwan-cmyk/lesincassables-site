In 2009, in the garden of his house in Wiltshire, Terry Pratchett digs into the ground to pull out iron ore, mixed with fragments of meteorite that he laughingly calls thunderbolt iron. With the help of a specialist in ancient metallurgy, he smelts it in a makeshift kiln of clay and straw, then hands it over to a blacksmith: he has just been knighted by the Queen, and a knighted fantasy writer really ought to have a proper sword. "Thunderbolt iron, you see — highly magical, you've got to chuck that stuff in whether you believe in it or not," he would say. This mischievous knight has by then been living for eighteen months with a diagnosis meant to strip him, little by little, of his words.

Born on April 28, 1948, in Beaconsfield, Buckinghamshire, Terry Pratchett is not yet the satirical author of the forty-one Discworld novels when he leaves school at seventeen to become a journalist, then a press officer for nuclear power stations. His first novel appears in 1971, but it is with The Colour of Magic, in 1983, that Discworld is born, a universe that will become one of the steadiest literary successes in the United Kingdom. Nothing in that trajectory, crowned by the Order of the British Empire in 1998, hints at what is coming in 2007.

That year, at 59, Pratchett develops unusual disturbances of visual and spatial perception, at first wrongly put down to a minor stroke. Further tests reveal posterior cortical atrophy, also known as Benson's syndrome: a rare, early-onset form of Alzheimer's that attacks first the areas of the brain that handle vision and space, before reaching memory. In December 2007, he makes the diagnosis public and coins the word that will stay with him to the end: "the Embuggerance." Coarse and reticent at once, it sums up his way of facing the disease — seriously, without ever bowing to it.

Because Pratchett goes on writing. The illness attacks his ability to read first — "When you read, I'm sure you don't realize that your eyes are going backwards and forwards and to this place and that place. Mine don't do that," he would explain to NPR in 2013 — well before it touches his memory. He leans on speech recognition software and on his assistant Rob Wilkins to keep composing: "I'm a writer. What do I do? Sit there watching my wife clean up?" Between 2008 and his death, he publishes some ten books: the young adult novel Nation (2008), the Discworld novels Unseen Academicals (2009), I Shall Wear Midnight (2010), Snuff (2011, more than 55,000 copies in three days) and Raising Steam (2013), the novel Dodger (2012), and several volumes of The Long Earth series co-written with Stephen Baxter. His last novel, The Shepherd's Crown, finished by dictation shortly before his death, appears posthumously in August 2015.

In March 2008, Pratchett announces a donation to the Alzheimer's Research Trust (today Alzheimer's Research UK), of which he becomes the patron: "We need you and you need money. I'm giving you a million dollars. Spend it wisely," he declares — a gesture valued by the British press at around £500,000. Furious to learn that dementia research receives only 3 percent of the funding allocated to cancer, he campaigns for the cause and presents, in February 2009, Terry Pratchett: Living with Alzheimer's, a two-part documentary for the BBC, named best factual series at the British Academy Scotland Awards — the Scottish, regional edition of the BAFTAs.

His diagnosis pushes him to take a stand on the right to die with dignity. In February 2010, he becomes the first novelist to give the Richard Dimbleby Lecture; unable to read a text that long aloud, he has it read by the actor Tony Robinson, seated beside him. Titled Shaking Hands with Death, it argues for the right to a chosen end of life; in it Pratchett says he would like to die at home, before the disease launches its final assault, "in a chair on the lawn, with a brandy in my hand [...] and with Thomas Tallis on my iPod, I would shake hands with Death." He then funds the Commission on Assisted Dying (2010-2011) and presents, in 2011, the documentary Choosing to Die, which follows a patient all the way to his assisted death in Switzerland, at Dignitas. Broadcast in June despite fierce controversy, the film earns him, in 2012, the actual British Academy Television Award for best single documentary — and not Living with Alzheimer's, contrary to a confusion sometimes made: it is Choosing to Die that receives that national award, honored as well with an International Emmy the same year.

Terry Pratchett dies on March 12, 2015, at home in Broad Chalke, Wiltshire, surrounded by his family, his cat asleep on the bed — of a natural death linked to the complications of his illness, his publisher would specify, and not by assisted suicide, despite his advocacy for it. The news is announced on his Twitter account in three messages written in the voice of Death: "AT LAST, SIR TERRY, WE MUST WALK TOGETHER," "Terry took Death's arm and followed him through the doors and on to the black desert under the endless night," "The End." To the very end, the man who had forged his own sword out of a meteorite will have chosen the shape of his own story.
