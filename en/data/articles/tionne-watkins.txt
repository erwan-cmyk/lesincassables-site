On August 15, 2026, in Franklin, Tennessee, "It's Iconic: The Tour" opens, an American run of some thirty dates that brings TLC, Salt-N-Pepa and En Vogue together on one bill for the first time. On stage, Tionne "T-Boz" Watkins is reunited with her partner Rozonda "Chilli" Thomas to perform the hits that made them, thirty years ago, one of the best-selling girl groups in recording history. At 56, she sings and strings together dates from one end of the United States to the other, with the ease of someone who has never left the stage.

What the audience often does not know is that every T-Boz tour is also a medical balancing act. Tionne Tenese Watkins is born on April 26, 1970, in Des Moines, Iowa. Her parents, James and Gayle, divorce when she is three. Her mother raises her alone, before settling in Atlanta when Tionne is nine, after stops in California and Houston. In between, at seven, the girl is hospitalized for the first time: the diagnosis comes, sickle cell disease, a genetic condition that deforms red blood cells and blocks blood flow, causing acute pain crises and heavy complications. The doctors are pessimistic. "I was told I would never live past 30," she would tell Robin Roberts much later, on the set of Good Morning America.

In Atlanta, the teenager grows up between repeated hospital stays and an early-set determination. An apprentice in a hair salon, she wins her first competition at 14, goes through cosmetology school and works a string of odd jobs, including one at McDonald's, before music takes over. From her mother, who pushes her relentlessly, she takes away one message: "I could do anything that I set my mind to."

The trio comes together in Atlanta in the very early 1990s, first under the name Second Nature, around Tionne Watkins, Lisa "Left Eye" Lopes and Crystal Jones — the last of whom is replaced in 1991 by Rozonda "Chilli" Thomas. Spotted by the producer Perri "Pebbles" Reid, the group signs to LaFace Records, the label founded by L.A. Reid and Babyface, and takes the name TLC, from the initials of their stage nicknames: Tionne becomes "T-Boz." Their first album, Ooooooohhh... On the TLC Tip, comes out on February 25, 1992, and will be certified quadruple platinum in the United States.

Success explodes with CrazySexyCool, released on November 15, 1994: more than eight million copies certified in the United States, more than 23 million worldwide, carried by the hits "Creep" and "Waterfalls." The album earns TLC two Grammy Awards in 1996, including best R&B album. Five years later, FanMail, released on February 23, 1999, and sold in more than ten million copies worldwide, brings the group two more Grammy Awards in 2000, taking their total to four. Over the course of its career, TLC will sell more than 65 million records worldwide, becoming, according to Grammy.com, the second best-selling girl group in U.S. history, behind the Supremes.

It is at the height of that rise, in 1996, that Tionne Watkins chooses to make public an illness she had kept secret until then. She becomes a spokesperson for the Sickle Cell Disease Association of America, then for the Sickle Cell Foundation of Georgia, appears again and again at blood drives and calls for bone marrow donations within the African American community, statistically the most affected by the disease. She also creates T-Boz Unplugged, charity concerts she prefers to the usual gala dinners. In 2017, she publishes her memoir, A Sick Life: TLC 'n Me: Stories from On and Off the Stage, before launching, in late 2019, her own CBD wellness line, TLCBD, to ease her chronic pain.

Two episodes sum up the ridge she has always walked. In 2000, shortly after the birth of her daughter Chase, she collapses in the middle of an attempt to breastfeed: the loss of fluid, dangerous for a sickle cell patient, sends her into a coma. "Eventually, my body shut down and I fell into a coma," she would write in her memoir. Six years later, a brain tumor diagnosis plunges her back into uncertainty: several surgeons refuse to operate because of the risks tied to sickle cell disease. She finds a doctor willing to attempt the procedure. "I don't have time to die," she would recall thinking before going into the operating room.

From that operation she is left with a slight asymmetry in her smile and a partial loss of hearing and vision, after-effects she owns without flinching. The mother of two children, Chase and Chance, T-Boz today divides her life between Los Angeles and the tour circuit. In 2024, a hospital stay had forced her to cancel several concerts and to float the idea of easing off. Two years later, here she is on American roads with TLC, Salt-N-Pepa and En Vogue, on one of the summer's event tours — proof, every night on stage, that a disease meant to carry her off before she turned thirty has, in the end, taken nothing from her.
