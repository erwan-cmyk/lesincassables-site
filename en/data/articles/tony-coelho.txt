In November 1963, when John Fitzgerald Kennedy is assassinated in Dallas, Tony Coelho has just begun his senior year at Loyola University in Los Angeles, where he is elected student body president. The son of Portuguese immigrants, raised on the family dairy farm in Los Banos, California, he makes a decision that is meant to give his existence its whole meaning: he will be a Catholic priest. He wastes no time in starting the application process for the seminary.

Entering the seminary requires a prior medical examination. The one Coelho undergoes reveals epilepsy, a condition he did not know until then that he had. Under the canon law of the time, such a diagnosis bars access to the priesthood. In the space of a single consultation, the vocation that was supposed to organize the young man's entire life collapses.

The illness, in fact, goes back several years. At sixteen, Coelho had been in a pickup truck accident; doctors will later cite that episode as a possible trigger for his epilepsy, without asserting it with certainty. But for a long time he had lived without knowing the name of what he suffered from. The diagnosis made on the eve of the seminary abruptly changes everything: the law of the time requires that it be reported to the authorities, and Coelho promptly loses his driver's license and his health insurance. In barely a few months, the man who saw himself as a priest finds himself without a vocation, without the right to drive and without medical coverage.

It is during this period of rebuilding that he crosses paths, in 1964, with the actor and comedian Bob Hope, who encourages him to turn to politics rather than the priesthood. Coelho takes the advice: in 1965, he joins the staff of the Democratic congressman from California Bernie Sisk. He stays thirteen years, climbing the ranks to become his chief of staff, a post he holds for eight years, until Sisk's retirement in 1978.

That same year, Coelho succeeds him: elected at thirty-six in California's 15th district, he sits in the House of Representatives without interruption for six terms, from January 3, 1979, to June 15, 1989. His mastery of campaign finance carries him to the chairmanship of the Democratic Congressional Campaign Committee in the early 1980s; in late 1986, his colleagues elect him Majority Whip, the third-ranking post in the House Democratic hierarchy, responsible for counting and rallying his side's votes on decisive ballots. Twenty years earlier, canon law had closed the altar to him; now, from the corridors of the Capitol, he directs the votes that make the country's laws.

On April 29, 1988, while still in office, he introduces in the House the very first text of what will become the Americans with Disabilities Act, the landmark federal law on the rights of people with disabilities. He is its principal author and original sponsor. The text draws directly on what he has lived through: a license revoked, an insurance policy lost, a religious vocation destroyed, because of a diagnosis he had not chosen.

Coelho will not, however, see the text come to fruition from his seat. In the spring of 1989, the press reveals that he had benefited, three years earlier, from a $50,000 loan granted by a savings and loan executive to finance the purchase of $100,000 in high-yield bonds — junk bonds — for a time registered in the name of his campaign committee, without the loan having been properly disclosed. Rather than expose his party to a prolonged investigation, in the midst of the American savings and loan crisis, Coelho announces his resignation at the end of May. It takes effect on June 15, 1989, his forty-seventh birthday. No charges are brought against him.

On July 26, 1990, a little over a year after his departure from Congress, President George H. W. Bush signs the Americans with Disabilities Act. The text he had originally championed becomes the reference law in the United States on the rights of people with disabilities; four years later, the U.S. Census Bureau counts 800,000 additional jobs held by people with disabilities.

Coelho then pursues a career in finance, chairs from 1994 to 2001 the President's Committee on Employment of People with Disabilities under Bill Clinton, runs Al Gore's presidential campaign in 2000, then founds in 2018 the Coelho Center for Disability Law, Policy and Innovation at Loyola Marymount University, the institution where, half a century earlier, a medical examination had closed the door of the seminary to a twenty-one-year-old student. Honored twice by the Epilepsy Foundation, whose board of directors he chaired before remaining a member, he today chairs the Partnership to Improve Patient Care, a coalition devoted to patient advocacy.
