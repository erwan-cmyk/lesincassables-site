For thirteen days in November 1989, Prague's Laterna Magika theater becomes the nerve center of a revolution. In its backstage rooms, turned into a permanent meeting hall, a playwright whose face bears the marks of years in prison is part of almost every decision. On November 24, he addresses from a balcony a crowd of 300,000 people gathered in Wenceslas Square. A month later, on December 29, 1989, Václav Havel and his wife Olga wave to the citizens from Prague Castle: the Federal Assembly has just named him president of Czechoslovakia.

Nothing in the rules of the communist game prepared this man for such a destiny. Born on October 5, 1936, in Prague into a wealthy family — his grandfather developed Wenceslas Square, his father built the Barrandov Terraces — Václav Havel carries throughout his youth a social background deemed bourgeois, which under the communist regime closes the doors of higher education in the humanities to him. He becomes an apprentice in a chemistry laboratory, takes evening classes, then enters the theater as a stagehand before starting to write.

His plays, driven by a keen sense of the absurd, ridicule the wooden language of power. The Garden Party, first staged in 1963, and then The Memorandum, two years later, earn him international recognition. After the invasion of Czechoslovakia by Warsaw Pact troops in 1968, his texts are banned from the national stages but continue to be produced abroad, notably in the United States. Prevented from practicing his craft at home, Havel then shifts toward a more direct form of engagement.

On January 6, 1977, he is among the ten founders of Charter 77, a text demanding respect for the human rights promised by the Helsinki Accords and signed by 242 people. He is arrested while attempting to deliver it to the Federal Assembly, alongside the writers Ludvík Vaculík and Pavel Landovský. Other arrests follow. In October 1979, he is tried for subversion with five other members of the Committee for the Defense of the Unjustly Persecuted, then imprisoned until 1983 — nearly four years, during which he writes to his wife Olga the letters posterity will remember under the title Letters to Olga.

A decade later, in a matter of weeks, the regime collapses and the former prisoner becomes head of state. Reelected in 1990, Havel resigns on July 20, 1992, so as not to endorse the partition of Czechoslovakia, which he fought without managing to prevent. The country splits on January 1, 1993, into the Czech Republic and Slovakia. A month later, on February 2, 1993, he becomes the first president of the Czech Republic, then is reelected and completes two full terms, until February 2, 2003 — thirteen years in all at the head of two successive states.

This decade in power is compounded by a series of personal ordeals. On January 27, 1996, Olga Havlová, his wife since the early 1960s and his closest ally during the years of dissidence, dies of cancer at 62. Eleven months later, in December 1996, doctors detect in Havel himself a malignant tumor of about 15 millimeters on the right lung, at an early stage, in this former heavy smoker who had cut down without entirely giving up. On December 2, the surgeon Pavel Pasko operates on him for three and a half hours and removes half of the affected lung. The president resumes his duties a few weeks later.

The respite is short-lived. Around 1998, the cancer reappears; this time, Havel quits smoking for good. The same year, on vacation in Austria, a ruptured colon forces him to undergo an emergency colostomy in Innsbruck. Between the bereavement, the recurrence and this abdominal emergency, the man then leading the Czech Republic goes through one hospitalization after another without ever leaving the presidency.

After the Castle, Havel remains a public voice. He founds Forum 2000, dedicated to international democratic dialogue, and continues to defend human rights around the world.

The decline, for its part, continues more discreetly. A week before his death, in December 2011, he receives the Dalai Lama in Prague, in a wheelchair, gaunt but present. Václav Havel dies on December 18, 2011, at 75, at his country house in Hrádeček. A state funeral is held in St. Vitus Cathedral; Prague's international airport today bears his name.

In thirteen years at the head of two states, Václav Havel will not have interrupted his duties for any of these ordeals: not the bereavement, not the cancer, not its recurrence, nor the surgical emergency that followed. He completed his second term in 2003, ten years after beginning it, in the same Castle where the crowd, one evening in December 1989, had chanted his name.
