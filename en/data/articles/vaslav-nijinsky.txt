That evening in late May 1913, at the Théâtre des Champs-Élysées, the audience starts whistling before the first bars are even over. Laughter spreads through the rows, then boos. The police escort some forty spectators out; Diaghilev has the house lights switched on and off to calm the hall, in vain. In the wings, Vaslav Nijinsky is said to have climbed onto a chair to shout the count to dancers who can no longer hear the orchestra over the din. On stage, feet turned inwards, arms bent at right angles: in a single evening, The Rite of Spring has just tipped the history of Western dance. Nijinsky is its choreographer. He is already, in the eyes of his peers, one of the greatest dancers of his generation, the one said to hang in the air longer than anyone else.

Vaslav Nijinsky is born in Kyiv, in the Russian Empire — today the capital of Ukraine — on March 12, 1889 according to most biographers, though some sources give 1890. His parents, Thomas Nijinsky and Eleonora Bereda, are both professional dancers of Polish origin, leading a touring life across the Russian Empire. Polish parents, a birthplace now in Ukraine, training and fame acquired in imperial Russia, an international career then built in Paris: no single country can claim Nijinsky's story as its own. Admitted at around the age of nine to the Imperial Ballet School in Saint Petersburg, he graduates in 1907, one of the best of his year.

Spotted by Prince Pavel Lvov, Nijinsky meets Sergei Diaghilev in 1908, who the following year brings him into his brand-new Paris-based company, the Ballets Russes. The two men become lovers; Diaghilev takes charge of his dancer's career down to the smallest detail. Nijinsky goes from one role that marks the history of ballet to the next — Petrushka, Scheherazade, Le Spectre de la rose — before turning to choreography. L'Après-midi d'un faune (Afternoon of a Faun), created in 1912 to music by Debussy, already shocks with its final gesture; the following year, The Rite of Spring seals his reputation as a radical innovator, even at the cost of offending the public.

In September 1913, during a South American tour in which Diaghilev has not taken part, Nijinsky marries the Hungarian dancer Romola de Pulszky in Buenos Aires. Furious to learn the news by telegram, Diaghilev dismisses him from the company. Nijinsky, who has never had a contract or a fixed salary, finds himself without a troupe overnight. Interned as a Russian national in Austria-Hungary at the start of the war, he is released thanks to international negotiations and briefly goes back on tour in the United States in 1916, before giving, on September 30, 1917 in Montevideo, what will remain his last professional performance.

Settled with his wife and daughter in St. Moritz, Switzerland, Nijinsky gradually withdraws from the world. On January 19, 1919, in the ballroom of the Suvretta House hotel, he gives before a society audience what will remain his only, and last, public recital. "This is my marriage with God," he is said to have told Romola before going on. The improvisation, performed without music, would be seen in hindsight by several witnesses as the visible sign of a crisis already under way.

Six weeks later, on March 6, 1919, at the Burghölzli clinic near Zurich, Professor Eugen Bleuler — who had himself coined the term "schizophrenia" a few years earlier — examines Nijinsky. His report concludes: "Intelligence evidently very good in the past, now he is a confused schizophrenic with mild manic excitement." This diagnosis, one of the best documented in the psychiatry of its time, will be confirmed by other major figures in the discipline, including Ludwig Binswanger, who takes over his care. From January to March 1919, Nijinsky himself kept three notebooks, describing hour by hour his visions and his anxieties — a document psychiatrists today regard as one of the most precise autobiographical accounts ever written of the onset of psychosis.

So begins a seclusion of nearly thirty years, between Swiss, French, Hungarian and English clinics, in Romola's care. Nijinsky never sets foot on a stage again. In the 1930s, several renowned psychiatrists take up his case in turn, including Carl Jung, Julius Wagner-Jauregg and Alfred Adler, without achieving any lasting improvement. Between 1938 and 1940, a course of insulin shock therapy brings him a temporary and spectacular improvement — for a while he is seen sketching, almost in spite of himself, a few dance movements — before a relapse closes that interlude.

Nijinsky dies in London on April 8, 1950, of kidney failure. First buried in London, his body is transferred in 1953 to Montmartre Cemetery, in Paris, where a statue depicts him in his role as Petrushka, the broken puppet he had made famous nearly forty years earlier. Of that professional career of less than a decade and those thirty years of silence, there remain photographs, a few seconds of film, harrowing notebooks, and the certainty, shared by generations of dancers, that perhaps no other body has ever explored so closely the limits of the leap and of weightlessness.
