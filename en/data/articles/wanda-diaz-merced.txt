On April 8, 2024, while millions of people put on protective glasses to watch the Moon slide across the Sun, Wanda Díaz-Merced stays seated at her desk, in Puerto Rico. She does not look up at the sky. She listens. In her ears, a small box called LightSound translates the ambient light into sound: rapid clicks in full daylight, which slow, thin out, then almost fade away at the moment of totality, before picking up their pace again. Asked about it later by American public radio NPR, she sums up what she has just lived through: "I didn't have to go to the natural phenomena. The natural phenomena came to me inside the room." The Puerto Rican astrophysicist has been blind since her early twenties. The device she is listening to that day, she has spent nearly twenty years making possible.

Wanda Díaz-Merced grows up in Gurabo, a small town in the interior of Puerto Rico. As a child, she one day sees a meteor streak across the sky; her father, who never went beyond primary school, describes it to her simply as a stone fallen from the sky, with no mystical detour. The scene leaves a lasting mark and feeds an early fascination with astronomy, even if she first considers studying medicine before switching to physics, at the University of Puerto Rico's Río Piedras campus.

It is during those student years that diabetes sets in and, through complications, begins to damage her retina. The loss of sight advances in fits and starts throughout her bachelor's degree: she keeps attending classes, retakes some of them, hangs on. It will take her six years, instead of the usual four, to earn her physics degree. By her early twenties, she is completely blind.

For a science student, the news amounts to a sentence: the core of an astronomer's work rests on reading light curves, spectra, graphs. Without sight, how to go on interpreting data designed for eyes? The breakthrough comes from a fellow student, an amateur radio astronomy enthusiast involved in NASA's Radio Jove project, who one day plays her the recording of a solar flare captured in radio waves and transposed into audible frequencies. Díaz-Merced then understands that a light curve is, at bottom, nothing but a series of numbers — and that a series of numbers can be translated into just about anything, including sound.

In 2005, she does a summer internship at NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center, in Maryland. The heliophysicist Robert Candey, who supervises her, encourages her to take the view that she must be able to explore data as deeply as any sighted astronomer. From that encounter comes xSonify, one of the first pieces of software able to convert astrophysical signals — light curves, X-ray or gamma-ray bursts — into variations in pitch, loudness and duration. Díaz-Merced makes it the foundation of her doctoral thesis, defended in 2013 at the University of Glasgow under the supervision of Stephen Brewster.

What could have remained a tool of individual compensation soon proves more ambitious. Díaz-Merced has her sonified data tested by seasoned sighted astronomers, tasked with spotting faint signals buried in noise: pairing sound with image improves the detection of events the eye alone sometimes misses. Sonification then ceases to be a mere workaround for disability and becomes a research instrument in its own right, of interest to an entire scientific community, well beyond blind or visually impaired researchers.

Her career then takes her to the Harvard-Smithsonian Center for Astrophysics, to the South African Astronomical Observatory, in Cape Town, then to the National Astronomical Observatory of Japan. Since 2014, she has led the AstroSense project at the International Astronomical Union's Office of Astronomy for Development, charged with spreading these accessible methods through astronomy programs around the world. In 2016, she tells her story in a TED talk in Vancouver, where she argues for a science that excludes no one on account of a sense they happen to lack.

It is in that same vein that she designs, in 2017, the LightSound device, with the astronomer Allyson Bieryla, who runs Harvard's astronomy lab. Originally conceived for the total eclipse that crosses the United States that year, the box has since been rebuilt and distributed free of charge by the hundreds at every major eclipse — including the one in April 2024 — to let blind and visually impaired people around the world follow live, through sound, a phenomenon until then reserved for the eyes.

Since September 2023, Wanda Díaz-Merced has directed the Arecibo C3 center, a body devoted to science education in Puerto Rico, while teaching at the Universidad del Sagrado Corazón, in San Juan, where she has set up one of the first university courses in astrophysics and cosmology designed to be followed with all the senses at once. Twenty years after believing she had lost access to the stars, she goes on training a new generation of researchers to listen to what only the universe has ever been able to show.
