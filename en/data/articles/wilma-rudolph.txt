Rome, September 8, 1960. On the final straight of the 4x100-meter relay, a twenty-year-old American closes her hand around the baton her teammate Williams has just handed her — the exchange is botched, the baton nearly drops. The stadium holds its breath. In a split second she recovers it, straightens up and throws her legs toward the line, passing the last German runner at exactly the right moment. Wilma Rudolph has just won her third gold medal of these Olympic Games — becoming the first American woman to pull off such a treble in track and field at a single Olympics. That evening, the Italian press nicknames her "La Gazzella Nera," the French press "La Perle Noire"; she is hailed as the fastest woman in the world.

Twenty years earlier, nothing hinted at such a scene. Wilma Glodean Rudolph is born prematurely on June 23, 1940, in Saint Bethlehem, a hamlet since absorbed into Clarksville, Tennessee. She weighs a little over two kilos at birth and grows up in a large family, the twentieth of the twenty-two children born of her father's two marriages. Her childhood is fragile: double pneumonia, scarlet fever, then, around the age of five, polio, which leaves her left leg and foot lastingly weakened.

With no facilities available for Black patients in Clarksville in the 1940s, her mother takes her every week, for two years, by bus to Nashville — eighty kilometers away — for treatment sessions at Meharry Medical College. At home, the family takes turns several times a day massaging her leg and removing her brace. "My doctors told me I would never walk again. My mother told me I would. I believed my mother," she would say later. She wears a brace and then an orthopedic shoe until she is about eleven or twelve; past that age, she walks with no appliance at all.

Freed from her leg, the former sick child throws herself into sports. At Burt High School in Clarksville, she is a revelation at basketball: her coach, C. C. Gray, nicknames her "Skeeter" — mosquito — she moves so fast on the court. In her sophomore year she scores 803 points in the season, a record for girls' high school basketball. It is on that same court that Ed Temple, the track coach at Tennessee State University, spots her and invites her, at fourteen, to join his summer training program: she becomes a "Tigerbelle."

At sixteen, still in high school, she is eliminated in the heats of the 200 meters at the 1956 Melbourne Olympics, but takes bronze in the 4x100-meter relay, the American team equaling the world record in 44.9 seconds. She then continues her studies and her training at Tennessee State, under Temple's direction.

Four years later, in Rome, she dominates the Games: the 100 meters first, in exactly 11 seconds, an extraordinary time but not ratified as a world record because of an over-favorable wind — she nonetheless becomes the first American woman since Helen Stephens, in 1936, to win gold at that distance. Then the 200 meters, where she sets an Olympic record of 23.2 seconds in the heats before taking the final. Then, finally, that cliffhanger of a relay, which seals her third crown.

Back in Clarksville, her town organizes a parade in her honor. Wilma Rudolph refuses to attend if it remains segregated by skin color: the town gives way, and the event becomes the first integrated public gathering in its history. In May 1963, she takes part in a demonstration to desegregate a Clarksville restaurant; the mayor announces soon afterward the integration of all municipal public facilities.

In 1962, after fresh victories in the 100 meters and the relay at a United States-USSR meet at Stanford, she retires from competition at twenty-two, at the top. "If I won two gold medals, there would be something lacking. I'll stick with the glory I've already won like Jesse Owens did in 1936," she explains. A Tennessee State graduate in 1963, she becomes a second-grade teacher at Cobb Elementary School, then a track coach at Burt High School — the school that had seen her start out.

In the decades that follow, she goes on passing it down: in 1981, she founds the Wilma Rudolph Foundation in Indianapolis to train young athletes, directs the women's track program at DePauw University and covers the 1984 Olympic Games for the ABC network. Her achievements earn her the Sullivan Award as early as 1960, then inductions into the Black Sports Hall of Fame, the National Track and Field Hall of Fame and the US Olympic Hall of Fame; a US postage stamp bearing her image is issued in 2004.

Wilma Rudolph dies of brain cancer on November 12, 1994, in Brentwood, Tennessee, at fifty-four. Decades earlier, a little girl in Tennessee wore a brace and doctors doubted she would ever walk normally again; grown into a woman, she had run faster than any other before her, and changed, on her way past, the history of her town.
