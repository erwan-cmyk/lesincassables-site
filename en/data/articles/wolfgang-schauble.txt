On October 12, 1990, Wolfgang Schäuble steps onto the stage of a community hall in Oppenau, a small town nestled in the Black Forest, for an election campaign rally. Around three hundred people have come to hear him. Nine days earlier, Germany was officially reunified, under a treaty Schäuble himself signed the previous August 31, on behalf of the Bonn government, at the end of negotiations he led with the East German side. That evening, in the audience, a man approaches him. Dieter Kaufmann, who suffers from mental illness, pulls out a gun and fires three times at the federal interior minister.

The bullets hit Schäuble in the face and the spine. One of them severely damages his spinal cord, narrowly sparing the use of his arms. He is rushed to the hospital. The verdict comes quickly: his legs are paralyzed. He will never walk again. The man who has just spent months negotiating his country's restored unity, and whom many at the time already see as the natural successor to Chancellor Helmut Kohl, seems doomed to vanish from the political stage at the very moment his career reaches its peak.

That is not what happens. Schäuble is back at work in less than three months, before he has even finished his rehabilitation program. For one of the last major rallies of the 1990 campaign, Chancellor Kohl travels to Offenburg, where Schäuble makes one of his first public appearances since the attack, before a crowd of around nine thousand. He is reelected to the Bundestag in December, as he will be at every election until his death, thirty-three years later.

Nothing in Schäuble's path up to that point hinted at such a political destiny. The son of an Offenburg accountant, he joins the CDU's youth wing in 1961, studies law and economics in Freiburg and then Hamburg, and defends a doctoral thesis in law in 1971. He then practices as a lawyer at the Offenburg district court while climbing the party ranks, before being elected to the Bundestag in 1972, at just thirty, in the very Offenburg constituency he will represent without interruption until his death. Chief whip of the CDU/CSU parliamentary group from 1981 to 1984, then minister for special affairs from 1984, he becomes federal interior minister in April 1989. It is in that post that he leads, on the West German side, the negotiations that produce the 1990 Unification Treaty, the founding text of contemporary Germany, carried by a man who comes close to losing his life over it a few weeks later.

The rest of his career is built entirely from a wheelchair. Chairman of the CDU/CSU parliamentary group from 1991 to 2000, he returns to the Interior Ministry from 2005 to 2009 in Angela Merkel's government, before taking over the federal Finance Ministry in 2009, a post he holds until 2017. Those years place him at the heart of the eurozone sovereign debt crisis: it is Schäuble who, facing Greece, defends an inflexible budgetary line, rejecting in 2012 the calls of IMF managing director Christine Lagarde to give Athens more time to put its accounts in order. His rigor earns him a controversial reputation, hailed by some as a guarantee of seriousness, denounced by others as an intransigence with heavy social consequences for Greece. In 2014, he presents the first German federal budget without new debt since 1969.

In 2017, Schäuble leaves the Finance Ministry to become President of the Bundestag, one of the highest offices of the federal state, which he holds until 2021. That same year, on October 21, 2017, he becomes the longest-serving member in the history of the German parliament, surpassing a record set by August Bebel at the turn of the twentieth century. Elected without interruption since 1972, the chamber's most senior member, he remains an active member of the Bundestag until his final months, present on the floor from the same chair that has carried the entire second half of his career.

Wolfgang Schäuble dies on December 26, 2023, at 81, of cancer. He will have spent nearly two-thirds of his fifty-one years in parliament in a wheelchair, without it ever keeping him away from the hardest-fought issues in German and European politics: reunification, the euro, the Greek crisis. What the Oppenau attack failed to interrupt is half a century of continuous presence at the heart of power, from 1972 to 2023, including thirty-three years spent governing, debating and negotiating from a chair that none of his offices ever had to apologize for.
