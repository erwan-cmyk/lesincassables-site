On July 29, 2019, the day the newly elected members officially take office, Yasuhiko Funago (舩後靖彦) passes through the main entrance of Japan's House of Councillors on an access ramp installed specially for him. Inside the chamber, rows of seats have been removed to make room for his reclining wheelchair, and the standing rules — unchanged for decades — have been hastily amended to allow a caregiver at his side during sessions. Funago can no longer speak: to express himself, he fixes his eyes on a screen, letter after letter, until an eye-tracking device composes his sentences, which an aide then reads aloud to the assembly. That day, for the first time in its history, the upper house of Japan's parliament adapts to one of its members, and not the other way around.

Nothing in Yasuhiko Funago's background marked him out for such an entrance. Born on October 4, 1957, in Gifu, he grows up from the age of ten in Chiba, where for a time he dreams of becoming a professional musician before giving up on that path. He turns to studies in political science and economics at Takushoku University, then joins a watch trading company, Sakata Watch Trading, in 1982, as soon as he graduates. He marries at 28 — the path, up to then, of a Japanese salaried employee like so many others, with no apparent connection to politics or activism.

In 2000, Funago is diagnosed with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), also known as Lou Gehrig's disease — a neurodegenerative disease that progressively destroys motor neurons and gradually robs patients of control over their muscles. Over the following years, the disease costs him the use of his limbs, then of his speech. He becomes dependent on round-the-clock assistance for everyday tasks, from getting up in the morning to going to bed.

His life is upended a second time in 2019, when Reiwa Shinsengumi, a young left-wing opposition party founded that same year by the actor turned activist Taro Yamamoto, places him at the top of its national list for the House of Councillors election, alongside another severely disabled candidate, Eiko Kimura, paralyzed from the neck down since the age of eight months as a result of cerebral palsy. Yamamoto's calculation is clear: at the top of the list, the two candidates will be elected as soon as the party crosses the required threshold, whatever number of votes they receive personally — an unusual strategy in a country where people with disabilities remain largely absent from political life. And indeed, in July 2019, Reiwa Shinsengumi wins two proportional-representation seats — and it is Funago and Kimura who fill them, while Yamamoto himself, though he received the most personal votes within the party, remains outside parliament.

Funago and Kimura thus become the first two severely disabled people elected to Japan's National Diet, an event the international press describes as "unprecedented." In a statement read by his caregiver after the results are announced, Funago says: "I am full of emotions that this moment has arrived, it has been a matter of life and death for me." He adds, in substance, that while he may look frail, he in fact feels more determined than many others. The event is widely covered by the international press, presented as the symbol of a major step forward for the political representation of disability, well beyond Japan's borders.

The election of the two lawmakers does not remain an isolated symbol: it forces the upper house — created in 1947 — to urgently rethink, for the first time, how it operates around one of its members: access, the layout of the chamber, the presence of caregivers during debates, all accommodations the institution had never before deemed necessary. Once in office, Funago makes the fight against the mistreatment of people with disabilities the heart of his mandate, arguing that the isolation and suffering he himself went through should not be repeated for others, and pushing for the development of truly accessible infrastructure, in transportation as well as in public buildings.

Funago sits in the House of Councillors until the end of his six-year term, in July 2025. Twenty-five years after a diagnosis that promised a progressive and inexorable loss of all physical autonomy, the man who now expresses himself only through the movement of his eyes will have occupied, for six years, one of the most visible seats in Japanese political life — he who, as a child, simply dreamed of one day stepping onto a music stage. The ramp installed for him at the entrance to the House of Councillors, for its part, has stayed in place.
