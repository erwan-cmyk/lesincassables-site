On November 15, 1955, Georgia O'Keeffe's sixty-eighth birthday, a young and still unknown Japanese painter sends her a letter from Matsumoto, in the mountains of central Japan, along with two watercolors. She has just discovered the American's work in a secondhand bookstore and asks her to help her find her way. She is twenty-six, her name is Yayoi Kusama, and she dreams of leaving for the United States. Twenty days later, O'Keeffe writes back, encourages her to show her work to anyone willing to look at it, and even offers to present it to a gallerist.

Yayoi Kusama was born on March 22, 1929, in Matsumoto, into a family of prosperous merchants who ran a plant nursery and a seed business. Her father supplies her with drawing materials; her mother, by contrast, steadfastly disapproves of a vocation she considers unbecoming for a girl, sometimes tears up her drawings before she can finish them, and sends her to spy on her father's infidelities — an experience Kusama would later say fed in her a lasting aversion to sexuality, a theme that would run through part of her New York work. It is in this tense childhood that her first hallucinations occur, at around seven to ten years of age depending on the biographies: pumpkins and violets that speak to her, auras around objects, fields of patterns that seem to engulf her. As a teenager, in the middle of the war, she sews parachutes for up to twelve hours a day in a factory, without ever ceasing to draw. After the war, she studies traditional nihonga painting in Kyoto and, as early as 1952, organizes her first solo exhibition in Japan. That same year, a psychiatrist, Shiho Nishimaru, presents her at a conference as an artist of genius with schizophrenic tendencies.

The letter to O'Keeffe changes the course of her life. With postwar Japan strictly limiting the export of currency, Kusama sews dollar bills into the lining of her kimono to finance her departure. She reaches Seattle in 1957, where she exhibits at the Zoe Dusanne Gallery, then settles in New York the following year. There she becomes a figure of the avant-garde: her Infinity Net canvases, dense meshes painted in strokes repeated to the point of exhaustion, her soft sculptures from the Accumulation series bristling with protuberances, then in 1965 her first mirror room, Infinity Mirror Room—Phalli's Field, build a body of work turned toward repetition and the dissolution of the body in the pattern. She would later assert that several of her ideas were borrowed from her without her receiving the recognition then granted to her male counterparts, citing in particular Lucas Samaras, who presents a mirror room shortly after hers in a more prominent gallery, as well as Andy Warhol and Claes Oldenburg.

At the turn of the 1970s, her health deteriorates and the New York scene, where she has staged happening after happening and exhibition after exhibition, becomes harder for her. In 1973, after sixteen years in the United States, she returns to Japan, where the welcome is far from warm. She then goes through a period of great distress. In 1977, on her own initiative, she enters the Seiwa psychiatric hospital in Tokyo. She has lived there ever since, by choice, while working every day in a studio located nearby. In her autobiography Infinity Net, she sums up what binds her to creation: "I fight pain, anxiety, and fear every day, and the only method I have found that relieves my illness is to keep creating art."

Her career gets a second wind at the end of the 1980s: a traveling retrospective in 1989, then her selection to represent Japan at the 45th Venice Biennale in 1993, lead to her rediscovery on an international scale. Honors follow: the Asahi Prize in 2001, France's Order of Arts and Letters in 2003, the Order of the Rising Sun and the Praemium Imperiale for painting in 2006. In 2017, a museum entirely devoted to her work opens in Tokyo.

Since then, her Infinity Mirror Rooms have drawn crowds unprecedented for contemporary art. When her traveling retrospective stopped at the Hirshhorn Museum in Washington in 2017, more than 14,000 visitors crowded in during the first week alone, lifting the museum's attendance to its highest level in nearly forty years, while more than 58,000 people tried to obtain a free ticket within a minute of their going online. A new retrospective, presented at the Fondation Beyeler in Switzerland in late 2025 and then set to travel across Europe until 2027, has included Infinity Mirror Rooms designed specially for the occasion. Now 97 years old, Kusama continues to paint every day in her Tokyo studio. What began with a letter from an unknown woman asking to be shown the way has become one of the most visited, most cited and most commercially significant bodies of work in contemporary art, built year after year out of what threatened to overwhelm her.
