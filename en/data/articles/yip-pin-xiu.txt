She is five years old when, at the edge of a swimming pool in Singapore, she watches her two older brothers take their swimming lessons. She wants to go in too. Her mother asks the instructor whether he can take her into the group. Yip Pin Xiu enters the water that day — she can still walk — without knowing that this pool will become, years later, the place where she will say she finally feels free, able to be herself without depending on anyone.

Doctors first speak of muscular dystrophy. A more precise diagnosis follows: Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease, a genetic condition that progressively weakens the peripheral nerves and the muscles. In April 2004, at twelve, she attends a para sport training session at the CCAB Swimming Complex, on Evans Road; a volunteer from the Singapore Disability Sports Council spots her and invites her to join a structured program. The following year, she loses the ability to walk and moves to a wheelchair. In the water, however, nothing stops: she keeps swimming, gradually giving up freestyle for backstroke as her legs allow her fewer and fewer kicks.

In 2008, at sixteen, she flies to the Beijing Paralympic Games. She returns with gold in the 50-meter backstroke and silver in the 50-meter freestyle, setting a world record along the way — Singapore's first Paralympic gold medal in history. Four years later, in London, her signature event is dropped from the Paralympic program. She leaves without a medal, an episode that says more about the instability of the classification system in which disabled swimmers compete than about any failing of the swimmer herself.

The comeback happens in Rio, in 2016. There she completes a double in the 50- and 100-meter backstroke, in the S2 class — reserved for swimmers with the most severe functional impairments — becoming along the way the first Singaporean athlete to win two gold medals at the same Paralympic Games. Over the longer distance, she improves her own world record by two seconds. Singapore pays her a bonus of 160,000 Singapore dollars before tax, against the million promised the same year to Joseph Schooling, who has just become the first Olympic champion in the country's history. The gap feeds, in her, a conviction that will soon extend beyond sport alone: recognition of disability sport remains unequal, including in her own country.

She defends both titles in Tokyo in 2021, then again in Paris in 2024, where the Singaporean press hails an unprecedented triple over this pair of distances, three consecutive Games. After five Paralympic appearances, from Beijing to Paris, her record stands at seven gold medals and one silver. As early as 2015, she becomes the first para athlete inducted into the Singapore Sports Hall of Fame; three years later, Forbes magazine places her on its 30 Under 30 Asia list.

That same year, in the midst of her sporting career, Yip Pin Xiu is appointed, in September 2018, a Nominated Member of Parliament — a seat the Singaporean system reserves for figures from outside the political parties. She is sworn in on October 1, at twenty-six, the youngest Nominated Member of Parliament in the country's history. She sits for nearly two years, until the dissolution of the 13th Parliament, on June 23, 2020. There she argues for shared governance of able-bodied and disability sport, and in February 2020 delivers a widely noted speech, calling on universities to adopt a unified national code against sexual harassment on campuses. "I don't think you need to go through what others have gone through to speak up for them," she would later explain to Tatler Asia.

Her term over, she continues her commitment with the National Youth Council and the Purple Parade, Singapore's movement for the inclusion of people with disabilities, while sitting since 2022 on the Athlete Council of the World Anti-Doping Agency. In February 2024, she becomes the first Singaporean para athlete to receive a wax figure at Madame Tussauds Singapore, a sign of recognition that has now reached the general public. She remains clear-eyed, however, about the road still to travel: in Singapore, she explains to Vogue Singapore, one rarely encounters people with disabilities in public spaces, so that they are often forgotten when decisions are made.

Nearly thirty years after asking, through her mother's voice, to join her brothers in a pool, Yip Pin Xiu keeps swimming, and keeps carrying, far beyond the pools, the voice of those who are too often forgotten.
