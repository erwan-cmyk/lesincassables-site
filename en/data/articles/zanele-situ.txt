On September 7, 2016, at the Maracanã Stadium in Rio de Janeiro, Zanele Situ enters the arena in a wheelchair, the South African flag in her hand. She is forty-five, this is her fifth appearance at the Paralympic Games, and the honor of carrying her country's colors before the packed stands falls to a woman whose childhood gave no hint of such a destiny.

Zanele Situ — Ntombizanele Situ, by her full name — is born on January 19, 1971, in Kokstad, a small town tucked between KwaZulu-Natal and the Eastern Cape, in a South Africa still locked down by the laws of apartheid. Around the age of twelve, her legs begin to weaken for no apparent reason. Doctors diagnose spinal tuberculosis; the disease plunges her, for about two years, into a state close to a coma. When she emerges from it, paralysis has set in from the fourth vertebra down: she will never walk again.

She is then sent to Mthatha, to a school for children with disabilities. It is there, between classes, that she discovers the shot put and the discus — disciplines she takes up at first with no particular ambition, curious about everything that comes her way. The country around her is transforming at the same moment: apartheid collapses in the early 1990s, Nelson Mandela is elected president in 1994, and South Africa returns to an international sporting stage from which decades of boycott had kept it away. Situ has practiced athletics since the mid-1980s without aiming at international competition; she joins the national team at the end of that decade of transition.

Her debut, at the World Championships in Birmingham in 1998, is immediately promising: she wins gold in the javelin with a throw of 14.45 meters, and bronze in the discus. Two years later, in Sydney, she writes her name into the history of South African sport: crowned Paralympic champion in the javelin, she becomes the first Black South African woman to win a Paralympic title, and adds a silver medal in the discus to her record. In a country barely completing its democratic transition, the feat reaches far beyond the stadium.

She defends her world crown in Lille in 2002, then retains her Paralympic title in Athens in 2004, where she receives the Whang Youn Dai Achievement Award, which honors, at each edition, a Paralympic athlete for the spirit of overcoming embodied beyond the sporting result alone. Situ becomes its first African recipient, in any category. The following year, the South African presidency awards her the Order of Ikhamanga in Silver, one of the country's highest civilian honors, for her contribution to national sport.

Her trajectory is anything but a straight line. Beijing, in 2008, ends without a podium. Rather than hanging up her javelin, she reworks her technique and builds back up: bronze at the World Championships in Christchurch in 2011, fourth place in London in 2012 with a throw of 16.22 meters, another world bronze in Lyon in 2013 and then in Doha in 2015. In 2016, in Rio, she climbs onto the Paralympic podium once more — bronze, with a personal best of 17.90 meters — the same year the South African federation chooses her to carry the flag at the opening ceremony.

Five years later, at fifty, she is still there. The Tokyo Games, delayed a year by the Covid-19 pandemic, mark her sixth Paralympic appearance — a rare longevity in her discipline. The health restrictions have upended her preparation, but she qualifies among the world's best in her category. Settled for several years in Stellenbosch, where she trains within the university's parasport program, she then divides her life between elite sport and her daughter, Azamazi, a teenager of whom she speaks with pride.

Zanele Situ dies on November 1, 2023, in Kokstad, the town where she was born, after a brief illness whose nature has not been made public. She was 52. Her sudden death shakes South African sport. The president of the International Paralympic Committee, Andrew Parsons, pays tribute to a pioneer: "The Paralympic Movement is built on pioneers and Zanele certainly forged a path. Her achievements at the Sydney 2000 Paralympic Games paved the way for a generation of South African athletes." The chief executive of the South African Sports Confederation and Olympic Committee (SASCOC), Nozipho Jafta, describes her as a true South African hero, faced with adversity early in life and having reached, despite everything, the very pinnacle of Paralympic sport.

From the child pinned by illness to a bed in a South African hospital ward to the flag bearer of the Maracanã, Zanele Situ leaves behind six Paralympic appearances, two Paralympic titles and the image of a quiet endurance, little inclined to turn her condition into a speech. She also leaves a daughter and a sister, and the memory of an athlete who, for nearly thirty years, turned up at the most demanding competitions in world parasport with the same curiosity as in her teens, when she first weighed a discus in her hands.
