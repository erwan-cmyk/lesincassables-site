On February 15, 2021, in the gym of the high school in Massillon, Ohio, Zion Clark gets into position, hands flat on the floor, torso upright. At the signal, he launches himself over twenty meters on the strength of his arms alone, watched by his partner and a small group who have come to see the attempt. The clock stops at 4.78 seconds: a new Guinness World Record. "Before I knew it, I had crossed the finish line!" he would tell Guinness World Records.

Zion Clark was born on September 29, 1997, in Columbus, Ohio, with caudal regression syndrome, which left him without legs from birth. His biological mother, unable to raise him, hands him over very early to the state's foster care system.

Years of instability follow: shuttled from one home to another at the whim of administrative decisions, he grows up away from the adults he becomes attached to. In his memoir Work With What You Got, published in 2023, he would return to episodes never told before, describing how dangerous his situation had become inside the foster care system. Not until he is sixteen does Kimberly Hawkins, already the mother of two children, take him in for good; she would officially become his adoptive mother at the end of his time in high school. As an adult, Zion Clark has in turn taken up the cause, through a fundraising effort meant to draw public attention to the failings of the American foster care system.

It is at seven, in Massillon, that his hands are placed on a wrestling mat for the first time. The beginning is rough: not a single win in his first two years at Massillon Washington High School. Then the progress speeds up. In his senior season, he posts a record of 33 wins and 15 losses and comes within a point of qualifying for the Ohio state championship, beaten in sudden death by an opponent carrying nine kilos more than he does. He also takes the state title in wheelchair racing, twice. His motto, "No excuses," which he would later have tattooed across his back, takes root in those years on the mat.

Next comes Kent State University, on the Tuscarawas campus, where he studies business management while joining the Golden Eagles wrestling team, in the 125-pound class. Early on, he climbs as high as eighth in the national rankings of the National Collegiate Wrestling Association. "He has a can-do attitude that never waivers," his coach Dave Schlarb would say of him.

The record of February 15, 2021, is only the first of a series. In October 2022, Zion Clark adds two more marks to his Guinness tally: the highest box jump performed on the hands alone (0.83 m) and the greatest number of diamond push-ups in three minutes (248). "There's nobody faster on their hands than me in the world," he says at the time to Guinness World Records, which would induct him into its Hall of Fame in 2024.

On December 17, 2022, he takes another step: his professional MMA debut, on a Gladiator Challenge card held in California. Against Eugene Murray, he imposes his wrestler's base, strings together takedowns and wins by unanimous decision at the end of three rounds. He celebrates his victory with a backflip in the middle of the cage.

That trajectory had first reached the wider public on screen, well before the cage and the college mats. In 2018, the short documentary Zion, directed by Floyd Russ and selected at the Sundance festival before its release on Netflix, follows the young wrestler from the high school in Massillon. The film wins two Sports Emmy Awards, including one for best editing.

The following years confirm his standing as a public figure: a semifinalist on America's Got Talent in the summer of 2023, more than a million followers on Instagram, a video of his Guinness record viewed tens of millions of times. The same year, he publishes the memoir Work With What You Got with the journalist James S. Hirsch. He wanted to be able to "tell my side of the story from my memory," he told Publishers Weekly.

In February 2026, in Miami, Zion Clark steps back onto a mat, this time for a grappling match against UFC heavyweight Valter Walker, on the undercard of Karate Combat 59. The weigh-in turns into a physical altercation between the two men. The next day, the bout itself is over quickly: Walker submits him by choke in a matter of moments. At nearly 29, between wrestling mats, the MMA cage and public advocacy on foster care, Zion Clark remains one of the most closely followed athletes of his generation.
